# Family Wealth Governance

## When to Use
- A wealthy family is grappling with how to transfer wealth across generations without creating entitlement or destroying motivation
- A family office is deciding which functions to perform internally and which to outsource
- A family needs a governance structure to manage the complexity of multi-generational wealth
- The "shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations" concern is front of mind

## Fails When
- The client is an individual without multi-generational wealth concerns
- The family needs legal structuring advice (trusts, estate planning) rather than a governance philosophy
- The family is unwilling to have difficult conversations about wealth, roles, and responsibilities

## Core Concept

Managing family wealth is much more than managing financial assets. A family has multiple forms of capital: financial, human, intellectual, social, emotional, philanthropic. Maximizing financial wealth is rarely enough for all members of the family to feel fulfilled. Certain families have adopted the mental framework that financial wealth is an enabler; it facilitates family members to seek overall, complete fulfillment, while less wealthy individuals often cannot focus on much more than making financial ends meet.

The analogy Brunel draws is between a family and a corporation. The successful CEO of a corporation cannot focus on a single dimension (engineering, or finance, or marketing) and expect the whole enterprise to succeed. The head of a family's wealth, what Charlotte Beyer calls the CEO of "My Wealth, Inc.," faces the same multi-dimensional challenge. Just as it would be misguided for a corporate CEO to focus on only one aspect of the business, it is equally misguided for a family to consider just one aspect of the wealth management challenge, yet the traditional approach would be exactly that.

**Educating future generations** is the concern Brunel encounters most frequently among the ultra-wealthy. The logic is best illustrated by the multi-billionaire who broke down in tears at the prospect that he might have "ruined my grandchildren by giving them too much money too easily." There is no one-size-fits-all solution, but there is a one-size-fits-all requirement: the issue deserves to be thought through carefully and in depth, and whatever decision is made must be carefully implemented.

Families adopt different approaches. Some choose to hide the wealth until legally required to disclose it, which risks the situation Brunel describes where a family's wealth was "almost totally dissipated one generation ago, because they had not been aware of the wealth or prepared to deal with it." Others disclose early but emphasize values and family history. Others use a trickle approach, giving small amounts to teach financial responsibility progressively. In every case, wealth transfers should not be driven solely by tax considerations but should reflect what the family expects future generations to do. Sheltering the family against all transfer taxes could work if younger generations are simultaneously prepared; otherwise, it may work directly against crucial family goals.

**The Wisdom Council** is a governance mechanism Brunel has seen work well. One of Brunel's client families created a group of external specialists, all of whom understand the various interactions across disciplines crucial to the family's sustained well-being. The council meets no more than once a year in a strategic rather than tactical role, functioning like a corporate board of directors stripped of its governance role. It takes a fairly insightful patriarchal generation to recognize its own limits and seek counsel, but the rewards can be high. The council's value tends to fade over time as the patriarchal generation gains the experience it lacked at the outset.

Members of a Wisdom Council should recognize they are not involved in day-to-day matters and should never become advocates for their own specialty areas. They should be experienced enough to be at ease within their specialties and have seen enough different circumstances to react to them, but "young enough mentally" that they are not set in their ways. They can also mentor individuals within the family or the office.

**The make-or-buy decision** is the practical question every family office faces: which functions to perform internally and which to delegate to outside providers. The cost of running a fully staffed single-family office has increased substantially, and the number of required disciplines is too large for any one office to master. Families often make this decision by default rather than consciously, keeping functions in-house because they have always been there rather than asking whether delegation would be more effective. The decision involves four forms of cost: direct dollar cost (the easiest to measure), sustainability (can the office attract and retain talent over time), confidentiality (every staff departure is a potential leak), and professional growth of staff (exposure to competing ideas and multiple circumstances).

## How to Apply

1. **Frame wealth management as a multi-dimensional challenge.** Before focusing on financial assets, inventory the family's full picture: financial, human, intellectual, social, emotional, and philanthropic capital.

2. **Address intergenerational education explicitly.** Choose a disclosure and preparation strategy (hide, disclose early with values emphasis, or trickle) and implement it deliberately. Do not let the decision be made by default.

3. **Establish governance structures.** Consider a Wisdom Council for strategic oversight and a Family Council for decision-making. Define roles, meeting frequency, and boundaries clearly.

4. **Audit the make-or-buy decision consciously.** List every function the family office performs. For each, ask whether it should be internal or delegated, based on dollar cost, sustainability, confidentiality, and professional-growth considerations.

5. **Integrate governance with the goals-based framework.** The family's governance structure should support the goals identified through the dreams-and-nightmares diagnostic. Wealth transfer plans, education programs, and advisory boards all serve specific goals and should be evaluated against them.

## Examples

**Situation:** A second-generation family member defends his spouse's decision not to attend family wealth meetings and his sons' refusal to be involved in managing the family's wealth.

**Application:** Brunel made a direct point: the boys and their mother did not object to the benefits the wealth made possible. They would one day have to take responsibility for managing it. There were really only two options: add the wealth to the family's private foundation (making the reluctant family members "normal" with no income from the financial wealth) or accept responsibility for the wealth and be adequately trained in its management. The patriarch and his wife were delighted at having the issue framed so clearly.

**Illustrative result:** The family confronted the entitlement risk directly. The reluctant members could no longer avoid the question of whether they would participate in managing the wealth they benefited from.

**Situation:** A family whose wealth was hidden from the next generation until legal disclosure was required.

**Application:** The younger generation, unprepared for the wealth and unaware of the responsibilities that came with it, squandered it within one generation.

**Illustrative result:** The family's wealth was "almost totally dissipated" because they had not been aware of it or prepared to deal with it. The lesson: hiding wealth without preparing heirs is a strategy with a known failure mode.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Let wealth-transfer strategy be driven solely by tax minimization.
**Why:** Sheltering the family against transfer taxes without simultaneously preparing younger generations for the advantages and responsibilities of wealth can work directly against the family's most important goals.

**Don't:** Make the in-house vs. outsource decision by default.
**Why:** Keeping functions in-house because "they've always been there" is not a decision; it is inertia. Each function should be evaluated on cost, sustainability, confidentiality, and the professional growth it provides to staff.

**Don't:** Create a Wisdom Council that becomes an advocate for one specialty area.
**Why:** Council members must see across disciplines. A council dominated by one perspective (investment management, legal, tax) becomes a lobbying group, not an advisory board.
