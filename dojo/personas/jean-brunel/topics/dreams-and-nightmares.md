# Dreams and Nightmares Diagnostic

## When to Use
- Starting a new client relationship where the adviser needs to understand what the client actually cares about
- A client has been managed for years against abstract return targets without ever having been asked what their money is for
- An adviser finds that standard risk-tolerance questionnaires produce answers that do not reflect the client's real priorities
- A family needs to surface goals that different family members may prioritize differently

## Fails When
- The client has already gone through a thorough goals-discovery process and needs portfolio construction, not more conversation
- The adviser uses the diagnostic as a one-time exercise and never revisits it; client goals evolve over time

## Core Concept

The standard approach to discovering a client's investment preferences begins with a risk-tolerance questionnaire: "On a scale of 1 to 10, how much risk are you comfortable with?" The resulting number drives a single blended portfolio. This approach fails because it asks the wrong question. Risk tolerance in the abstract is meaningless; it depends entirely on what the risk is for.

The dreams-and-nightmares diagnostic replaces the abstract questionnaire with two concrete questions:

**"We are now five years from now and you want to say that the program we have designed and implemented has been successful. How will you justify that?"**

**"We are now five years from now and you want to say that the program we have designed and implemented has been a failure. How will you justify that?"**

These questions surface what the client actually cares about, in the client's own words, without requiring the client to translate their priorities into financial jargon. The answers reveal goals that a risk questionnaire would never capture: "I don't want to ruin my grandchildren by giving them too much money too easily." "I want to maintain my lifestyle without ever having to think about it." "I want to endow a foundation that outlasts me."

Brunel uses this diagnostic consistently across decades of practice. In the 2012 CFA Institute paper, he describes a 67-year-old CEO and owner of an NYSE-listed company worth billions who, when asked what would indicate failure in five years, broke down in tears: "If I ruined my grandchildren by giving them too much money too easily." In the 2015 book, he describes a multi-billionaire founder, a separate client, who gave the same answer to the same question, confirming this is a recurring diagnostic device applied across engagements, not a one-off anecdote.

The diagnostic accomplishes several things simultaneously. It shifts the conversation from financial jargon to the client's own language (needs, wants, wishes, dreams). It reveals the emotional weight behind each goal, which no quantitative questionnaire can capture. It often surfaces goals the client has never explicitly articulated, particularly dynastic and philanthropic priorities that the client may not have recognized as investment-relevant. And it creates a feedback loop: the answers to these two questions become the criteria against which the entire wealth management program is evaluated over time.

The Das, Markowitz, Scheid, and Statman paper (2010) provides the theoretical underpinning for why this works: risk, properly defined for individuals, is not the volatility of returns but the probability of failing to achieve a specific goal. The dreams-and-nightmares diagnostic is the practical, client-facing implementation of that redefinition. When a client says "this is a need," they are implicitly saying "the required probability of success is very high." When they say "this is a dream," they are implicitly accepting a lower probability. The adviser's job is to translate those intuitive categories into portfolio constraints.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask the two questions early in the relationship.** Frame them exactly as stated: five years from now, success or failure, how will you justify that? Do not paraphrase into financial terms; let the client answer in their own language.

2. **Listen for the emotional weight.** The goals that produce tears, hesitation, or visible discomfort are the goals with the lowest tolerance for failure. These become the highest-priority sub-portfolios, funded first and with the most conservative allocation.

3. **Categorize the answers.** Map each goal to the needs/wants/wishes/dreams hierarchy. A need demands near-certainty of success. A want is highly desirable but some failure is tolerable. A wish would be wonderful but is not critical. A dream is in the 50/50 range; the client will die with unfulfilled dreams and that is acceptable.

4. **Translate into portfolio constraints.** Each goal becomes a sub-portfolio with a time horizon, a required probability of success, and an asset allocation matched to those constraints.

5. **Revisit periodically.** Dreams and nightmares evolve. The client who initially demanded "do not lose money" may, after five years of a well-diversified portfolio, shift to thinking about long-term dynastic goals. The diagnostic should be re-administered when circumstances change materially.

## Examples

**Situation:** A multi-billionaire founder, recently retired, is asked the two diagnostic questions.

**Application:** When asked what would indicate failure in five years, the founder broke down in tears and said: "I have ruined my grandchildren." This answer revealed that the client's dominant concern was not investment returns, not tax minimization, not even lifestyle maintenance; it was the intergenerational impact of wealth on family character.

**Illustrative result:** The wealth management program was structured around dynastic goals as the highest priority, with specific mechanisms for staged wealth transfer, financial education for younger generations, and a family governance structure designed to prevent the outcome the founder feared most.

**Situation:** A client who sold his business for $250 million, far more than he ever expected to have, gave one instruction: "Do not lose money."

**Application:** The goals-based process started where the client was. His only articulated dream was capital preservation. Over five years, as the portfolio performed and the client grew comfortable, he began articulating goals beyond preservation: lifestyle flexibility, children's education, philanthropic ambitions.

**Illustrative result:** The iterative process accommodated the evolution. The initial nightmare (losing money) was addressed first; as that fear receded, the client's true dreams emerged and the portfolio structure evolved to serve them.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Translate the client's answers into financial jargon before the client is finished speaking.
**Why:** The act of translating prematurely signals that the adviser values their own framework more than the client's actual priorities. It also risks distorting the client's meaning.

**Don't:** Treat the diagnostic as a checklist to be completed once and filed away.
**Why:** Client goals evolve. The diagnostic is a tool for ongoing relationship management, not a one-time intake form.

**Don't:** Dismiss emotional answers as irrational or irrelevant.
**Why:** The emotional weight is the information. A client who cries when discussing grandchildren is telling you exactly where the lowest-risk-tolerance, highest-priority sub-portfolio should be directed.
