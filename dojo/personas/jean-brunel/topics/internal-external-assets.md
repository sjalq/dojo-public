# Internal vs. External / Lifestyle vs. Non-Lifestyle Assets

## When to Use
- A family has both self-managed assets (an operating business, directly held real estate, a family venture capital arm) and externally managed financial portfolios, and needs a coherent framework that integrates both
- A family-office adviser needs to determine how much of the total wealth picture should be managed internally and how much delegated to outside managers
- A client needs to understand how lifestyle-funding assets and growth/legacy assets should be structured differently
- The user is working through the "make or buy" decision for family-office functions

## Fails When
- All assets are liquid, externally managed financial instruments with no family-controlled operating businesses or direct holdings
- The family is unwilling to recognize that different asset categories require different management approaches and different reporting

## Core Concept

Families with substantial wealth typically hold two fundamentally different kinds of assets. Internal assets are those the family controls and manages directly: an operating business, directly held commercial real estate, timberland, a family-run venture capital operation. External assets are those for which the family hires professional managers: publicly traded securities, managed funds, hedge funds, private equity allocations through outside managers.

This distinction matters because internal and external assets have different risk characteristics, different liquidity profiles, different governance requirements, and different relationships to the family's identity and sense of purpose. A family's operating business may represent 60% of total net worth but is illiquid, concentrated, and deeply tied to the family's identity. Treating it the same way as a diversified public equity portfolio is a category error.

Within external assets, a further distinction is critical: lifestyle assets versus non-lifestyle assets. Lifestyle assets fund the family's day-to-day living expenses and near-term obligations. Non-lifestyle assets fund longer-term goals (dynastic, philanthropic, opportunistic) where immediate liquidity is less important. Brunel identifies this as a more important division than it might seem at first: "The biggest nightmare any of my clients face is a change to their lifestyle."

Lifestyle needs are addressed on two time horizons. Short to medium-term (3 to 5 years) is funded with fixed-income strategies that provide predictable cash flow. Long-term (6 to 15 years) uses a balanced allocation with some inflation-fighting equity exposure. GenSpring focused on a 15-year time span "because 15 years offers the highest probability of achieving positive returns." For clients in their fifties, two successive 15-year cycles carry the lifestyle plan to age 80.

Non-lifestyle external assets divide further into capital preservation (discretionary) and capital growth (nondiscretionary), drawing on Jarrod Wilcox's discretionary wealth hypothesis (2003). Attribution: the discretionary/nondiscretionary wealth distinction is Wilcox's concept, cited by Brunel, not originated by him. The key insight is that wealth above what is needed for lifestyle and dynastic obligations is discretionary, and the family can take significantly more risk with it. "Clients can regret the loss of discretionary wealth just as much as they can regret the loss of any other wealth," but the consequences of that loss are materially different from the consequences of failing to fund lifestyle needs.

Internal assets similarly divide into capital preservation (low risk, income producing, such as timberland harvested regularly or stabilized real estate) and growth assets (higher risk, such as a family-operated venture capital arm or an operating business in expansion mode).

The practical challenge for family offices is integrating internal and external assets into a single goals-based framework. The operating business is not "diversification" in the portfolio sense; it is a concentrated, illiquid position that happens to generate the cash flow funding everything else. The goals-based framework treats it as a component of the overall picture, with its own risk characteristics and its own relationship to specific goals, rather than ignoring it or treating it as a separate financial universe.

## How to Apply

1. **Inventory all assets by category.** Separate internal (family-controlled) from external (professionally managed). Within each, separate capital preservation from growth.

2. **Identify which assets fund which goals.** The operating business may fund lifestyle through distributions and fund dynastic goals through appreciation. The external portfolio may fund lifestyle through short-term fixed income and philanthropic goals through long-term growth allocations.

3. **Fund lifestyle first and conservatively.** Calculate total lifestyle needs over two 15-year cycles. Fund the first cycle with short-term and long-term lifestyle modules. Fund the second with a replenishment module. This amount is non-negotiable; it must be funded before discretionary or growth goals are addressed.

4. **Allocate non-lifestyle assets according to their discretionary status.** Wealth above the lifestyle reserve is discretionary and can bear more risk. Within that discretionary pool, allocate according to the family's dynastic and philanthropic goals.

5. **Manage internal and external assets as a single picture.** The family's operating business generates income that flows into the external portfolio. The external portfolio may fund internal investments (such as capitalizing a family venture). The interactions between the two must be managed as part of the overall goals-based process.

## Examples

**Situation:** A family has $35 million in total assets, including a $2 million apartment complex (internal, capital preservation) and a $1 million venture capital investment managed by the second generation (internal, growth).

**Application:** Internal capital preservation ($2 million) and internal growth ($1 million) are set aside and managed according to their own characteristics. The remaining $32 million in external assets is allocated across short-term lifestyle, long-term lifestyle, lifestyle replenishment, opportunistic, and capital growth modules.

**Illustrative result:** The family's total wealth picture integrates all four asset categories (internal preservation, internal growth, external lifestyle, external growth) in a single framework. Each category has its own risk profile, its own reporting, and its own relationship to specific family goals.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Ignore the family's operating business or real estate holdings when constructing the investment policy.
**Why:** The operating business may represent the largest single position in the family's total wealth. Ignoring it overstates the diversification of the portfolio and understates the family's concentration risk.

**Don't:** Treat all non-lifestyle assets as identical.
**Why:** Discretionary wealth (above what is needed for lifestyle and dynastic obligations) can tolerate significantly more risk than nondiscretionary wealth. Conflating the two produces an allocation that is either too conservative for the discretionary portion or too aggressive for the nondiscretionary portion.

**Don't:** Fund lifestyle needs with illiquid assets.
**Why:** Lifestyle assets must produce predictable, accessible cash flow. Private equity, venture capital, and directly held real estate may be excellent growth assets but are unsuitable for funding next year's living expenses.
