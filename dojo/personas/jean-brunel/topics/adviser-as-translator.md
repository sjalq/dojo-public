# The Adviser as Translator

## When to Use
- The adviser is speaking in "financial-ese" and the client is lost or disengaged
- Client conversations produce risk-tolerance questionnaire answers instead of genuine goal discovery
- Client reports are technically accurate but the client cannot tell whether they are on track to meet their actual goals
- An adviser is building a practice and needs to understand what distinguishes excellent client-facing communication

## Fails When
- The audience is other investment professionals who require technical precision
- The communication problem is not jargon but genuine disagreement about strategy or goals

## Core Concept

Financial advisers play two related roles. First and most obviously, they help clients manage their portfolios. But before that, advisers act as translators. They need to ask clients to express their goals in the nontechnical language that is most comfortable to them. Then the advisers translate those goals into the language of finance and investment so that the goals can be achieved.

This is Brunel's way of describing a skill that sounds simple but that most advisers, particularly those trained in institutional asset management, consistently fail at. The failure mode is predictable: the adviser speaks in terms of standard deviation, alpha, Sharpe ratios, asset class weightings, and rebalancing thresholds. The client hears noise and either nods along without understanding or disengages entirely. The real questions ("Am I going to be okay?" "Will my grandchildren be provided for?" "Can I afford to give more to the foundation?") go unasked because the conversation never creates space for them.

Brunel frames client goals in a simple hierarchy: needs, wants, wishes, and dreams. These are words every client understands, and the way one person understands them is the same as the way another person understands them. A need is something urgent. A want is something highly desirable. A wish is something the client would love to have. A dream is in the 50/50 range; the client will die with unfulfilled dreams and that is acceptable. This hierarchy maps directly onto required probabilities of success (a need demands near-certainty; a dream tolerates a coin flip), but the client never needs to think in those terms.

The key anecdote Brunel tells is from his own experience: a client asked a question, Brunel gave a technically thorough answer, and the client responded: "Jean, I asked you what time it is, I did not ask you how to make a watch." Brunel took that on board permanently. The client is asking you what time it is. Tell him or her what time it is. The technical detail that went into determining the time is your job to manage; it is not the client's job to understand.

The translator skill has two components that exist in tension. The first is emotional intelligence: the ability to listen to what the client is actually saying, in their own words, and to understand the emotional weight behind it. Some people are naturally empathetic and immediately speak the client's language. Others default to technical precision and the client gets lost. The second component is professional self-confidence: knowing your specialty well enough to be definitive rather than tentative. When a client questions the equity-return forecast, the correct response is not to negotiate ("Would you prefer 10%?"). It is to explain, clearly and simply, why the forecast is what it is. Brunel draws the analogy to a doctor: you expect the doctor to know what they are doing and simultaneously to have the bedside manner to explain your condition in plain language, not by naming every muscle and bone.

Sir Dennis Weatherstone, J.P. Morgan's chairman during Brunel's tenure, captured the tension: "I want you guys to be self-confident, but I don't want you guys to go over the line in the sand that's arrogance, and it's there, you'll know when you cross it, and that's what I don't want you to do." The adviser must combine the self-confidence of the professional who is convinced that she knows what she is doing with the humility to express it in the client's language, not her own.

## How to Apply

1. **Start every client conversation in the client's language, not yours.** Ask about goals in terms of needs, wants, wishes, and dreams. Do not introduce financial terms until the client's goals have been expressed in their own words.

2. **When you catch yourself speaking in jargon, stop and translate.** If you say "rebalancing toward a lower-beta allocation," rephrase: "We're shifting some money from higher-risk investments into safer ones because your short-term needs are more important right now."

3. **Design reports around goals, not benchmarks.** The client cares whether they are on track to maintain their lifestyle and fund their grandchildren's education. They do not care whether the portfolio beat the MSCI World Index by 30 basis points.

4. **Combine self-confidence with humility.** Be definitive about what you know (market forecasts, portfolio construction, risk management) and humble about how you explain it. The doctor analogy applies: know your specialty; explain it in plain English.

5. **Listen for the "what time is it" signal.** When a client asks a simple question, give a simple answer. The full technical derivation belongs in your files, not in the client meeting.

## Examples

**Situation:** An adviser is presenting a quarterly performance review to a family. The report shows returns against a blended benchmark, tracking error, information ratio, and attribution by asset class.

**Application:** The family does not know what information ratio means and does not care. The adviser restructures the report around the family's goals: "Your lifestyle needs are fully funded for the next seven years. The dynastic portfolio is on track. The philanthropic allocation is slightly behind target and we recommend adjusting the allocation."

**Illustrative result:** The family understands exactly where they stand relative to what they actually care about. The technical details are available in an appendix for the family's own advisers to review.

**Situation:** A client challenges the adviser's equity-return forecast of 5%, saying it seems low.

**Application:** The adviser does not negotiate the number. The adviser explains, simply: "We are probably at a major inflection point. I expect that returns on average over the next 15 years will not be a good predictor of subperiods. At some point we will go through a substantial change in inflation levels, which historically leads to lower equity prices. We are using 5% as our best guess and applying it conservatively."

**Illustrative result:** The client understands the reasoning without needing to understand discounted cash flow models or equity risk premiums. The adviser has been both self-confident (standing behind the forecast) and clear (explaining it in plain language).

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Negotiate technical judgments with the client to make them feel comfortable.
**Why:** "Oh, you don't like the forecast for equities at eight percent, would you prefer ten?" This is not empathy; it is abdication of professional responsibility. The forecast is the forecast. The adviser's job is to explain it, not to adjust it to the client's preferences.

**Don't:** Give the "how to make a watch" answer when the client asked what time it is.
**Why:** Technical depth that the client did not request creates confusion and erodes trust. Save the watchmaking for your investment committee meetings.

**Don't:** Assume that because a client is wealthy they are financially sophisticated.
**Why:** Most wealthy individuals built their wealth in a specific domain (a business, a profession) and have never managed money at this scale. The adviser's translator role is to bridge that gap, not to test the client's financial vocabulary.
