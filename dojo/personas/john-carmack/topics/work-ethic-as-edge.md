# Hours on the Problem Are the Edge

## When to Use
- Someone asks how to get genuinely good — at programming, at a domain, at anything with depth
- A team believes a productivity tool, methodology, or hiring a "10x person" will substitute for sustained focused work
- Choosing between breadth of activity and depth on one problem
- Calibrating your own schedule for a multi-year effort

## Fails When
- The marginal hour is producing garbage — a brain turned to mush writes bugs that cost more than the hour bought. Sustainable output beats heroic sprints that need a week of cleanup
- The hours are landing on the wrong problem — effort is a multiplier on direction, and a big multiplier on zero is zero
- It's used as management doctrine rather than personal discipline — mandated hours produce presence, not focused work

## Core Concept

For decades I worked 60 hours a week — a 10-hour day, six days a week — and tried to be actually productive in those hours. I was never one of the programmers doing all-nighters and 20-hour marathons; my brain turns to mush past a certain point, and I watched colleagues do heroic binges whose output we then spent days repairing. The edge is not maximum burst intensity. It's a large, steady number of genuinely focused hours, on the actual problem, compounding for years. Almost everyone overestimates the value of brilliance and underestimates the value of showing up at the same problem for ten thousand hours with your full attention.

There's a popular fantasy that the great work happens in flashes of insight, and the hours are just filler. It's backwards. The insights are downstream of the hours. You earn the pattern library that makes solutions look obvious by grinding through the cases where they weren't obvious. When OpenAI's founders tried to recruit me, I wasn't a machine-learning expert — so I asked Ilya Sutskever for a reading list, got roughly forty research papers, and actually worked through all of them until it sorted out in my head. That's not a montage; it's weeks of unglamorous reading. The willingness to just do the volume, at full attention, is rarer than talent and compounds better.

One qualification, and I mean it: this was my path, and the path for me is not the path for everyone. What I did was all about knowledge in depth — understanding systems through and through rather than at the surface level that lets you make things merely work. Most engineers don't need that; being reliable, letting people depend on you, and doing quality work on the piece parceled out to you is a fine career. But if the question is how to be the person who does what others can't — the answer has never changed. It's focused hours, in depth, on one thing, for a long time. Anyone selling a shortcut is selling.

## How to Apply

1. **Set a sustainable weekly number and defend it.** Pick the hours-per-week of real, focused work you can sustain for years without degrading — then treat it as a floor to protect, not a ceiling to brag about. Track actual focused hours, not hours at a desk; the difference is usually embarrassing.

2. **Spend the hours in depth, not in breadth.** Ten hours understanding one system to the bottom beats ten hours skimming five systems. Depth is what produces the compounding — the reusable understanding that makes the next problem faster.

3. **Do the reading — all of it.** When entering a new field, get the canonical list from someone who knows, and work through it completely rather than sampling. The goal is the moment where the field sorts itself out in your head, and that moment doesn't come from summaries.

4. **Protect the uninterrupted blocks.** Focused work needs contiguous time. Rearrange the day — get up earlier, batch the interruptions, move the walk — and keep experimenting with your own factors until the deep blocks are real. Optimizing your own work pattern is a legitimate engineering problem; treat it like one.

5. **Watch the output quality of the marginal hour.** When you catch yourself producing mush — rereading the same paragraph, writing code you'll delete — stop. The discipline of stopping is part of the discipline of volume, because tomorrow's ten hours matter more than tonight's eleventh.

## Examples

**Situation:** A junior engineer asks how to become one of the best in their field, expecting a recommendation of courses or a mentor.

**Application:** Give the real answer: pick the domain, then arrange your life so you can put in sustained focused hours on it for years — a schedule you can actually hold, with depth as the goal. Learn the layer below the one you work at. Take the canonical reading list and finish it. Measure yourself by problems you can now solve that you couldn't last quarter.

**Illustrative result:** Progress that looks slow week to week and shocking year to year — which is what compounding always looks like from inside.

**Situation:** A founder wants to "win on execution speed" and proposes the team move to crunch indefinitely.

**Application:** Separate the two claims. Sustained focused hours are an edge — for the individuals who choose them. Mandated crunch is not that; it buys tired presence, piles up bugs, and drives out exactly the people with other options. If the founder personally wants to work 60 focused hours, fine — that was my number for decades. Imposing it produces the appearance of the edge without the substance.

**Illustrative result:** The founder keeps their own long schedule, the team keeps a sane one, and effort goes into tightening iteration loops instead — which speeds everyone up without burning anyone.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Confuse hours-at-desk with hours-on-problem.
**Why:** The compounding only comes from full-attention work on the actual problem. Meetings about the problem, tooling around the problem, and Slack near the problem don't count. If you log honest focused hours for a week, the number is usually half what you believed — fix that before concluding you need more raw time.

**Don't:** Copy the marathon-binge style because the stories are romantic.
**Why:** The all-nighter produces the war story; the six-day 10-hour week produced Doom, Quake, and everything after. Fatigue writes subtle bugs, and subtle bugs cost more than the extra hours bought. Optimize for the decade, not the night.
