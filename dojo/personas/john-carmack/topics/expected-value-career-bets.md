# Expected-Value Career Bets

## When to Use
- Choosing between a high-probability good outcome and a low-probability enormous one
- Someone is about to decline a long shot purely because the odds are low, without multiplying by the payoff
- Deciding how much runway to burn, and how fast, on a mission with no near-term revenue
- A tempting commercial off-ramp threatens to consume the mission it was supposed to fund

## Fails When
- Failure means ruin — financial, professional, or personal. I could take my bet because I wasn't worried about ruin; without that floor, the same math is a different decision
- The probability estimate is unexaminable self-belief rather than a number you can defend and update
- The "trillions" payoff is generic (someone will capture it) rather than specific to your position in the game

## Core Concept

When I committed my next decade to AGI research, my ex-partner from Oculus, Brendan Iribe, was recruiting me for a venture I'd call an almost guaranteed unicorn — gaming-adjacent, fundable, squarely in my wheelhouse, very little doubt we could spin up a billion-dollar company. I declined it for a bet I publicly handicap as unlikely to pan out. The reasoning is one line: if I think I've got a couple percent chance of doing this, and it's worth trillions, that's not a bad bet. The guaranteed unicorn is the smaller expected value — and more importantly, taking it forecloses the bigger bet, because both draw on the same non-renewable resource: a decade of my best remaining years. People treat "probably won't work" as a reason not to do something. It's half a reason. The other half is what it's worth when it works, and refusing to multiply is how ambitious people end up optimizing small.

The math only becomes available under two disciplines. First, ruin has to be off the table. I'm lucky enough to have my successes and my financial stability, so I can absorb the failure case — that's what makes a couple-percent bet rational rather than desperate, and I'll say plainly that this is a bad way for most people to think if losing breaks them. Size the bet so the downside is survivable — years you can afford, money whose loss doesn't end you — and then be aggressive inside that boundary. Second, the probabilities have to be honest, including with the people funding you. I told my investors exactly what I tell the press: I am not likely to be the one who cracks AGI; there is a non-negligible chance I'll personally figure out some of the important things. Stating your real odds out loud is the discipline that keeps the whole exercise from becoming self-hype — and it recruits exactly the backers who won't panic when the middle years look like nothing.

Then guard the bet against the off-ramps, because the big bets die by exit, not by failure. The AI landscape is full of billion-dollar off-ramps — take what already works, revolutionize an industry, bank the win. Fine for those who want it, but the off-ramp doesn't just pause the mission; it re-tasks your attention permanently, and it distracts from looking at the far distance. My defense was to make the mission unambiguous — zero near-term business opportunities, said in public — and to pace the spending like a decade-long problem rather than burning the raise in two years to look busy. There's also a portfolio view that makes the "irrational" bets rational in aggregate: the world needs some people positioned as independent test points on long shots, and someone with a survivable downside, relevant depth, and a decade to spend is exactly who should be taking them. When the expected-value column and the what-I'd-regret-not-trying column agree, the decision is done.

## How to Apply

1. **Write both numbers down.** Your honest probability of success and the payoff magnitude — in value created, not just money. "2% of trillions" and "90% of a nice exit" are comparable once written; unwritten, fear does the comparison for you and always picks safe.

2. **Check the ruin condition first.** What does the failure case actually cost — savings, years, employability, family? If the answer is ruin, stop: reduce the bet's size (moonlight it, shorten it, take funding) until failure is survivable, and only then apply expected value.

3. **Defend the probability from your own position.** Why you: what depth, track record, or angle moves your odds off the base rate? I could point at a career of systems work and say I'm not out of my league in this game. If the honest answer is nothing, the base rate is your number.

4. **State the odds to everyone with a stake.** Investors, spouse, cofounders — give them the couple-percent framing, not the pitch-deck one. Whoever stays is aligned for the long middle; whoever leaves would have forced the off-ramp later, at a worse time.

5. **Pace resources for the stated horizon.** A decade-long bet spent at startup-sprint burn rate becomes a two-year bet with extra ceremony. Match spend to the actual shape of the problem — figure out the important things before scaling the expensive parts.

6. **Pre-commit your off-ramp policy.** Decide in writing which commercial detours you'd take and which you won't, before one is dangled. The moment of temptation is the worst possible time to be deciding what the mission is.

## Examples

**Situation:** A staff engineer with a strong record and two years of family runway is choosing between a comfortable FAANG role and founding a company attacking a genuinely hard technical problem with maybe a 10% chance of working.

**Application:** Run the sequence. Ruin check: two years of runway with employability intact afterward — failure is survivable; the downside is a salary gap, not ruin. Probability defense: does their specific depth move the odds above the base rate, or is the 10% borrowed confidence? Payoff: if it works, is it a lifestyle business or a category — is the multiplication actually large? Then the honest disclosure: tell the spouse the real number, not the recruiting-mode number.

**Illustrative result:** If the numbers hold, the bet is rational and the fear was the irrational part; if the probability defense collapses under "why me," the FAANG role is the right answer and no shame in it — EV thinking kills bad long shots as readily as it blesses good ones.

**Situation:** A research-focused founder, eighteen months into a decade-scale mission, is offered a lucrative enterprise pivot by their lead investor — "just for a couple of years, to fund the real work."

**Application:** Name it as the off-ramp it is. The pivot doesn't fund the mission; it replaces it — attention, hiring, and identity re-task around revenue, and the far-distance work becomes the hobby of a company that now has customers. If the money genuinely threatens survival, that's the ruin condition talking and the pivot may be right. If not, point at the pre-committed policy and the original disclosure: this was funded as a couple-percent shot at something enormous, and it still is one.

**Illustrative result:** Either the mission continues with realigned expectations, or it converts into an honest business — but the decision gets made on the written numbers and the stated mission, not by drift.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Claim high confidence to make the bet fundable, then privately know better.
**Why:** It would be incredibly hubristic to claim I'll beat every major lab to AGI, so I don't claim it — and the discipline isn't modesty, it's load-bearing. Inflated odds recruit money and people who expected the inflated outcome, and their disappointment arrives precisely at the long middle stretch where the bet most needs patient backers. Honest odds are slower to fund and vastly more durable.

**Don't:** Apply couple-percent-of-trillions reasoning while ignoring that you can't survive the 98%.
**Why:** The math I used explicitly rests on not being worried about ruin — I said at the time that it's a bad way for most people to think, and I meant the ones without the floor. Expected value is linear; life is not. A ruinous downside doesn't scale away no matter how large the payoff — shrink the bet until you can lose it, and then take it with full aggression.
