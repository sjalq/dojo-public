# The Advance Is Permanent; the Declines Are Temporary

## When to Use
- When a client is calling during a 20-30% drawdown asking whether to sell
- When someone says "this time it's different" about the current crisis
- When an advisor needs to anchor the conversation in long-term historical returns
- When a new client is being onboarded and needs to understand what to expect

## Fails When
- The advisor recites statistics without genuine emotional engagement; it sounds like a lecture, not counseling
- The client has already sold and is now sitting in cash; the conversation must shift from prevention to recovery
- The advisor has not established trust; historical data from someone you do not trust is just noise

## Core Concept

The post-World War II S&P 500 has delivered approximately 10% compound annual nominal returns over the long term. That return was not delivered in a straight line. It was delivered through a series of terrifying declines, averaging roughly 30% about every five years, followed by recoveries that exceeded the prior peak. Without exception. Every single time.

The advance of earnings, dividends, and share values is permanent. The declines in stock prices are temporary. This is the central thesis of Murray's life's work, and it is stated as a chapter title in Simple Wealth, Inevitable Wealth.

The critical insight is that the permanent advance and the temporary declines are inseparable. The premium return of equities over bonds (roughly 7% real versus 3% real over a century) is the compensation the equity investor receives for enduring the periodic, gut-wrenching temporary declines. An investor who is unwilling to ride out the declines does not earn the advance. There is no way to capture the long-term return while avoiding the periodic crashes, because the crashes end suddenly and violently upward. The market explodes off the bottom, and the investor who has gone to cash misses the recovery and is left behind.

Murray cites the same pattern across every major crisis of the modern era: the S&P 500 fell 34% in 33 days during COVID in 2020, then regained its peak in five months. From that trough to early 2025, it compounded at nearly 20% annually. The 2008-09 Global Financial Crisis saw a 57% decline over 17 months, followed by a 16% annual compound return over the next 16 years. The dot-com crash, the 2022 inflation bear market, the tariff panic of 2025: different causes, identical pattern. The insoluble problem gets solved. The great companies reassert themselves. The advance resumes.

$100,000 invested in the S&P 500 at the peak of the dot-com mania in 2000, moments before the worst series of crises in a generation, compounded to over $600,000 by late 2024. Seven genuine investor panics later. "This time it's different" did not tell the investor anything useful. Worse, it blinded him to the realization that something much more important and inexpressibly more powerful was going on throughout the entire period: the fundamental strength and resilience of the great American companies, expressed in their seemingly limitless capacity to innovate, increase their earnings, and raise their dividends.

## How to Apply

1. **Pre-train the expectation.** During onboarding, tell the client: "About every five years, about a third of your capital is going to appear to disappear. There will be a crisis. It will feel existential. Every financial journalist in the world will tell you it is unprecedented and that this time is different. They will be right about the surface details and catastrophically wrong about the long-term outcome. We are planning for this. This is not a surprise; it is a known cost."

2. **When the crisis hits, lead with empathy, then deploy the framework.** "I understand this is frightening. I feel it too. And I need you to hear me: the S&P 500 has compounded at 10% for 100 years through two world wars, a depression, stagflation, a global financial meltdown, a pandemic, and whatever this is. The advance is permanent. The declines are temporary. We planned for this."

3. **Name the pattern, then point to the recovery.** "In the 33-day COVID crash, did anyone stop and think: do I really believe that Apple, Walmart, JP Morgan, and Caterpillar have permanently lost a third of their intrinsic value? The prices of the stocks had broken off from the enduring values of the companies. They were moving in opposite directions. And within five months, the market had recovered completely."

4. **Refuse to predict the bottom or the recovery.** "I cannot tell you when this ends. Nobody can. What I can tell you is that it ends. It has always ended. And when it does, the market will not send you an engraved invitation to get back in. It will explode off the bottom without warning. The people who sold will still be sitting in cash, waiting for a pullback that never comes, or that comes to nowhere near the level at which they sold."

5. **Identify the "financially undead."** Murray uses this phrase for investors who went to cash during a crash and never got back in. They exist. They are still out there. Invite the client to imagine their ceaseless agony. The temporary pain of riding out the decline is nothing compared to the permanent pain of having sold and watched the market triple without you.

## Examples

**Situation:** The S&P 500 drops 21% in a matter of weeks during a tariff panic. A client calls wanting to sell everything and "wait for clarity."

**Application:** "I understand. This is exactly the kind of crisis that makes selling feel like the only rational thing to do. But let me share some context. When the S&P 500 was last at this level, it was January of last year. So at the very worst, all we've had to give back was the last one of 16 amazing years. Now, we do not know how the tariff situation resolves. But we know this: the VIX closed above 45 on 20 previous occasions, all clustered in 2008-09 and 2020, some of the greatest long-term buying opportunities of this century. There has never been a 12-month period following one of those readings in which the S&P 500 failed to achieve a positive total return. The average 12-month return was 39%. I am not predicting that. I am showing you what the historical record says about moments exactly like this one."

**Illustrative result:** The client stays invested. Twelve months later, they are grateful.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use "the advance is permanent, the declines are temporary" as a dismissive slogan.
**Why:** If the client hears it as "stop worrying, you're being irrational," the trust breaks. The phrase must be delivered with genuine empathy and backed by specific historical evidence, not recited as a bumper sticker.

**Don't:** Guarantee that any specific recovery will follow the historical pattern.
**Why:** Past performance is not a guarantee of future results. Murray frames every historical reference as "all of history and logic teaches us" and "what we can know," never as a prediction. The advisor must do the same.

**Don't:** Wait until the crisis to deliver this message for the first time.
**Why:** If the client has never heard this framework before the crash, it will sound like a desperate rationalization rather than a pre-planned response. The training must happen during onboarding, reinforced in every calm-market conversation, so that when the storm hits, the client thinks: "My advisor told me this would happen. We planned for this."
