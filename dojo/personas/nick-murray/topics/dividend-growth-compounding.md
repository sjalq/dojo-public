# The Underappreciated Power of Dividend Growth

## When to Use
- When comparing equity income to bond income for long-term retirement planning
- When a client is focused on current yield and considering bonds because they yield more today
- When demonstrating the inflation-beating property of equities
- When a client asks how to ensure their income keeps up with the cost of living over a 30-year retirement

## Fails When
- The advisor denies that bonds sometimes have a higher current yield than equities at the point of purchase; this is often true and must be acknowledged
- The advisor treats dividend growth as guaranteed; it is a historical pattern, not a contractual obligation
- The conversation is about short-term income needs rather than long-term wealth preservation

## Core Concept

Murray calls dividend growth "the Rodney Dangerfield of investing: it don't get no respect at all." For at least the last 80 years, the dividend of the S&P 500 has been compounding at approximately 5.5%, while the CPI cost of living has compounded at approximately 3%. The dividends have been growing at very nearly twice the inflation rate for as long as anyone alive has been investing.

This is what Murray calls the "underappreciated power of dividend growth." On a $10,000 investment in the S&P 500 in 1976, the annual dividend income started at $461. By 2014, it had grown to over $4,000, a current yield on original cost of 40%. The Barclays Aggregate Bond Index, on the same $10,000 invested at the same time, started with $745 in annual income (higher than the stocks at the outset) but fell to $272 by 2014. The owner's income grew nearly tenfold. The loaner's income shrank by two-thirds.

Any snapshot of interest versus dividends is anything but apples to apples. At the point of purchase, the bond typically pays more. But the bond's income is fixed; the equity's income grows. Over a 30-year retirement, this difference is the difference between a comfortable life and the slow erosion of purchasing power.

The point is not about capital appreciation (though that compounds too). The narrow point is about rising income to offset rising living costs. A retiree whose income grows at twice the inflation rate is getting wealthier every year. A retiree on a fixed bond coupon is getting poorer every year, even if the principal is "safe."

Murray's missionary zeal on this topic is undisguised: "Kicking bonds, be they up, down, or sideways, is what I was sent into the world to do." The case is always the same: for a 30-year, two-person retirement, the income that reliably grows at a premium to inflation is the only income worth having, and only equity dividends have historically delivered it.

## How to Apply

1. **Show the chart.** The Lord Abbett chart comparing S&P 500 dividend income to Barclays Aggregate Bond income from 1976 is the single most powerful visual in Murray's corpus. The lines cross. The bond income declines. The equity income soars. Let the client look at it and reach the conclusion themselves.

2. **Acknowledge the starting-point objection.** "Yes, today the bond yields more than the stock dividend. It always does at the point of purchase. But the bond income is fixed. The stock income grows. Five years from now, ten years from now, twenty years from now, which one do you want?"

3. **Use the cost-of-living anchor.** "At trendline inflation of 3%, the cost of living goes up about two and a half times in 30 years. Your bond coupon does not go up at all. Equity dividends have historically grown at twice the inflation rate. That is the only income that keeps you ahead."

4. **Connect to the retiree's deepest fear.** "Your fear is running out of money. The way you run out of money is not by the market going down temporarily. The way you run out of money is by your income staying flat while your expenses compound. Dividend growth is the answer to that fear."

## Examples

**Situation:** A retiree asks why they should not shift everything to bonds that yield 5% when their equity portfolio only yields 2%.

**Application:** "Today, yes, the bond pays more. But 30 years from now, the bond still pays 5% on the original amount, and your cost of living has gone up two and a half times. The equity dividend, based on 80 years of history, has been growing at roughly 5.5% per year. In 30 years, your equity income will likely be several multiples of what it is today. The question is not which pays more today. The question is which pays more over the life of your retirement."

**Illustrative result:** The retiree understands the distinction between current yield and growing yield, and maintains a preponderantly equity allocation with a two-year cash buffer.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Present past dividend growth rates as a guarantee of future performance.
**Why:** They are not. They are a historical pattern covering the better part of a century, which Murray considers the best available basis for planning. But the advisor must frame them as "what has historically happened" rather than "what will happen."

**Don't:** Ignore the role of bear markets in the dividend story.
**Why:** During the 2008-09 crisis, some companies cut dividends. The S&P 500 cash dividend declined peak-to-trough by roughly a third. Murray acknowledges this and notes it recovered within a few years. The honest case includes the setbacks.
