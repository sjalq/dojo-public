# Ask Nick / Scripts

## When to Use
- When an advisor needs specific, word-for-word language for a client conversation
- When handling objections about market timing, portfolio tweaking, fee justification, or "this time it's different"
- When preparing for a prospect meeting that may involve difficult questions
- When an advisor is new and needs a repertoire of proven responses

## Fails When
- The advisor treats scripts as rote recitation rather than as frameworks to internalize and adapt
- The advisor deploys a script without first empathizing with the client's emotional state
- The script is used to shut down conversation rather than to redirect it productively

## Core Concept

The "Ask Nick" clinic is a core feature of Nick Murray Interactive. Subscribers email Murray directly with specific client or prospect situations, and he formulates a response, usually within two business days. Selected exchanges are anonymously reprinted in the newsletter. The emphasis is always on giving advisors actionable, real-life scripts: what to say, not just what to think.

Murray's book Scripts is the book-length version of this clinic. The entire body of work rests on one insight: advisors need not just a philosophy but exact language to deliver that philosophy under pressure. When a client calls in a panic, or a prospect asks a loaded question, the advisor does not have time to derive the optimal response from first principles. The response must already be there, internalized, rehearsed, ready to deploy with conviction.

The scripts are not manipulative. They are the truthful expression of the behavioral investment counseling philosophy, packaged in language that the advisor can actually say out loud to a real human being. The format is always the same: the advisor describes the situation, Murray provides the words.

The defining characteristic of Murray's scripts is that they often refuse the premise of the question. A client asks about portfolio tweaking; Murray says do not respond directly to a stupid question. A prospect wants references and fee negotiation; Murray says tell them to find another advisor. A client asks what the advisor thinks about tariffs; Murray says the advisor does not have a view on tariffs because tariffs are irrelevant to the plan. The scripts teach the advisor to reclaim the agenda rather than chase the client's anxieties.

## How to Apply

1. **Build a personal script library.** Keep a document of tested language for the ten most common client situations. Update it after each difficult conversation with what worked and what did not. The goal is to never be surprised by a question.

2. **Internalize, then adapt.** Murray's scripts are starting points. Make them your own. Use your own words, your own cadence, your own personality. The principle must be Murray's; the voice must be yours.

3. **Refuse the premise when necessary.** When a client asks "Should we reduce our international exposure?", the answer is not about international exposure. The answer is: "Nowhere is it written that an advisor is obligated to speak directly to a question that is irrelevant to the plan. Let me redirect to what actually matters." This is a skill. Practice it.

4. **Deliver scripts with empathy, not authority.** "Thank you for your several inquiries about possible portfolio adjustments. I think the larger question is whether tweaking these things would meaningfully enhance the performance of the overall portfolio. In my judgment they would not." This is firm but respectful. It is not dismissive.

5. **Know when to end the conversation.** If the client perseverates after receiving a clear, empathetic answer, they are not listening. Stand on your statement. If they continue, it may be time to end the relationship. "If he comes back with another brace of these questions, he's not listening, and it's a pathology."

## Examples

**Situation:** A client with $2M and an 11% annual return net of fees for 15 years starts asking about U.S. versus international allocation, the dollar versus other currencies, and real estate exposure.

**Application:** Do not answer any of the individual questions. "Thank you for your several inquiries. In my judgment, none of those adjustments would meaningfully enhance the performance of the overall portfolio. The late Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman wonderfully said, 'We would all be better investors if we just made fewer decisions.' From my perspective, there are no decisions you currently need to make, and therefore none you should make."

**Illustrative result:** The client is redirected from fiddling to the only question that matters: have your goals changed? If not, the plan does not change.

**Situation:** A young advisor asks Murray how to create urgency with prospects who agree with the approach but will not move their money.

**Application:** "The first thing to realize is that there is no urgency as such. You cannot create what does not exist. But keep telling our story to more people, with more passion and conviction, speaking into people's hearts as much as and more than their minds. And remember: in the end, you cannot help people more than they want to be helped by you."

**Illustrative result:** The advisor stops trying to manufacture false urgency and focuses on reaching more people with authentic conviction.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use scripts to avoid thinking.
**Why:** Scripts are tools, not substitutes for judgment. Every client situation has nuances that require the advisor to think on their feet. The script provides the framework; the advisor provides the judgment.

**Don't:** Deliver a script that contradicts your actual practice.
**Why:** If you tell a client "we would all be better investors if we made fewer decisions" while simultaneously recommending quarterly portfolio tweaks, you have destroyed your credibility. The script must be consistent with how you actually run the practice.
