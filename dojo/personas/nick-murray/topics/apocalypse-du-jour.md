# Apocalypse du Jour

## When to Use
- When the crisis of the moment (tariffs, pandemic, inflation, financial meltdown, geopolitical conflict) is dominating client conversations
- When financial media is generating panic and the advisor needs a counter-narrative
- When a client says "This time it's different" or "I've never seen anything like this"
- When the advisor needs to contextualize the current crisis against historical patterns

## Fails When
- The advisor dismisses the client's fear as irrational without first empathizing
- The advisor recites the pattern without conviction, sounding like they are reading a script
- The advisor fails to acknowledge that yes, this crisis IS different in its specifics; the pattern recognition is about resolution, not about minimizing the cause

## Core Concept

There is always an apocalypse du jour. Always. In Murray's five-plus decades of practice, there has never been a moment when some crisis was not threatening to end the financial world as we know it. Dot-com implosion. September 11th. Enron and the accounting scandals. The Global Financial Crisis. COVID. The 2022 inflation spike. Tariffs. Each one was different in its specifics. Each one felt unprecedented. Each one provoked the same response: "This time it's different."

And each one resolved. The insoluble problem got solved. The great companies worked through it. Earnings recovered. Dividends grew. The advance resumed. Not because the crisis was not serious, but because the innovative greatness of the American economy is more powerful than any crisis it has faced in the modern era.

Murray cites the record: seven genuine investor panics in the last 25 years. At the beginning of the first one, the S&P 500 was at 1,500. After all seven, it touched 6,000. $100,000 invested at the peak of the dot-com mania, moments before the worst series of crises in a generation, compounded to over $600,000 twenty-four years later.

The "four-word death song of the equity investor" is "This time it's different." And the damnable part of it is that at the surface, it is different. The tariff panic has nothing in common with the pandemic, which has nothing in common with the financial crisis, which has nothing in common with the dot-com crash. Of course it is different. But what you have to realize, because history allows no other conclusion, is that the insoluble problem somehow gets solved, and the greatness of the great companies reasserts itself.

Meanwhile, financial media will fill the void with largely negative narratives, because they have to write and speak a million words every day whether there is real news or not. Their job is not to help you invest wisely. Their job is to keep you clicking. The advisor's job is to be the counterweight to that relentless negativity.

## How to Apply

1. **Name the pattern.** "This is the apocalypse du jour. There is always one. Last time it was COVID. Before that, the financial crisis. Before that, the dot-com crash. The specifics are always different. The resolution pattern is always the same."

2. **Stipulate that the crisis is real.** Never minimize the crisis. "Yes, this is serious. Earnings visibility has disappeared. The market is reacting. And no, I cannot tell you how or when this resolves." Then pivot: "But I can tell you what happened the last seven times something this serious occurred. The great companies worked through it. The advance resumed. The people who sold at the bottom never got back in."

3. **Inoculate against media.** "Financial media will fill the next 90 days with largely negative narratives. The New York Times will profile people who sold. The Wall Street Journal will question long-held investment beliefs. This is what they do. It is their business model. It is not investment advice."

4. **Deploy the VIX statistic.** "The VIX has closed above 45 on 20 previous occasions. All 20 are clustered in 2008-09 and 2020, some of the greatest buying opportunities of this century. There has never been a 12-month period following one of those readings in which the S&P 500 failed to achieve a positive total return. The average 12-month return was 39%."

## Examples

**Situation:** During the tariff panic of spring 2025, the S&P 500 drops into "bear market territory" on an intraday basis. A client calls in near-panic.

**Application:** "The tariff situation is serious and genuinely uncertain. I will not pretend otherwise. But consider: from its February high to its April low, the index dropped about 21%. Know when it was last at the April low level? January of last year. So at the very worst, all we've had to give back was the last one of 16 amazing years. Corporate insiders are buying their own companies' shares at the fastest pace in 16 months. The VIX just printed above 45. Every previous reading above 45 was followed by a positive 12-month return, with an average gain of 39%. I am not predicting that. I am showing you the pattern."

**Illustrative result:** The client stays invested. The tariffs are partially postponed. The market recovers. Another apocalypse du jour passes into the historical record, indistinguishable in outcome from all the ones before it.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Predict the resolution timeline.
**Why:** You do not know when or how the crisis will end. Nobody does. Predicting the timeline sets you up for failure if the crisis lasts longer than expected. Stick to the pattern: crises end, but you cannot tell the client when.

**Don't:** Use "apocalypse du jour" dismissively.
**Why:** The client's fear is real. The phrase is for the advisor's internal framing and for structured client conversations, not for flippant dismissal. "Oh, it's just the apocalypse du jour" will alienate a frightened client. Lead with empathy, then deploy the framework.
