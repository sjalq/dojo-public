# Purchasing Power, Not Principal, Is the Real Risk

## When to Use
- When a client says "I can't afford to lose my money" and is considering going to cash or bonds
- When a retiree is planning a 25-30 year retirement and considering a bond-heavy allocation
- When someone conflates the safety of principal with the safety of their financial future
- When explaining why bonds are not "safe" for long-term investors

## Fails When
- The distinction is presented as a lecture rather than as a caring reframe of the client's genuine concern
- The client's time horizon genuinely is short; for money needed within a year or two, principal preservation is the right goal
- The advisor uses this to dismiss the real emotional pain of watching a portfolio drop 30%

## Core Concept

Most investors, and tragically most retirees, assess risk in terms of principal: "My $500,000 must not go below $500,000." This is a fatal confusion. The real risk in a three-decade, two-person retirement is not that your principal will fluctuate. The real risk is that you will outlive your purchasing power. That the cost of everything you buy, food, healthcare, housing, will compound relentlessly upward while your income stays flat or declines in real terms.

Murray distinguishes between "currency" and "money." Currency is the number of dollars you have. Money is what those dollars will buy. A dollar of fixed income buys less every year. At trendline inflation of 3%, the cost of living goes up about two and a half times in 30 years. A retiree who puts $500,000 into bonds yielding 5% is earning $25,000 a year today. In 30 years, that $25,000 buys what $10,000 buys today. The principal is "safe." The purchasing power is destroyed.

This is what Murray means when he writes: "In real life, bonds are destructive of clients' purchasing power over time, while equities are peerlessly accretive to it. And, again in real life, purchasing power is the single most important of all financial elements. Indeed, it's the only rational definition of money."

The solution is equity ownership, specifically for the dividend growth. Since at least 1960, the dividends of the S&P 500 have been growing at a rate very close to twice the CPI, just under 6% for dividends versus just over 3% for inflation. A retiree whose income grows at twice the rate of inflation is getting wealthier every year of retirement, even while drawing down. A retiree on fixed-income bonds is getting poorer every year, even if the statement balance never drops.

## How to Apply

1. **Make inflation concrete.** "In the five years ending December 2025, the CPI rose approximately 25%. A dollar of fixed income now buys only three-quarters of what it did five years ago. And it will not go back down. Prices never go down. They only go up, at some greater or lesser rate."

2. **Use the postage-stamp analogy.** Show what a stamp cost 30 years ago and what it costs today. That is inflation. That is what a fixed income cannot keep up with. What are you doing to make sure your income grows faster than that?

3. **Contrast the income trajectories.** "On the same $10,000 invested in 1976, the S&P 500's annual dividend income grew from $461 to over $4,000. The bond index's annual income fell from $745 to $272. Which one of those looks safe to you for a 30-year retirement?"

4. **Reframe the question.** When a client says "I can't afford the risk of stocks," respond: "The question is which risk you prefer. The risk of temporary fluctuation with permanently growing income, or the risk of permanent purchasing-power destruction with nominally stable principal. Both are real risks. Only one of them has a happy ending."

## Examples

**Situation:** A couple approaching retirement says they want to move everything to bonds because "we can't afford to take risks anymore."

**Application:** "A non-smoking couple retiring at 62 has a joint life expectancy of 30 years. At trendline inflation, the cost of living goes up about two and a half times in those 30 years. If you put your money in bonds yielding 5%, you will be earning the same dollar amount in year 30 that you earn in year one, but it will buy less than half as much. That is the risk. The risk is not that your statement balance will fluctuate. The risk is that in year 25, you cannot afford your groceries."

**Illustrative result:** The couple understands that the real risk is on the bond side, not the equity side. They maintain an equity-dominant allocation with two years of living expenses in cash as a buffer.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Dismiss the client's concern about volatility as irrational.
**Why:** It is entirely rational to be afraid of watching your portfolio drop 30%. The advisor's job is to validate the emotion while reframing the risk: "Your fear of volatility is natural. But volatility is temporary. Inflation is permanent. The real danger is the one you cannot see on your statement."

**Don't:** Suggest that bonds have no role in a portfolio.
**Why:** Murray explicitly recommends one to two years of living expenses in cash or cash equivalents. The argument is about the bulk of long-term capital, not about eliminating all fixed income. Overstating the case undermines credibility.
