# The Game of Numbers

## When to Use
- When an advisor is building a practice and needs a client-acquisition framework
- When an advisor is discouraged by rejection and questioning whether this career will work
- When an advisor is spending too much energy on individual outcomes and not enough on inputs
- When discussing the economics and discipline of professional prospecting

## Fails When
- Volume replaces quality: showing your work to 100 people who are not your target market is not prospecting
- The advisor has no clear value proposition; the game of numbers only works when the advisor has something genuinely worth showing
- The advisor uses this as a mechanical process without genuine enthusiasm for the work

## Core Concept

Murray's book The Game of Numbers is about professional prospecting for financial advisors. The core insight is reducible to a formula: P = 1/N. A qualified prospect (P) equals one divided by N, the number of activities you do. All you have to do is find your N, and then you do not need to worry about any individual lost P, because you know the formula for converting prospects to clients.

The formula is a tautology, true by definition. But its power is psychological. It redirects the advisor's attention from outcomes (which are outside their control) to inputs (which are entirely within their control). The quality of your business in the long run is an absolute function of the number of people you show your work to. Worrying about how any one person responds, or feeling personally rejected by any ten or twenty people who turn you aside, is irrational when you understand that the law of large numbers will get you wherever you want to go.

This is the algebraic expression of a deeper Murray principle: focus on inputs, not outcomes. The advisor controls the quality of their pitch, the conviction with which they deliver their philosophy, the number of people they see, and the authenticity of their engagement. The advisor does not control whether any individual prospect says yes. Accepting this fully eliminates the despair that kills advisory careers in their early years.

Murray's own experience: "You're inevitably going to get there as long as you don't stop." The game of numbers is a framework for not stopping, for understanding that every "no" brings you one step closer to the "yes" that the law of large numbers guarantees.

## How to Apply

1. **Find your N.** Track how many prospect interactions it takes to produce one new client. This is your number. Now your job is not to close the next prospect. Your job is to do N activities.

2. **Focus on inputs, not outcomes.** You control: the clarity of your value proposition, the conviction with which you present it, the number of people you present it to, and the authenticity of your follow-up. You do not control: whether they say yes. Act on what you control.

3. **Detach from individual rejections.** A rejection is not a failure. It is one unit of N. Each rejection brings the formula closer to producing the next P. The math does not care about your feelings. The math always works.

4. **Keep your value proposition sharp.** The game of numbers only works when the advisor has something genuinely worth showing. If your pitch is vague, unclear, or not authentically yours, no amount of volume will fix it. Get the message right, then do volume.

## Examples

**Situation:** A new advisor has been at it for two years and is considering quitting because growth feels impossibly slow.

**Application:** "How many people did you show your work to last month? If the answer is ten, that's your problem. You're making a sample-size error and calling it a career conclusion. Find your N. If it takes 20 contacts to produce one client, do 40 this month. The math always converges. You are inevitably going to get there as long as you don't stop."

**Illustrative result:** The advisor refocuses on volume and inputs. Within months, the practice begins to grow, not because anything changed about the market or the message, but because the law of large numbers started working.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Confuse volume with value.
**Why:** Showing a bad pitch to 100 people is not prospecting; it is spam. The game of numbers requires a clear, authentic value proposition. Volume without value produces nothing but annoyance.

**Don't:** Use P = 1/N to avoid improving your approach.
**Why:** If your N is 50 (fifty contacts per client), that is a signal that your approach needs work. A good N for a skilled advisor might be 5 to 10. Track it, and if it is too high, improve the message before increasing the volume.
