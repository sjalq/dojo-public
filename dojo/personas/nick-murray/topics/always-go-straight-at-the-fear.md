# Always Go Straight at the Fear

## When to Use
- When meeting a prospective client couple for the first time and sensing unspoken anxiety
- When a client says they want to make a change but never follows through
- When an advisor realizes they are spending more time on the client's money than on the client's emotions
- When the traditional sales approach of reassuring past the fear is not producing results

## Fails When
- The advisor asks about fears mechanically, as a technique, rather than with genuine curiosity
- The advisor hears the fear and immediately tries to "fix" it with a reassurance or a statistic; the goal is to understand, not to overcome
- The advisor lacks the emotional capacity to sit with a client's fear without becoming defensive

## Core Concept

In the sales paradigm, when a prospect expresses fear, the trained response is to reassure past it. "Don't worry, we're a very fine firm, we won't let that happen." This is outcome-oriented: the goal is to get the business. It does not work because it does not address the fear. It papers over it. The prospect still has the fear; they have just been told not to talk about it.

Murray's alternative: always go straight at the fear. Not to the exclusion of hopes and goals, but packaged with them and given equal weight. Ask the prospect directly: "What are the things you most hope will happen in your relationship with a financial advisor? And, just as important, what do you fear might happen?"

Most advisors spend far more time thinking about the client's money than about the client's emotions, and far less thinking about their fears in particular. This almost always comes back to haunt the advisor. A fear that has never been named will surface at the worst possible moment, during a market crash, and the advisor will have no established framework for addressing it.

When you go straight at the fear, you learn what the real barrier is. If the answer has Bernie Madoff in it, you know you are the issue: the prospect does not trust advisors. If it has "the risk of the stock market" in it, you know you are talking to people who do not understand the difference between volatility and risk. In either case, you have started to track the fear to its lair, something you could never have accomplished with empty, outcome-oriented reassurance.

The discipline of always going straight at the fear weans the advisor off the critical mistake of spending too much time on money and too little on emotions.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask the question explicitly.** "What do you fear might happen?" Not "What are your concerns?" which is too corporate. Not "Are you worried about anything?" which invites a "no." Ask about fear directly.

2. **Listen without solving.** When the prospect describes the fear, do not immediately reassure. "I can certainly understand that." Then probe: "How would you see that happening?" Track the fear to its source before offering anything.

3. **Distinguish the input from the outcome.** The sales paradigm says: "This person is afraid; I need to overcome the objection to get the business." The input paradigm says: "This person is afraid; I need to understand the fear to see if we can build a genuine relationship." The outcome (getting the business) is outside your control. The input (genuine empathy, honest engagement with the fear) is entirely within it.

4. **Use the fear conversation to pre-train for crises.** If the prospect's fear is "I'll lose everything in a crash," that is the opening for: "That fear is understandable. Let me show you what actually happens in crashes, and what my job will be when that day comes."

## Examples

**Situation:** A prospective client couple sits across from the advisor. They are referred. They seem willing but hesitant.

**Application:** Instead of launching into the presentation, the advisor says: "Before we talk about money, I want to ask you two questions. What do you most hope will happen from working with a financial advisor? And what do you fear might happen?" The couple exchanges a look. The wife says: "I'm afraid someone will take our money and we'll have nothing for retirement." The advisor says: "I can understand that. Tell me more about where that fear comes from." The conversation opens up. The curtain begins to come down. The advisor has not said a word about asset allocation.

**Illustrative result:** The couple decides to work with the advisor not because of a superior investment strategy, but because for the first time, someone in the financial industry asked about their fear and listened to the answer.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Treat "going straight at the fear" as a closing technique.
**Why:** If the goal is to get the business, the prospect will sense it. The goal is to understand the human being sitting across from you. The business follows from genuine understanding, not from tactical deployment of empathy.

**Don't:** Skip the fear conversation and go straight to the plan.
**Why:** The plan is correct. The asset allocation is sound. But if the client's fears have never been surfaced, the first crisis will blow the relationship apart, because the advisor will be addressing the portfolio while the client is drowning in unnamed fear.
