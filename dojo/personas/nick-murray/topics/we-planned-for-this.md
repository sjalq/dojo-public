# "We Planned for This"

## When to Use
- During or immediately before a significant market decline, to steady clients
- During new-client onboarding, to pre-build the expectation of periodic declines
- When an advisor needs a specific, actionable script for crisis conversations
- When preparing materials (a seminar, a letter, a Client's Corner essay) to preempt panic

## Fails When
- The advisor did not actually build the expectation of periodic 30% drawdowns into the plan; the statement is then a lie
- The phrase is delivered without emotional conviction; clients can tell the difference between a scripted line and a deeply held belief
- The advisor uses it to avoid a real conversation; "we planned for this" is the opening of a conversation, not the end of one

## Core Concept

Murray's framework begins with a factual observation: the 10% long-term compound return of the S&P 500 included an average drawdown of roughly 30% on an average of about every five years across the entire post-WWII period. These declines are not aberrations. They are structurally embedded in the return. An advisor who assumes the historical return must also assume the historical frequency and depth of temporary declines. They are inseparable.

This means that from the very first moment an advisor proposes a long-term plan, the advisor can and should make it unmistakably clear: "We fully expect the onset of an average decline of perhaps a third or so, on an average of about every five years." The plan already accounts for this. The contributions are calibrated to this. The asset allocation assumes this. When the decline arrives, the advisor can say with fierce conviction: "In a very real sense, we planned for this."

The power of the phrase comes from its truth. It is not a motivational slogan. It is a statement of fact about how the plan was built. The client protests that the current crisis is different from all previous ones. Of course it is. Every crisis capable of peeling a third off the prices of America's greatest companies is starkly different from all the others. The shock of that difference is what generates the panic. But the resolution pattern is always the same: the great companies work through the problem, earnings recover, dividends rise, and the advance resumes.

Murray introduced this framework explicitly in a November 2024 essay of the same title, as preparation for what he was already anticipating: the next market decline, whenever it came. The phrase is designed to be a pre-rehearsed, pre-committed response that the advisor has practiced, believed, and internalized before the crisis hits, not invented in the heat of the moment.

## How to Apply

1. **Build the expectation during onboarding.** "Our plan assumes mainstream equities will continue to deliver their historical return of approximately 10% per year. That return has always included temporary declines averaging about a third, about every five years. We are not hoping those declines won't happen. We are planning for the certainty that they will."

2. **Rehearse the crisis script in advance.** When the market drops 15%, 20%, 30%, the advisor delivers: "We planned for this. This is what we told you would happen. This is what the plan was built to absorb. Your goals have not changed. Therefore your plan does not change."

3. **Stipulate that the crisis is different, then redirect.** The client says this crisis is unprecedented. The advisor says: "You're right. The details are always different. COVID was different from the Global Financial Crisis was different from the dot-com crash. Every one of those terrified people into selling. Every one of those was followed by a recovery that exceeded the prior peak. The plan does not depend on knowing which crisis will hit or when. The plan depends on staying invested through all of them."

4. **Refuse to predict the timeline.** "I cannot tell you when this will end. I can tell you that it will end, because every crisis in the modern era has ended, and the great companies have come through stronger than before. Our job right now is not to predict. Our job is to hold."

5. **Reinforce after the recovery.** When the market recovers, come back to the client and say: "Remember when we were at the bottom and I said we planned for this? This is what I meant. The plan worked. The plan always works if you don't get in there and blow it up."

## Examples

**Situation:** During the COVID crash, the S&P 500 fell 34% in 33 days. A long-time client calls in near-panic.

**Application:** "I know this is terrifying. I feel it too. But we built your plan around the historical fact that this happens roughly every five years. COVID is the crisis; the plan was built to absorb the crisis, whatever it turns out to be. Did you really believe that the enduring value of Apple, Walmart, JP Morgan, and Caterpillar has been permanently impaired by a third? The panic-driven prices of the stocks have broken off from the enduring values of the companies. We planned for this. Stay the course."

**Illustrative result:** The S&P 500 regained its peak in five months. From that trough to early 2025, it compounded at nearly 20% annually. The client who stayed in captured the entire recovery. The client who sold at the bottom became one of the financially undead.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use "we planned for this" as your only response to a panicking client.
**Why:** The phrase is the door-opener, not the entire conversation. The client needs empathy, historical context, and a specific reaffirmation of their goals and plan. "We planned for this" is the first sentence, not the last.

**Don't:** Say "we planned for this" if you did not actually build the expectation of periodic declines into the plan.
**Why:** If the client was told to expect 10% annual returns and was never told about the 30% drawdowns that come with them, "we planned for this" is a lie. It will destroy trust permanently.

**Don't:** Allow the phrase to become a substitute for genuine conviction.
**Why:** The words only work if the advisor believes them with their whole being. An advisor who is privately terrified and publicly reciting a script will be found out. The conviction comes from having internalized the historical record, not from having memorized a sentence.
