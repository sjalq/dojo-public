# Tear Down the Curtain

## When to Use
- When an advisor is building relationships with new prospects
- When an advisor has technically sound advice but clients will not follow it
- When an advisor is relying on intellectual proof rather than emotional connection
- When a client seems to agree with the plan but will not move assets

## Fails When
- The advisor treats trust as a technique to be deployed rather than as a byproduct of genuine care
- The advisor has not done the internal work of believing in their own value proposition
- The advisor is still in the sales paradigm, trying to "get the business" rather than establish a relationship

## Core Concept

There is a naturally existing curtain of distrust between a financial advisor and a prospective client. People do not come to a potential advisory relationship with the same predisposition to trust that they would accord a physician, an accountant, or an attorney. They arrive with a reservoir of distrust, a fear, however unconscious, that they may be taken advantage of.

If the advisor is actively aware of this curtain and perceives it as the barrier it is, the advisor will begin dealing with it immediately and will keep dealing with it until the curtain is torn down. If the advisor ignores it, the curtain remains, impeding not just the ability but the willingness to speak warmly and freely to the human issues that really matter.

The most common mistake advisors make is trying to talk through the curtain rather than tearing it down. They carry a self-imposed burden of "proof," relying on their ability to answer financial questions convincingly: explaining the yen carry trade, citing standard deviation, walking through the efficient frontier. All of this is head, not heart. And it does not work. You cannot convince someone through the curtain. The curtain has to come down first.

There is a world of difference between simply assuring people that they need not worry, blessedly devoid of amplification, and trying to demonstrate to them intellectually or statistically why they need not worry. "A bunch of allegedly sophisticated speculators piled into an allegedly sophisticated trade that used a ton of leverage. It is now blowing up on them, as all such trades do sooner than later. This is not something a long-term investor in great businesses, like you, needs to be concerned about. It's a sandstorm, not a real crisis." That says the client need not worry rather than why, and it carries immeasurably more emotional weight because it rests on trust, not on data.

The curtain comes down through empathy, authenticity, and the willingness to be emotionally present with the client. It comes down when the advisor stops trying to prove things and starts trying to connect. The how does not matter nearly as much as the decision to go there.

## How to Apply

1. **Recognize the curtain exists.** In every first meeting, assume the prospect has a reservoir of distrust. It is not personal. It is structural. The financial services industry earned that distrust over decades.

2. **Lead with heart, not head.** Instead of explaining why your asset allocation methodology is superior, say: "Can you imagine what it feels like to know that you won't run out of money in retirement, that your dividend income alone historically grows at twice the inflation rate?" Heart first, then a supporting statistical fact. Not the other way around.

3. **Go straight at the fear.** Ask the prospect: "What are the things you most hope will happen in your relationship with a financial advisor? And, just as important, what do you fear might happen?" People will welcome this candor. The emotional distance between you and the close shrinks dramatically when they get to talk openly about their fears.

4. **Stop trying to prove things.** If the prospect asks about the yen carry trade, do not deliver a lecture. Dismiss it offhandedly: "It's a sandstorm, not a real crisis." The prospect does not need to understand the mechanics. The prospect needs to trust that you understand the mechanics and that this is not worth their worry. Your confidence communicates more than your data.

5. **Make tearing down the curtain a daily priority.** Every morning, wake up with a renewed commitment to touching clients emotionally. Bring this to your team. The curtain does not come down once; it is a continuous process of deepening trust through authenticity.

## Examples

**Situation:** A young advisor with five years of experience has mastered the intellectual framework of Behavioral Investment Counseling but cannot get prospects to move their assets over.

**Application:** Murray diagnoses the problem: too much head, not enough heart. The advisor has been carrying an entirely self-imposed burden of proof. The fix is not better arguments; it is deeper emotional connection. "In one intensely human sentence: 'There'll come a day when most people you know will be panicking out of their long-term investments, and you won't be, because of the work we'll be doing together.' Nobody ever forgets that sentence. That is the essence of Behavioral Investment Counseling."

**Illustrative result:** The advisor shifts from features to benefits, from intellectual proof to emotional resonance, and prospects begin moving forward.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Respond to the prospect's fear with data.
**Why:** Data does not dissolve fear. Connection does. If the prospect says "I'm worried about losing my retirement money" and you respond with a 100-year return chart, you have talked through the curtain instead of tearing it down.

**Don't:** Fake empathy.
**Why:** People can sense authenticity. If the empathy is a sales technique, the curtain stays up. The advisor must genuinely care about the client's fears and outcomes, or the relationship will not survive the first real crisis.
