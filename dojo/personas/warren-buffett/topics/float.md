# Float as Investable Capital (Insurance Economics)

## When to Use
- You want to understand how Berkshire Hathaway built its capital base through insurance
- You are evaluating an insurance company and need to understand what makes one more valuable than another
- Someone asks how it is possible to obtain "free" money for investment
- You are studying the relationship between underwriting discipline and investment returns

## Fails When
- The insurance company has consistently poor underwriting discipline and the float is actually very expensive
- The user is asking about insurance regulation, actuarial science, or claims management rather than capital allocation
- The question is about personal insurance purchasing decisions, not business economics

## Core Concept

Float is money an insurance company holds but does not own. In an insurance operation, float arises because premiums are received before losses are paid. The interval between premium collection and loss payment can stretch to many years, especially in reinsurance and long-tail liability lines. During that interval, the insurer holds the money and can invest it.

What counts in an insurance business is, first, the amount of float generated and, second, its cost. Float is a major component of an insurer's intrinsic value that is not reflected in book value. An insurance business has value if its cost of float over time is less than the cost the company would otherwise incur to obtain funds. But the business is an albatross if the cost of its float is higher than market rates for money.

The cost of float is determined by underwriting results. In years when the insurer earns an underwriting profit, the cost of float is negative. In those years, the insurer is being paid to hold money. This is the economic equivalent of being given a loan with no interest, and in some years being paid to take the loan.

This sounds too good to be true, and for most insurance companies, it is. The industry as a whole frequently incurs underwriting losses, which means the float is expensive. The reason is simple: most insurers compete on price, which drives premiums below the level needed to cover losses and expenses. The competitive dynamics of insurance naturally push toward underpricing, because the consequences of underpricing take years to appear while the premium revenue appears immediately.

The exceptional insurer, one with underwriting discipline and the ability to price risk accurately, can generate large amounts of float at zero or negative cost. Over time, this float compounds into an enormous investment portfolio. The key insight is that this portfolio was funded not by equity capital or debt but by someone else's money, held at no cost. The returns on the invested float flow entirely to the shareholders.

Since 1967, when Berkshire entered the insurance business, its float grew at an annual compounded rate exceeding 20% while the cost of that float, in more years than not, was less than nothing. This access to free money was the primary engine of Berkshire's growth. The acquisition of GEICO materially increased the probability of continuing to obtain free funds in increasing amounts.

## How to Apply

1. **When evaluating an insurer, measure float growth and cost.** Calculate float by adding loss reserves, loss adjustment reserves, funds held under reinsurance, and unearned premium reserves, then subtracting agents' balances, prepaid acquisition costs, and deferred charges. Track the cost by measuring underwriting profit or loss as a percentage of average float. Compare that cost to the yield on long-term government bonds for the same period.

2. **Prize underwriting discipline above premium growth.** An insurer that grows premiums rapidly while losing money on underwriting is accumulating expensive float. An insurer that grows slowly but maintains underwriting profits is accumulating free money. The second is far more valuable.

3. **Understand that float is a form of leverage.** Float amplifies investment returns because the capital being invested does not belong to the shareholders. When float is free or negative-cost, this is costless leverage, which is the best kind. When float is expensive, it is worse than debt because the cost is variable and can spike in catastrophe years.

4. **Look for structural cost advantages in the insurance operation.** GEICO's direct-to-consumer model permanently undercuts the agent-based model on costs. That structural advantage translates directly into underwriting profit, which translates into free float, which translates into investment returns for shareholders.

5. **Recognize that catastrophe risk is the price of admission.** In the super-cat reinsurance business, years of excellent results will be punctuated by truly terrible years. A truly terrible year is not a possibility; it is a certainty. The only question is when it will come. The investor who understands float economics is not surprised by the occasional catastrophe loss and does not panic-sell.

## Examples

**Situation:** An investor is comparing two insurance companies. Company A has $10 billion of float and has achieved an average underwriting profit of 3% of premiums over the last decade. Company B has $15 billion of float but has run an average underwriting loss of 5% of premiums.

**Application:** Company A's float has been free. The $10 billion has been invested at no cost to shareholders, and the investment returns flow entirely to equity holders. Company B's float has been expensive. The 5% underwriting loss means Company B is paying roughly $750 million per year for the privilege of holding $15 billion. Whether that is cheaper or more expensive than debt depends on the comparison to market interest rates, but it is certainly not free.

**Illustrative result:** Company A, despite having less float, is the superior insurance operation because the quality of its float is higher. The investor who focuses only on the amount of float, ignoring its cost, will systematically overvalue poorly underwritten insurers.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Assume all insurance float is free.
**Why:** Float is only free when underwriting is profitable. Most insurance companies, most of the time, run underwriting losses. The size of the float is irrelevant if the cost exceeds market interest rates.

**Don't:** Ignore the lumpy nature of catastrophe losses.
**Why:** Super-cat reinsurance can produce spectacular results for years and then record a catastrophic loss in a single quarter. An investor who extrapolates the good years without reserving for the bad ones will be shocked when the bill arrives. The correct expectation is that the business will be lumpy, and the question is whether the long-run average cost of float, including catastrophe years, remains attractive.
