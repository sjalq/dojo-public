# Owner Successor Planning / "Praise by Name, Criticize by Category"

**Attribution note:** "Praise by name, criticize by category" is Tom Murphy's phrase, given to Buffett sixty years ago. Buffett adopted and applied it throughout his career. The management philosophy is Buffett's own, developed over six decades of running Berkshire and selecting subsidiary managers. The succession approach reflects Buffett's handling of the CEO transition to Greg Abel (effective 2026-01-01).

## When to Use
- You are selecting a CEO or a key operating leader
- You are planning leadership succession for a business you own or run
- You need a framework for how much authority to delegate to operating managers
- You want to assess whether a management team thinks like owners or like bureaucrats

## Fails When
- The business is so small that the owner IS the management and there is nobody to select or delegate to
- The user is looking for a management process framework (sprints, OKRs); this is about character and structure
- The organization has deep operational dysfunction that requires hands-on intervention, not delegation

## Core Concept

Managing Berkshire is primarily a job of capital allocation, coupled with the selection and retention of outstanding managers to captain the operating subsidiaries. The CEO's job also requires replacing a subsidiary's CEO when that is called for. These duties require a rational, calm, and decisive individual who has a broad understanding of business and good insights into human behavior. It is important as well that the CEO knows his or her limits.

The management philosophy rests on several interlocking principles.

**Extreme delegation.** Berkshire has never had a committee at headquarters, never required subsidiaries to submit budgets, and has no departments for human relations, public relations, investor relations, strategy, or acquisitions. The operating managers run their businesses as they did before Berkshire acquired them. This is not negligence; it is a deliberate structural choice. After all, what can corporate headquarters tell operating managers about the details of businesses they have run for decades?

**Character over credentials.** The cardinal qualities in a manager are integrity, intelligence, and energy, but if the first is missing, the other two will destroy you. A CEO who is "all in" for the company rather than for personal enrichment is rare and priceless. Whether someone attended a famous school has never been a factor. A very large portion of business talent is innate, with nature swamping nurture.

**Praise by name, criticize by category.** When discussing specific successes, name the person responsible. When discussing failures, discuss the category of mistake without naming the individual. This is not political correctness; it is practical wisdom. Public praise reinforces the behavior you want. Public blame of individuals for honest mistakes creates a culture of risk aversion and cover-ups.

**Admit mistakes early.** The cardinal sin is delaying the correction of mistakes, what Munger called thumb-sucking. Problems cannot be wished away. They require action, however uncomfortable. The CEO who never uses the word "mistake" in shareholder letters is either deluded or dishonest.

**Resist the ABCs of business decay.** The successor's most important quality, beyond capital allocation skill, is the ability to fight arrogance, bureaucracy, and complacency. When these corporate cancers metastasize, even the strongest companies falter. Only a vigilant and determined CEO can ward off these forces.

**Plan succession early and honestly.** The right time to plan succession is when the current leader is at peak effectiveness, not after decline has begun. Unexpected longevity has its own risks: the people designated as successors age too, and their peak effectiveness may not wait. The goal is to accelerate the transition while the successor is at full strength and the outgoing leader can provide support rather than obstruction.

## How to Apply

1. **Select for character first.** When evaluating a CEO candidate, check whether they think like an owner. Do they speak candidly about mistakes? Do they resist the institutional imperative? Do they care about the business's long-term competitive position or about their own compensation ranking? If the character check fails, stop. Intelligence and energy without integrity are dangerous.

2. **Delegate radically and verify through results.** Give operating managers full authority over their businesses. Do not impose corporate budgets, strategy reviews, or performance processes from above. Trust produces better results than directives, reviews, and layers of bureaucracy. Verify through economic outcomes, not through process compliance.

3. **Apply "praise by name, criticize by category" in communication.** In annual reports, in board meetings, and in internal communications, name the individuals who produced exceptional results. When discussing mistakes, discuss the type of mistake (capital allocation error, personnel misjudgment, underwriting failure) without naming the responsible individual unless the situation requires accountability.

4. **Act on succession before it is urgent.** Identify and develop successors while the current leaders are strong. Do not wait for illness, age, or crisis to force the decision. The board should discuss succession at every meeting, not as a crisis item but as an ongoing responsibility.

5. **Protect the culture through tone at the top.** The successor's most important job is maintaining the culture that makes the organization work. At Berkshire, that means a shareholder-first attitude, extreme delegation, minimal bureaucracy, and a willingness to admit mistakes. If these non-economic values are lost, the economic value will follow.

## Examples

**Situation:** A holding company's CEO is 72 and the board has not discussed succession. The CEO is still effective but has not identified or developed any internal candidates.

**Application:** This is already late. The board should immediately identify the two or three qualities that matter most for the next CEO (probably capital allocation skill, cultural fit, and the ability to resist the institutional imperative). They should assess internal candidates against those criteria and, if no strong internal candidate exists, begin a discreet external search. The current CEO should be involved but should not have veto power over the process.

**Illustrative result:** A structured succession plan is in place before the current CEO's effectiveness declines. The transition, when it happens, is orderly and does not create a power vacuum or a culture disruption.

**Situation:** A subsidiary CEO has made a significant capital allocation error that cost the company $50 million. The holding company's CEO must address this in the annual shareholder letter.

**Application:** Praise by name, criticize by category. In the letter, discuss the type of mistake (capital allocation into a declining market without adequate understanding of the competitive dynamics) without naming the subsidiary CEO. The mistake was honest, not caused by fraud or negligence. Publicly shaming the manager would cause talented people to avoid taking risks, which is worse than the $50 million loss.

**Illustrative result:** Shareholders understand that mistakes happen and are addressed. The subsidiary CEO learns from the error without being publicly humiliated. The culture of honest risk-taking is preserved.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Confuse delegation with abdication.
**Why:** Extreme delegation works only when you have selected managers of exceptional character and competence. Delegating to mediocre or dishonest managers is not empowerment; it is negligence. The selection step must precede the delegation step.

**Don't:** Delay removing a manager who has lost effectiveness, whether through dishonesty, declining ability, or cultural misfit.
**Why:** The failure to act on a management problem, particularly when dementia or other debilitating conditions are involved, is one of the most common and most damaging mistakes in corporate governance. The board must be alert and the CEO must be willing to act, however uncomfortable the conversation.
