# Mr. Market

**Attribution:** This allegory belongs to Benjamin Graham, Buffett's teacher at Columbia Business School. Buffett explicitly credits Graham every time he invokes it. It appears in Graham's *The Intelligent Investor* and Buffett retold it in his 1987 shareholder letter. Do not present it as Buffett's own invention.

## When to Use
- You are watching stock prices fluctuate wildly and feeling the urge to act on the movement itself
- You need a mental model for the relationship between market quotations and business reality
- Someone is making investment decisions based on price trends rather than value assessment
- The market is in a panic or a euphoria and you need to decide whether the mood is justified

## Fails When
- The price decline accurately reflects a permanent impairment of the business (Mr. Market is occasionally right)
- The user has no capacity or interest in valuing the underlying business; without that skill, ignoring Mr. Market is just stubborn holding
- The question is about short-term trading strategies where the entire game is played inside Mr. Market's moods

## Core Concept

Imagine that you own a small share of a private business. Every day, a partner named Mr. Market shows up and offers to buy your interest or sell you his. He always quotes a price, and that price is driven entirely by his mood. Some days he is euphoric and sees nothing but favorable factors, so he names a very high price. Other days he is depressed and terrified, naming a very low price.

The critical point is that Mr. Market does not mind being ignored. Transactions are strictly at your option. The more manic-depressive his behavior, the better it is for you, because it gives you more chances to buy cheaply when he is gloomy and to sell expensively when he is giddy. But there is one absolute warning: Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his pocketbook, not his wisdom, that is useful. If you fall under his influence and let his moods dictate your decisions, you have turned a servant into a master.

This allegory may seem quaint in an age of quantitative models, dynamic hedging, and beta coefficients. But those tools are techniques shrouded in mystery that clearly have value to the purveyor of investment advice. What witch doctor ever achieved fame and fortune by simply advising "Take two aspirins"? The reality is simpler: investment success comes from coupling good business judgment with the ability to insulate your thoughts and behavior from the contagious emotions of the marketplace.

The market, in the short run, is a voting machine reflecting popularity. In the long run, it is a weighing machine reflecting value. The speed at which business success is recognized is not important as long as intrinsic value is increasing at a satisfactory rate. Delayed recognition can even be an advantage: it gives you the chance to buy more of a good thing at a bargain price.

## How to Apply

1. **Establish intrinsic value independently.** Before you ever look at the stock price, work out what the business is worth based on its earning power, competitive position, and cash generation. If you cannot do this, Mr. Market will control you because you have no independent anchor.

2. **Treat quotations as offers, not information.** When Mr. Market names a price, the only question is: does this offer suit me? A price below your estimate of value is an opportunity to buy. A price above your estimate is an opportunity to sell (or do nothing). A price near your estimate is noise.

3. **Check your emotional state.** If you feel compelled to act because prices are moving, you are being guided by Mr. Market. Step back. The business has not changed in the last hour; only Mr. Market's mood has. Make sure any action you take is justified by the economics, not the quotation.

4. **Use volatility as your ally.** High volatility means Mr. Market is offering extreme prices more often. For the investor who has done the valuation work, this is a gift. For the investor who has not, it is a trap.

5. **Remember you can always say no.** Mr. Market will be back tomorrow with a new price. There is no compulsion to act today unless the price is genuinely extraordinary relative to value.

## Examples

**Situation:** A long-term investor owns shares in a business with stable earnings and a wide moat. The stock drops 30% in a week because of a broad market sell-off triggered by geopolitical news.

**Application:** Check the business fundamentals. Has the earning power changed? Has the competitive position eroded? If the answers are no, Mr. Market is simply in a depressed mood. The correct response is either to do nothing or to buy more, depending on whether the new price represents a genuinely attractive entry point relative to intrinsic value.

**Illustrative result:** The investor who ignores Mr. Market's panic and focuses on business value avoids selling a wonderful business at a terrible price. The investor who follows Mr. Market's mood locks in a permanent loss on a temporary quotation.

**Situation:** A stock the investor owns has doubled in six months without any meaningful change in the underlying business. The investor feels excited and vindicated.

**Application:** Mr. Market is euphoric. The question is not whether you feel good but whether the current price exceeds intrinsic value. If it does substantially, consider selling. If the business remains wonderful and the price is not absurd, consider holding. But do not confuse Mr. Market's enthusiasm with confirmation that you are a genius.

**Illustrative result:** The disciplined investor either trims or holds based on valuation, not on the thrill of being up 100%.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Anchor your view of a stock's value to its recent price history.
**Why:** The fact that a stock traded at $100 last month tells you nothing about whether it is worth $70 today. Mr. Market's past quotations are as irrelevant as his current mood. Value comes from the business, not the tape.

**Don't:** Assume that because you are ignoring Mr. Market, you are automatically right.
**Why:** Mr. Market is sometimes correct. If a stock drops 50% and the business is genuinely impaired, holding and buying more is not disciplined investing; it is wishful thinking. The discipline of Mr. Market is not "always ignore the price." It is "always have your own estimate of value and compare it to the price."
