# Circle of Competence

## When to Use
- You are considering an investment or business decision in a domain where your knowledge may be thin
- You are tempted by an opportunity that looks attractive but that you cannot independently evaluate
- You want a framework for deciding what to say yes to and what to pass on
- You need to audit your own knowledge honestly before committing capital

## Fails When
- You draw the circle so small that you never act on anything
- You use it as an excuse to never expand your knowledge
- You overestimate the size of your circle because of past success in adjacent areas
- You confuse familiarity with understanding; reading about an industry is not the same as understanding its economics

## Core Concept

You do not have to be an expert on every company or even many. You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence. The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital.

This is the central idea. Most people get it backwards. They worry about how much they know. The productive worry is about what they do not know, and whether they are honest with themselves about the boundary.

To invest successfully, you need not understand beta, efficient markets, modern portfolio theory, option pricing, or emerging markets. You may, in fact, be better off knowing nothing of these. Investment students need only two well-taught courses: How to Value a Business, and How to Think About Market Prices.

Your goal as an investor should be to purchase, at a rational price, a part interest in an easily understandable business whose earnings are virtually certain to be materially higher five, ten, and twenty years from now. Over time, you will find only a few companies that meet these standards, so when you see one that qualifies, you should buy a meaningful amount of stock.

The discipline this framework demands is double-edged. First, be honest about what you understand. Second, when you do find something within your circle, act decisively. Thumb-sucking, passing up opportunities you are fully capable of understanding, has been among the most costly mistakes at Berkshire. The errors of omission, the big purchases served up on a platter that you fully understood and yet did not make, haunt you far more than the errors of commission.

The circle is not fixed. It can be expanded through study, experience, and honest feedback. But it cannot be expanded overnight, and expanding it to justify a decision you have already made is self-deception, not learning.

## How to Apply

1. **Map your circle honestly.** What industries do you genuinely understand well enough to value a business in them? Can you explain, without looking anything up, how the economics of that business work, what drives revenue, what threatens margins, and what the competitive landscape looks like?

2. **Test the boundary before you invest.** If you cannot write a one-page investment thesis that would convince a skeptical partner, you are outside your circle. If you cannot identify the three most important risks and explain why you think they are manageable, you are outside your circle.

3. **Stay inside the circle when deploying capital.** When you find a business you understand deeply, at a price that makes sense, buy a meaningful amount. Do not spread your capital across 40 businesses you vaguely understand. Concentrate on the few you truly know.

4. **Expand the circle deliberately, not reactively.** Study new industries because you are curious and disciplined, not because a hot opportunity showed up. The expansion is real only when you can pass the same honesty tests you apply to your existing circle.

5. **Resist the temptation to stray.** If you are not willing to own a stock for ten years, do not even think about owning it for ten minutes. This is not a slogan; it is a direct consequence of circle-of-competence discipline. If you do not understand a business well enough to hold it for a decade, you do not understand it well enough to buy it today.

## Examples

**Situation:** A successful real estate investor is pitched on a biotech startup by a friend. The projected returns are enormous. The investor knows nothing about drug development timelines, FDA approval processes, or clinical trial design.

**Application:** This is outside the circle. The projected returns are meaningless because the investor cannot independently evaluate the probability of success. The right answer is to pass, no matter how compelling the pitch sounds, and to feel no regret about it. There is no sin in missing a great opportunity outside one's area of competence.

**Illustrative result:** The investor preserves capital for opportunities within the circle, where the ability to evaluate risk is real rather than borrowed from someone else's optimism.

**Situation:** An investor has followed the insurance industry for 20 years and understands the economics of float, underwriting discipline, and claims reserves. A well-run insurer is trading at a significant discount to intrinsic value because the market is panicking about a hurricane season.

**Application:** This is inside the circle. The investor understands the business well enough to judge that the hurricane losses, while real, do not impair the long-term earning power. The right answer is to buy a meaningful position, not to hedge with a small speculative bet. When you see a business within your circle at a price that makes sense, act.

**Illustrative result:** The investor earns an excellent return because the willingness to act within the circle, combined with the discipline to pass outside it, concentrates capital where understanding is highest.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Confuse "I've read an article about it" with genuine understanding.
**Why:** If history supplied all the answers, the Forbes 400 would consist of librarians. Surface familiarity is not competence. Can you identify the three things that could destroy this business's economics? If not, you are a tourist, not an analyst.

**Don't:** Let FOMO pull you outside the circle.
**Why:** The fear of missing out is what drives investors to buy things they do not understand at prices they cannot justify. Every great investor has a long list of opportunities they passed on. The pain of missing a winner is far less than the pain of buying a loser you never understood.

**Don't:** Assume your circle is static.
**Why:** A circle of competence that never grows is a circle of competence that is actually shrinking, because industries and economics evolve. Study deliberately, but do not confuse the studying with the knowing.
