# Philanthropic Pledge / "Ovarian Lottery"

## When to Use
- A wealthy person is wrestling with how much to give and why
- Someone asks whether the rich have a moral obligation to give back
- A user wants to understand the relationship between luck, skill, and wealth
- Discussing how to structure giving: during lifetime versus at death, through endowments versus current spending

## Fails When
- The user is looking for investment advice or tax strategy
- The question is about how to run a foundation effectively (that is operational philanthropy, not the philosophical framework)
- The user has no interest in the moral dimension of wealth and just wants to maximize their estate

## Core Concept

Wealth on the scale that some people accumulate is the product of a combination of factors: living in a market economy that rewards certain skills disproportionately, possessing the right genetic endowments, and compounding capital over time in a system that makes this possible. Both the wealthy person's children and the wealthy person themselves won what Buffett calls the ovarian lottery. The odds against being born in a particular country, at a particular time, with particular abilities, in a particular family, are enormous.

The reaction to extraordinary good fortune should not be guilt but gratitude. And gratitude, when honest, leads naturally to the conclusion that retaining vastly more wealth than one could ever use while others drew short straws in the same lottery is an allocation that makes no sense on its own terms.

More than 99% of Buffett's wealth is pledged to philanthropy during his lifetime or at death. Measured by dollars, this is a large commitment. In a comparative sense, though, millions of people who regularly contribute to churches, schools, and other organizations give more, because they relinquish funds that would otherwise benefit their own families. The dollars dropped into a collection plate mean forgone dinners, movies, and personal pleasures. In contrast, the Buffett family gives up nothing they need or want by fulfilling the pledge.

The pledge does not contribute the most precious asset, which is time. Many people give extensively of their own time and talents to help others, and gifts of that kind often prove far more valuable than money.

What money can do, however, is convert claim checks, stock certificates that represent command over resources, into benefits for those who received the short straws in life. The directing principle is that the money should be spent on current needs rather than building permanent endowments. Nothing should go to endowments because the problems are now and the money should work now.

The children are the right trustees because they have the maturity, brains, energy, and instincts to disburse a large fortune. They also have the advantage of being above ground when new circumstances arise, and can adapt to a significantly changing world. Ruling from the grave does not have a great record.

Some material things make life more enjoyable; many would not. Too often, a vast collection of possessions ends up possessing its owner. The asset most worth having, aside from health, is interesting, diverse, and long-standing friends.

## How to Apply

1. **Acknowledge the role of luck honestly.** Before deciding how much to give, honestly assess how much of your wealth is the result of being born in the right place, at the right time, with the right abilities. The market system rewards the ability to detect mispriced securities with sums reaching into the billions while rewarding a teacher with thank-you notes. Fate's distribution of long straws is wildly capricious.

2. **Give during your lifetime, not only at death.** Lifetime giving allows you to see the impact, make corrections, and involve the next generation in the practice of philanthropy while they are at their peak effectiveness. Delayed giving risks the money being deployed by trustees who may not share your values or understand your intentions.

3. **Spend on current needs, not endowments.** Endowments accumulate capital for the future while current problems go unaddressed. Unless there is a specific reason to build a permanent institution, direct the money to problems that exist now.

4. **Trust your children but do not rule from the grave.** Give your trustees the flexibility to adapt. The world will change in ways you cannot anticipate, and rigid instructions from someone who is no longer alive will increasingly miss the mark.

5. **Do not confuse philanthropy with absolution.** Giving away wealth does not erase mistakes or injustices. It is simply the rational response to having drawn an extraordinarily long straw. The goal is not moral credit but the efficient reallocation of resources from where they do little good (a sixth home, a third yacht) to where they do the most good.

## Examples

**Situation:** A successful entrepreneur worth $200 million is debating whether to leave the majority of the estate to children or to philanthropic causes.

**Application:** Ask what the children actually need. If they have already received enough for education, housing, and a comfortable life, additional wealth beyond that is neither needed nor necessarily beneficial. A very large inheritance can rob the next generation of the motivation to build their own lives. The surplus, which may be the vast majority of the estate, can do far more good directed to philanthropic causes than sitting in a trust that produces dynastic wealth.

**Illustrative result:** The entrepreneur structures the estate to provide meaningfully for the children while directing the surplus to causes that address real current needs. The children know the plan, understand the reasoning, and ideally participate in the philanthropic work during the parent's lifetime.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Assume that because you earned the money, you deserve to keep all of it.
**Why:** You earned the money inside a system, in a country, with abilities that you did not choose. The market rewards certain talents disproportionately. Acknowledging this does not diminish your effort; it contextualizes it.

**Don't:** Build permanent endowments as monuments to yourself.
**Why:** Endowments accumulate capital and pay administrators while current problems go unsolved. The goal of philanthropy is impact, not permanence. Spend the money on current needs and let future generations solve future problems with future resources.
