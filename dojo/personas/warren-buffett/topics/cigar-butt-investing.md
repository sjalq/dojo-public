# Cigar-Butt Investing (and why Buffett moved away from it)

**Attribution note:** The cigar-butt metaphor and the self-critique are Buffett's own. The shift from cigar butts to quality investing is credited by Buffett himself to Charlie Munger's influence. The phrase "forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices" is Buffett's summary of Munger's blueprint.

## When to Use
- You are tempted to buy a stock primarily because it is statistically cheap relative to its assets
- Someone presents a "deep value" opportunity in a business with deteriorating fundamentals
- You want to understand the philosophical evolution from Graham-style net-net investing to Buffett-Munger quality investing
- A business is available at a discount to book value but has no competitive moat

## Fails When
- The investor genuinely has the skills and timeline for liquidation or special-situations investing (cigar-butt investing did work at small scale in the 1950s)
- The cheap stock in question actually has durable economics and is simply mispriced; that is a margin-of-safety opportunity, not a cigar butt

## Core Concept

A cigar butt found on the street that has only one puff left in it may not offer much of a smoke, but the bargain purchase will make that puff all profit. If you buy a stock at a sufficiently low price, there will usually be some hiccup in the fortunes of the business that gives you a chance to unload at a decent profit, even though the long-term performance of the business may be terrible.

That approach worked for small sums. In the 1950s, dozens of these free puffs produced the best decade for both relative and absolute investment performance. Ben Graham taught the technique, and it worked. But a major weakness gradually became apparent: cigar-butt investing was scalable only to a point. With large sums, it would never work well.

The deeper problem is not just scale. Unless you are a liquidator, buying mediocre businesses at bargain prices is foolish for two reasons. First, the original "bargain" price probably will not turn out to be such a steal after all. In a difficult business, no sooner is one problem solved than another surfaces. There is never just one cockroach in the kitchen. Second, any initial advantage you secure will be quickly eroded by the low return that the business earns. If you buy a business for $8 million that can be sold for $10 million and promptly take that course, the return is fine. But if the business is sold for $10 million in ten years and has earned only a few percent on cost in the interim, the investment is deeply disappointing.

Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre. A marginal business purchased at a cheap price may be attractive as a short-term trade, but it is the wrong foundation on which to build a large and enduring enterprise. Selecting a marriage partner requires more demanding criteria than does dating.

The corrective, taught by Munger, is simple: buy wonderful businesses at fair prices. A wonderful business is one with a durable competitive advantage that earns high returns on capital and can reinvest its earnings at similar rates. Over time, the compounding of such a business overwhelms even the most cleverly timed cigar-butt purchases.

## How to Apply

1. **When you see a cheap stock, ask why it is cheap.** If the answer is "the business is declining and the market is pricing in continued deterioration," the cheapness is probably justified. Statistical cheapness in the absence of quality is a cigar butt, not a bargain.

2. **Test for the cockroach problem.** Has this business had one problem, or does it have a pattern of problems? In difficult businesses, problems are not isolated events; they are structural features. If management has been "turning around" the business for five years and the economics have not improved, the next turnaround plan will not work either.

3. **Ask whether you would want to own this business for ten years.** If the answer is no because the economics are poor and the competitive position is eroding, then you are not investing; you are hoping for a greater fool to show up and take the position off your hands at a higher price.

4. **Compare the cigar butt to a quality alternative.** Even if the cigar butt produces a 20% gain in one year, the quality business compounding at 15% for ten years will produce a far better outcome. The cigar butt requires finding the next cigar butt, and the next, and the next. The quality business compounds on its own.

5. **Accept the lesson honestly.** The shift from cigar butts to quality investing is not a theoretical improvement; it is a confession of practical experience. The cigar butts produced good early results but eventually led to mediocre businesses consuming capital and management attention for years. The quality investments, compounding quietly, produced the real wealth.

## Examples

**Situation:** An investor finds a regional retailer trading at 60% of book value. The company has been losing market share for five years but still has valuable real estate on its balance sheet.

**Application:** This is a classic cigar butt. The "bargain" is the discount to book value. But the business is in decline, the real estate value will erode as the retail locations lose traffic, and the management has no plausible plan to reverse the competitive deterioration. The investor might extract one puff of profit if the real estate is monetized, but the ongoing business will continue to consume capital and management energy.

**Illustrative result:** The better use of the capital is a business with a wide moat trading at a fair price. The compounding of quality over ten years will dwarf the one-time gain from the cigar butt.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Confuse statistical cheapness with investment opportunity.
**Why:** A stock trading at 4 times earnings in a permanently declining industry is not cheap; the earnings are disappearing. A stock at 20 times earnings in a business with a widening moat and growing cash flows may be the actual bargain.

**Don't:** Assume that because Buffett once practiced cigar-butt investing, it is a valid strategy today.
**Why:** Buffett explicitly describes it as a mistake for anyone operating at scale or seeking to build enduring wealth. The strategy worked in a specific era with small sums. At any meaningful capital base, it produces mediocre results and consumes disproportionate time and attention.
