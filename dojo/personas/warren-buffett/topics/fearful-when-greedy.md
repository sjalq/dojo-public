# Be Fearful When Others Are Greedy, and Be Greedy When Others Are Fearful

## When to Use
- The stock market has fallen sharply and fear is widespread
- The market has risen sharply and euphoria is widespread
- You are deciding whether to invest, hold, or sell in an emotionally charged environment
- Someone asks when the right time to buy is

## Fails When
- You use the maxim as license to buy anything that has dropped in price, without evaluating the business
- The widespread fear is justified by genuine and permanent impairment of business value
- You apply it to speculative assets (crypto, meme stocks) where there is no underlying business to value

## Core Concept

A simple rule dictates buying: be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy when others are fearful. This is not a timing system or a market-prediction tool. It is a behavioral discipline that exploits the single most reliable pattern in markets: the tendency of crowds to oscillate between euphoria and panic, overshooting in both directions.

When fear is widespread, gripping even seasoned investors, businesses of lasting value get sold at prices that make no sense relative to their long-term earning power. The fears regarding the long-term prosperity of sound companies are irrational, even though their short-term earnings will suffer. Most major companies will be setting new profit records five, ten, and twenty years from now. The market's short-term mood says nothing about those long-term realities.

Conversely, when greed is widespread, prices detach from fundamentals and reflect hope, momentum, and narrative rather than earning power. At those moments, the disciplined investor restrains, sells overpriced holdings, and accumulates cash for the next fear cycle.

The critical caveat: this works only if you have done the valuation work. Buying into fear without understanding what you are buying is not contrarian investing; it is blind gambling in a down market. Circle of competence and margin of safety must apply before this maxim kicks in. You are buying sound businesses at marked-down prices, not buying everything that has dropped.

History reinforces the pattern relentlessly. During the Depression, the Dow hit its low in July 1932, months before the economic bottom. In the early days of World War II, the market bottomed in April 1942, well before Allied fortunes turned. In the early 1980s, the time to buy was when inflation raged and the economy was in the tank. Bad news is an investor's best friend because it lets you buy a slice of the future at a marked-down price.

If you wait for the robins, spring will be over. The market will move higher well before either sentiment or the economy turns up. If you wait for the comfort of good news, you will miss the opportunity.

## How to Apply

1. **When the market drops, check the businesses, not the headlines.** Are the businesses you own or want to own permanently impaired, or are they temporarily suffering along with everything else? If the underlying economics are intact, the price decline is an opportunity.

2. **Act on value, not on mood.** The question is never "how do I feel about the market?" The question is "are there businesses I understand, with durable competitive advantages, trading at prices well below intrinsic value?" If yes, buy. If no, wait.

3. **Build cash during euphoria.** When the market is euphoric and prices are high, finding bargains is hard. This is the time to restrain, to hold more cash, and to wait. Cash is sometimes thought of as an unproductive drag on returns, but cash is to a business as oxygen is to an individual: never thought about when it is present, the only thing in mind when it is absent.

4. **Do not try to time the bottom.** Nobody can reliably predict the exact low. The goal is to buy when prices offer a margin of safety, not to catch the precise turning point. Buying a wonderful business at a 30% discount to intrinsic value is a good outcome even if the stock drops another 10% before recovering.

5. **Resist the temptation to sell into panic.** Those who bought stocks only when they felt comfort and then sold when headlines made them queasy consistently destroy wealth. The hapless investor's pattern is to buy at the top when confidence is high and sell at the bottom when fear is overwhelming.

## Examples

**Situation:** It is October 2008. The financial world is in crisis. Unemployment is rising, business activity is faltering, and headlines are terrifying. A long-term investor is holding significant cash and considering putting it to work.

**Application:** The widespread fear means that sound companies are trading at prices that do not reflect their long-term earning power. The investor who has done the valuation work can identify businesses with durable competitive advantages at marked-down prices. The short-term outlook is genuinely bad, but the long-term prosperity of the nation's many sound companies is intact.

**Illustrative result:** The investor who buys during the panic earns extraordinary long-term returns. The investor who waits for the "all clear" signal misses the bulk of the recovery because markets move ahead of the economy.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Treat this maxim as a market-timing system.
**Why:** It is a behavioral discipline, not a prediction tool. You are not predicting when the bottom will occur or when the recovery will start. You are disciplining yourself to buy when others are selling in panic and to restrain when others are buying in euphoria.

**Don't:** Be "greedy" about things you do not understand.
**Why:** The maxim assumes you have done the valuation work and know the businesses you are buying. Being greedy about unfamiliar assets during a panic is not contrarian investing; it is hope masquerading as discipline.
