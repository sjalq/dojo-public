# Annuities as Longevity Insurance

## When to Use
- A client is approaching or past age 70 and the primary risk is outliving the portfolio
- The client has no pension and Social Security alone is insufficient to cover basic expenses
- The advisor wants to add a guaranteed-income floor to the retirement plan without relying on a three-bucket or complex income-layering approach
- Interest rates are at a level where annuity payout rates are reasonable (roughly correlated with 10-year Treasury yields)

## Fails When
- The client is in their 50s or early 60s; at younger ages, a traditional total-return portfolio with a cash-flow reserve is more efficient because the mortality return has not yet kicked in meaningfully
- The advisor recommends an expensive variable annuity with a 7 percent front-end load instead of a low-cost SPIA or DIA
- The client's portfolio is so large that longevity risk is immaterial and the annuity serves no planning purpose
- Rates are extremely depressed and the annuity payout is too low to justify locking up capital

## Core Concept

For a long time, I would wash my mouth out with soap before talking about annuities. The costs were too high, the products were too opaque, and the commissions created intolerable conflicts of interest. I have completely changed my mind, and I say that plainly because updating your views when the evidence changes is exactly what a responsible practitioner should do.

Single-premium immediate annuities (SPIAs) and deferred-income annuities (DIAs) will be the single most important tool in the coming decade for clients at or near retirement, largely because their fees have come down to reasonable levels. This is not about variable annuities, which remain expensive and appropriate only in narrow circumstances such as asset protection in states where they are considered insurance products and protected from creditors.

The key concept is the mortality return. Traditional investments offer three sources of return: capital gains, dividends, and interest. An annuity adds a fourth: the mortality return. When you buy a SPIA, you are pooling longevity risk with a large group of people. Some will die earlier than the actuarial average, and the money they leave on the table subsidizes the payments to those who live longer. That subsidy is the mortality return, and it starts to become meaningful around age 70.

Before age 70, there is very little mortality return because very few people are dying. A 55-year-old who buys a SPIA is essentially buying a very expensive bond. But a 72-year-old who buys a SPIA is buying something that no traditional investment can replicate: a guaranteed income stream that continues no matter how long they live, backstopped by the mortality return from those who did not.

I do not think annuities make much sense until someone is in probably their late 60s or 70s. At that point, the mortality return kicks in and the product becomes genuinely powerful.

There is a behavioral benefit too, similar to the cash-flow reserve. A client with a SPIA providing guaranteed lifetime income knows that their basic expenses are covered no matter what. The investment portfolio becomes purely aspirational: travel, gifts, legacy. That psychological separation makes it easier for the client to stay invested through volatility because the grocery money is guaranteed.

Research shows that 20 percent or more of a portfolio should be in SPIAs for many retirees. For fee-only advisors, this is uncomfortable because it means losing 20 percent or more of the fee base. We are going to have to suck it up, because it is the right thing to do. The advisor's fee is a consequence of the plan, not a driver of it.

Annuity returns are roughly dependent on 10-year Treasury rates. When rates are very low, I am inclined to wait. The risk of waiting is that mortality goes up (the client gets older and the annuity becomes more expensive per dollar of payout), but that incremental cost has historically been modest relative to the benefit of buying at higher rates. It is a judgment call, not a formula.

A 10-year guarantee period is worth considering. If the client dies the day after purchasing, the annuity continues paying for 10 years, going to heirs. The cost of this guarantee reduces the monthly payout only modestly. It does not eliminate the longevity risk the client faces, but it substantially reduces the "what if I die tomorrow" objection that stops many clients from acting.

## How to Apply

1. **Evaluate whether longevity risk is the binding constraint.** If the plan fails primarily because the client might live too long, not because of poor returns or excessive spending, an annuity may be the right tool. If the plan fails for other reasons, fix those first.

2. **Wait until the client is in their late 60s or older.** The mortality return is negligible before then. A total-return portfolio with a cash-flow reserve is more efficient for younger retirees.

3. **Size the annuity to cover the gap between guaranteed income and essential expenses.** Social Security plus pension (if any) covers some of the floor. The annuity covers the rest. Do not annuitize aspirational spending; that belongs in the investment portfolio.

4. **Consider current interest rates.** If 10-year Treasury yields are very low (below 2-3 percent), the annuity payout will be correspondingly low. Consider deferring the purchase if the client's cash-flow reserve can bridge the gap.

5. **Use a 10-year guarantee period.** The reduction in monthly payout is small, and it addresses the legitimate concern that the client might die shortly after purchase. Framing: "If you pass away early, your family receives the payments for 10 years. If you live to 95, you receive the payments for 25 years. Either way, you are covered."

6. **Accept the fee impact honestly.** If the plan says the client should annuitize 20 percent of the portfolio, recommend it even though it reduces your fee by 20 percent. The fiduciary standard demands it.

## Examples

**Situation:** A 71-year-old client with $1.5 million in savings, Social Security of $2,800 per month, and monthly essential expenses of $6,000 wants to know if they should buy an annuity.

**Application:** The income gap is $3,200 per month ($38,400 per year). At current rates, a SPIA with a 10-year guarantee costs approximately $400,000 to $450,000 for a $38,400 annual payout. That leaves $1.05 to $1.1 million in the investment portfolio for growth, aspirational spending, and legacy. Run the plan both ways: with and without the annuity. The version with the annuity has a dramatically higher probability of success through age 95 because the essential expenses are guaranteed regardless of market performance.

**Illustrative result:** The client purchases the SPIA. Their essential expenses are now fully covered by Social Security plus the annuity. The investment portfolio becomes purely aspirational. The client stops worrying about market declines because the grocery money is guaranteed for life.

---

**Situation:** A 58-year-old client asks whether they should buy a deferred-income annuity now that begins payments at age 80.

**Application:** At 58, the mortality return is negligible. The DIA is essentially a very expensive, illiquid bond for the next 22 years. A diversified total-return portfolio with a cash-flow reserve will almost certainly produce better outcomes over that horizon. Recommend deferring the annuity decision to the late 60s or early 70s when the mortality return justifies the purchase. In the meantime, continue building the portfolio and managing it with the core-and-satellite approach.

**Illustrative result:** The client avoids locking up capital prematurely and maintains the flexibility that a 22-year horizon demands. The annuity conversation is revisited at 68 or 70, when the math is more favorable.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Recommend high-cost variable annuities when a low-cost SPIA or DIA is more appropriate.
**Why:** Variable annuity expenses are typically 2 to 3 percent annually, plus surrender charges, plus the cost of riders. For most retirees, a simple SPIA with low or no ongoing expenses is the right tool. Variable annuities have narrow use cases (asset protection in certain states, tax deferral for clients who have maxed out every other vehicle), not broad ones.

**Don't:** Let your fee incentive drive the annuity recommendation.
**Why:** If the plan says 20 percent of the portfolio should be annuitized, recommend it even though it reduces your AUM fee. A fiduciary who skips the annuity recommendation to protect their revenue is not acting as a fiduciary. The research is clear. We have to suck it up.

**Don't:** Treat annuities as either always good or always bad.
**Why:** Annuities are a tool. Like any tool, they have conditions where they are appropriate (older client, longevity risk, reasonable rates) and conditions where they are not (young client, very wealthy client, very low rates). The dogmatic positions, both for and against, are wrong. Evaluate each situation on its merits.
