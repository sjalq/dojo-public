# Two-Bucket Cash-Flow Reserve Strategy

## When to Use
- A client is retired or within five years of retirement and needs a systematic way to generate income from a portfolio
- A client's primary anxiety is "where does the grocery money come from if the market crashes?"
- An advisor wants a distribution framework that is simple to implement, simple to explain, and behaviorally robust through bear markets
- A client is currently using a dividend-and-interest income strategy and needs a better alternative

## Fails When
- The cash reserve is sized too large, creating excessive opportunity cost that drags long-term returns
- Someone bolts on additional buckets (a bond bucket, an equity bucket, a long-term care bucket) and turns a simple structure into an unmanageable set of moving parts
- The advisor fails to rebalance and replenish the reserve when markets are up, letting it run dry
- The client has no financial plan underneath, no goals quantified, no risk tolerance assessed; no distribution mechanism fixes the absence of planning

## Core Concept

The idea is almost embarrassingly simple, and that is its greatest strength. You take the client's portfolio and split it into two pieces. The first is what I call the cash-flow reserve: enough liquid money, in money market or short-term instruments, to cover roughly one year of the client's basic living expenses beyond whatever they receive from Social Security, pensions, or other income. The second piece is everything else, the long-term investment portfolio, allocated across stocks and bonds in whatever proportion the financial plan dictates.

The cash-flow reserve is set up, through whichever custodian the client uses, to pay out on a monthly basis like a paycheck. My partner Deena Katz calls it "managing the paycheck syndrome." People who have worked for decades are used to getting regular payments. When they retire and that paycheck stops, there is a psychological gap. The monthly payout from the reserve fills that gap. Clients know where the grocery money is coming from.

The behavioral payoff is the headline benefit. In October 1987, the Monday after the crash, I told my small staff to get on the phones. Nobody had called. The first person I reached was a retired widow on Miami Beach who sounded like my grandmother. I said, "You may have noticed the market was a little rocky." She said, "I know. Five years, five years, five years." It was a non-event for her. She knew where her grocery money was coming from, and she did not have to sell a single share of anything to get it. We found the same response through the tech bust and the Great Recession. Nobody was happy, but nobody panicked, nobody sold out, and we did not lose clients.

I originally started with a two-year reserve in the mid-1980s. Later, working with two professors in the Texas Tech program, we did a more sophisticated analysis and concluded that one year was sufficient. One year keeps the opportunity cost manageable while preserving the behavioral benefit. The key principle underlying the sizing is what I call the five-year mantra: do not invest anything you are going to need in five years or less. That money needs to be liquid. The cash-flow reserve is the operational expression of that principle for clients in the distribution phase.

This is explicitly a two-component structure. I have heard many people take the bucket concept and extend it to three, four, or more buckets, splitting bonds into a separate bucket, adding a long-term care bucket, and so on. I think those approaches have serious fatal flaws. They add complexity, they add cost, they make rebalancing harder, and at the end of the day, they bifurcate what should be thought of as a total-return portfolio. The virtue of two buckets is that you have your spending money over here, safe and separate, and everything else is managed holistically as a total-return investment portfolio. Simple. Manageable. Behaviorally effective.

As a quarterly rebalancing discipline, you look at the reserve and top it up if it has been drawn down. If markets are up, you are selling some winners and refilling. If markets are down, the reserve buys you time; you do not have to sell losers to pay the grocery bill. We almost never had to go beyond the reserve, but as a fail-safe, we always had a short-term bond ladder in the investment portfolio that could serve as a second tier of liquidity if needed. In decades of practice, we never had to use it.

## How to Apply

1. **Quantify the gap.** Determine the client's annual basic living expenses (mortgage, food, utilities, insurance, not aspirational travel) and subtract all non-portfolio income (Social Security, pensions, annuity payments, part-time work). The remainder is what the reserve needs to fund for one year.

2. **Carve out the reserve.** Move that amount into a money market or short-term bond fund, held separately from the investment portfolio. Set up an automatic monthly payout through the custodian so the client receives a check or deposit every month.

3. **Invest the remainder as a total-return portfolio.** The long-term portfolio is allocated according to the financial plan: typically 40 to 70 percent equities, balance in bonds, with no artificial constraint from dividend or interest yields. This portfolio is managed for total return, not income generation.

4. **Rebalance quarterly and top up the reserve.** Each quarter, rebalance the investment portfolio toward its target allocation. When doing so, check the reserve. If it has been drawn down (by several months of payouts), replenish it from whatever the rebalancing is producing. You are selling the things that went up and using part of the proceeds to refill the reserve.

5. **In a severe downturn, do not replenish from equities.** If stocks are down significantly, do not sell equities to refill the reserve. Let the reserve draw down. If it gets low, sell short-term bonds from the investment portfolio as a second tier of liquidity. This keeps the client from being a forced seller of equities at the worst possible time.

6. **Communicate the structure to the client in one sentence.** "Your grocery money for the next year is sitting in cash, separate from your investments, and we will refill it as we go. You do not have to worry about what the market does today." That sentence is the entire behavioral intervention.

## Examples

**Situation:** A recently retired couple has $1.2 million in savings, $40,000 per year from Social Security, and annual living expenses of $85,000. They need $45,000 per year from the portfolio.

**Application:** Carve out $45,000 into a money market account, set up to pay $3,750 per month automatically. The remaining $1,155,000 goes into a 60/40 total-return portfolio. Each quarter, when the portfolio is rebalanced, the reserve is topped up if it has dropped below about $35,000. When markets crash, the couple sees their monthly deposit continuing to arrive. They do not panic. They do not call to sell.

**Illustrative result:** Through multiple bear markets, the couple stays invested. The total-return portfolio recovers and grows. The behavioral stability of knowing where the grocery money comes from is the difference between staying the course and making a catastrophic sell decision at the bottom.

---

**Situation:** An advisor is using a three-bucket approach (cash, bonds, equities) and finding it difficult to manage rebalancing across the buckets. A client asks why the bond bucket underperformed.

**Application:** Simplify to two buckets. Collapse the separate bond and equity buckets into a single total-return investment portfolio. The cash reserve covers one year of expenses. Everything else is managed holistically. The advisor stops having to explain which bucket to draw from in a downturn and stops fragmenting the asset allocation.

**Illustrative result:** Portfolio management becomes simpler, cheaper, and more transparent. The client understands "your spending money is here, your investments are there." Rebalancing is one action, not three. The behavioral benefit is preserved without the complexity.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Size the cash reserve at two or three years "to be safe."
**Why:** Every dollar in the reserve earns close to nothing after inflation. A three-year reserve on $50,000 of annual needs is $150,000 sitting in cash, dragging long-term returns. One year has been demonstrated to be sufficient. The opportunity cost of excess cash is real and compounds over a multi-decade retirement.

**Don't:** Use a dividend-and-interest strategy as the income mechanism instead of total return with a cash reserve.
**Why:** A dividend-and-interest approach forces the asset allocation to be driven by whatever yields happen to prevail at the time, not by what the client actually needs. When interest rates are low, the allocation shifts excessively toward bonds or high-yield instruments. When rates rise, the value of those holdings drops but the client feels richer because income went up. The client's feelings are the opposite of what is actually happening in the portfolio. Total return with a cash reserve severs that perverse feedback loop.

**Don't:** Add a third, fourth, or fifth bucket for different time horizons, asset classes, or goals.
**Why:** Every additional bucket adds complexity, transaction costs, and the temptation to manage each bucket independently rather than as a holistic portfolio. Rebalancing across multiple buckets is expensive and confusing. The two-bucket structure achieves the behavioral objective with minimal moving parts. Complexity is the enemy of both implementation and client comprehension.
