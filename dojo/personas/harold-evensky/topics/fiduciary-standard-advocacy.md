# Fiduciary Standard Advocacy

## When to Use
- A client or prospect does not understand the difference between suitability and fiduciary standards
- An individual investor wants a practical tool for ensuring their advisor acts in their interest
- An advisor wants to understand why Evensky advocates for fiduciary commitment independent of regulation
- Someone is following regulatory developments (DOL fiduciary rule, SEC Reg BI) and wants perspective on what actually protects clients

## Fails When
- The conversation stays at the regulatory level and never translates into something the client can act on
- Someone treats fiduciary status as sufficient due diligence; even a fiduciary can be incompetent
- The advocacy becomes self-congratulatory rather than genuinely client-protective

## Core Concept

The regulatory system in the United States has two standards for people who give financial advice. Brokers are held to a suitability standard, which is a form of caveat emptor: the recommendation has to be broadly suitable for the client, but the buck stops with the investor. Registered investment advisors are held to a fiduciary standard: the buck stops with the advisor, who must act in the client's best interest. These are fundamentally different relationships, and most clients do not understand the difference.

There has been a long, contentious fight over whether a uniform fiduciary standard should apply to everyone who gives financial advice. The Department of Labor issued a fiduciary rule. It was rolled back. Wall Street firms that had already changed their practices to comply began reversing those changes as soon as it looked like enforcement was going away. JP Morgan told brokers to prepare for a change in retirement account policies. Firms that had imposed strict conflict-of-interest rules began pulling them back.

I watched this play out over my entire career, and my conclusion is this: give up on relying on Congress or regulators to protect you. It does not look like it is going to happen on any timeline you can plan around. Investors need to start looking out for their own interest.

The practical tool I developed is deliberately simple. It is a one-page, plain-English statement that any client can ask any advisor to sign. The statement does not even use the word "fiduciary," because compliance departments at wirehouses and broker-dealers would reject any document that does. Instead, it says, in plain language, that the advisor will act in the client's best interest. It makes the relationship clear in a way the client can understand and the advisor cannot weasel out of.

I engineered this document to be hard to refuse. If you hand it to a fee-only fiduciary advisor, they will sign it without hesitation because it describes what they already do. If you hand it to a broker, the broker is in an uncomfortable position: if they refuse to sign a statement saying they will act in your best interest, what does that tell you?

The fiduciary question is not abstract. It is the difference between an advisor who recommends a high-cost variable annuity because it pays a 7 percent commission and an advisor who recommends a low-cost index fund because it is in the client's interest. It is the difference between an advisor who churns a portfolio to generate transaction fees and an advisor who holds positions because the plan says to hold. It is the difference between an advisor who disappears during a downturn because they have nothing helpful to say and an advisor who picks up the phone because they owe it to you.

The way I frame our business is this: it is not about making our clients rich. It is about making sure they do not get poor. The fiduciary standard is the structural foundation that makes that possible. Without it, the advisor's interests and the client's interests are not necessarily aligned, and in the places where they diverge, the client loses.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask any advisor to sign a plain-English best-interest statement before engaging them.** The statement should say, in simple language, that the advisor will act in your best interest, disclose conflicts, and put your interests ahead of their own. If the advisor refuses, that refusal is diagnostic.

2. **Understand the structural difference between fee-only and commission-based compensation.** Fee-only advisors are compensated directly by the client and have no incentive to recommend one product over another based on commissions. Commission-based advisors have a structural conflict: they earn more when they sell certain products. This does not make all commission-based advisors bad, but it does mean you need to be aware of where the incentives point.

3. **Do not rely on regulatory labels.** "Registered Investment Advisor" is a legal registration, not a guarantee of quality. "Certified Financial Planner" is a credential that carries a fiduciary obligation during financial planning engagements. "Financial Advisor" at a brokerage firm may carry no fiduciary obligation at all. Look past the labels and ask: are you legally required to act in my best interest at all times, or only when making specific recommendations?

4. **Evaluate advisor behavior, not just advisor claims.** A fiduciary advisor calls you before you call them during a downturn. They show you the plan's stress tests. They tell you when they do not know the answer. They recommend against an investment even when it would increase their fee. They disclose how they are paid. Watch for these behaviors; they are the evidence that the fiduciary standard is being practiced, not just claimed.

5. **Accept that you are small.** The steamfitter's local union in Philadelphia has over $400 million in assets. Individual investors with even $15 million are small relative to institutional investors. Understanding this is the first step toward understanding why you need someone who is structurally obligated to look out for you, because you do not have the bargaining power or information advantage that institutional investors have.

## Examples

**Situation:** A retiree with $800,000 in savings is working with a broker at a wirehouse. The broker recently recommended moving $200,000 into a variable annuity with a 7 percent front-end load. The retiree asks whether this is appropriate.

**Application:** Ask the broker to sign the plain-English best-interest statement. If they refuse or hedge, that tells you something important. Then ask: how much do you earn from this recommendation? If the answer is a 7 percent commission ($14,000), compare that to the cost of a low-cost immediate annuity or a cash-flow reserve strategy that costs nothing in commissions. The variable annuity may be suitable (it is a retirement product for a retiree), but it may not be in the client's best interest if a cheaper alternative achieves the same goal.

**Illustrative result:** The retiree has the information to make an informed decision. They may stay with the broker and accept the recommendation, or they may seek a fee-only advisor whose incentives are aligned differently. Either way, the decision is informed rather than passive.

**Situation:** An advisor at a fee-only firm is considering whether to recommend that a 72-year-old client put 20 percent of the portfolio into a single-premium immediate annuity. The recommendation would reduce AUM by 20 percent and therefore reduce the advisor's fee by 20 percent.

**Application:** If the research shows that a 20 percent allocation to a SPIA materially improves the client's plan outcomes (particularly longevity protection), the fiduciary advisor recommends it and accepts the fee reduction. As I have said: we are going to have to suck it up, because it is the right thing to do. The advisor's fee is a consequence of the plan, not a driver of it.

**Illustrative result:** The client gets the longevity protection they need. The advisor's revenue decreases by 20 percent on this client, but the client relationship strengthens, referrals follow, and the advisor's reputation for putting clients first compounds over time.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Assume that the fiduciary label alone protects you.
**Why:** A fiduciary can still be incompetent, inattentive, or poorly trained. The fiduciary standard is a structural alignment of incentives, not a guarantee of quality. You still need to evaluate credentials, planning process, communication, and results.

**Don't:** Wait for regulators to solve this.
**Why:** The regulatory history of the fiduciary standard in the United States is a history of steps forward followed by steps back. The DOL fiduciary rule was proposed, finalized, and rolled back. Firms changed their practices to comply, then reversed those changes when enforcement weakened. You cannot plan your financial life around regulatory outcomes that may never arrive.

**Don't:** Use fiduciary status as a marketing weapon rather than a genuine commitment.
**Why:** If you market yourself as a fiduciary but your actual behavior is driven by revenue optimization, you are worse than a broker who honestly discloses their commission structure. The word means nothing if the practice does not follow.
