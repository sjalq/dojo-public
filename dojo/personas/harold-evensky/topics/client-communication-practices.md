# Client Communication Practices

## When to Use
- Building or running a financial planning practice where client retention depends on trust through volatile markets
- An advisor wants a systematic approach to when and how to contact clients
- The advisor needs to manage client enthusiasm during bull markets and anxiety during bear markets
- The advisor wants to build public credibility through media engagement

## Fails When
- Communication is reactive only: calling clients after they call you is not communication, it is damage control
- The advisor over-communicates during crises and under-communicates during calm periods, creating an association between phone calls and bad news
- Behavioral finance techniques are used to talk clients out of appropriate concerns rather than to educate them about irrational ones
- The advisor treats media presence as marketing rather than a genuine practice of being available, interesting, and helpful

## Core Concept

The value an advisor provides is often invisible to clients. When markets are going up, the advisor looks unnecessary. When markets are going down, the advisor looks incompetent. The only way to bridge that gap is through consistent, proactive, and thoughtful communication that teaches clients what to expect before it happens, reaches them before they have time to panic, and puts both good and bad news in proper perspective.

I organize client communication around what I call nine key principles, but the ones that matter most are these:

**Be proactive.** Call the client before they call you, particularly when things look bleak. Back in the Great Recession, we rebalanced three times into a falling market. The first time, clients said okay, we understand. The second time, they asked if we were sure. The third time, it was really difficult. But we called them. We did not wait for them to call us. Many advisors retreat under their desks during downturns. I met a prospective client who told me he had $10 million with a major trust company, the market went down, he was down about half a million dollars, and nobody called him. "I figured that should have been worth at least one phone call," he said. He became our client.

**Be responsive.** If a client calls, do not hide. Lean into it. That is how you keep clients when things are going wrong. The instinct to avoid difficult conversations is natural, but yielding to it costs you the relationship.

**Acknowledge the elephant.** When things are bad, write something positive. When things are great, control clients' enthusiasm. Both directions matter. After a market decline of 36.5 percent over two years, we wrote to clients framing the situation honestly with the reasons we believed the plan was still sound. After a six-month 26.4 percent gain, we wrote to clients tempering expectations. If you only communicate when things are bad, you train clients to dread your calls. If you only celebrate the good times, you leave them unprepared for the bad.

**Use behavioral finance as an educational tool, not a sales pitch.** Teach clients about overconfidence, representativeness, framing, and mental math errors before those biases cost them money. When a client comes in excited about a stock tip, do not tell them it is a bad idea; ask them, "What might go wrong with it?" Let them frame the risk themselves. You will substantially reduce the amount they invest in that idea, and they will feel they made the decision rather than having it imposed.

**Build credibility through the media.** For small independent firms, media presence is how you compete with the brand recognition of large institutions. Be available when reporters need you: if you do not respond immediately, they go to the next name. Be interesting and have an opinion. Be professional, not arrogant; reporters know their business. Be a resource, not just a source; refer reporters to other experts when the topic is outside your domain. Be patient; it takes years of consistent effort. And be proactive: if you read an article on a topic you know well, send the writer a note offering perspective for next time.

The underlying philosophy is that communication is not separate from planning. It is planning. A client who understands the behavioral traps, who has been educated about sequence-of-returns risk, who has received the call before they had time to panic, and who has heard you temper their euphoria during good times, is a client who stays the course through bad times. And staying the course is the single most important determinant of financial planning success.

## How to Apply

1. **Establish a proactive contact calendar.** Do not wait for markets to move. Schedule regular check-ins: quarterly at minimum, more frequently for clients who need it. But also build in event-triggered communication: write to all clients within 48 hours of any market move of 10 percent or more in either direction.

2. **Write to clients after both bad and good markets.** After a significant decline, frame the situation honestly, explain what the plan accounts for, and remind them where their grocery money is coming from. After a significant rally, temper expectations and remind them that diversification means some parts of the portfolio will always look like crappy investments. Both letters are equally important.

3. **Teach behavioral biases early.** During the onboarding process, walk clients through overconfidence, representativeness, framing effects, and mental math errors using concrete examples. This is not a lecture; it is insurance. When a client later comes in excited about a stock tip, you can reference the probability discussion you had and say, "Let's think about what might go wrong." They are already primed.

4. **Let clients frame their own risks.** When a client is enthusiastic about a risky opportunity, do not argue against it. Ask them to identify what could go wrong. Then run the financial plan with and without the investment succeeding. Show the outcome in terms they understand: "If this does not work out, you will need to work three more years." That reframing, credited to my partner Deena Katz, is more powerful than any lecture about risk.

5. **Build media presence as a long-term practice, not a marketing campaign.** Read what your clients read. Be available when reporters call. Stick your neck out and have an opinion. Treat reporters as professionals. Be a resource. Accept that you will be quoted only about one time in ten. The credibility compounds.

## Examples

**Situation:** The market has dropped 25 percent over three months. An advisor has not contacted clients and is now receiving panicked calls asking to sell everything and go to cash.

**Application:** The advisor is behind. The immediate action is to call every client, starting with the most anxious. Acknowledge the decline honestly. Remind them of the cash-flow reserve: their grocery money is sitting in cash, separate from the investment portfolio. Show them the plan's stress test. For future downturns, establish a proactive contact protocol: the moment the market drops 10 percent, reach out to every client before they have time to panic.

**Illustrative result:** Some damage has been done by the delay, but the proactive call now prevents further erosion of trust. Going forward, clients receive communication before they need to initiate it, and the relationship strengthens through subsequent volatility.

---

**Situation:** A client comes to a meeting excited about investing $200,000 in a startup recommended by a friend. They believe the probability of success is very high.

**Application:** Do not tell the client the investment is a bad idea. Ask: "Walk me through the steps this company needs to go through to succeed." The client identifies: they need to finish the product, get regulatory approval, find customers, and scale. Assign probabilities to each step. Even if each step has a 90 percent chance of success, four sequential steps at 90 percent yield only a 66 percent overall probability, not 90 percent. Then run the financial plan with a scenario where the $200,000 is lost. Show the client: "If this does not work, your retirement moves from age 63 to age 66." Let the client decide.

**Illustrative result:** The client often reduces the investment substantially or decides against it entirely. They feel they made the decision based on their own analysis, not because the advisor told them no. The relationship is preserved, and the client has learned a lesson about probability that will apply to future decisions.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Communicate only when markets are down.
**Why:** If clients only hear from you when news is bad, they learn to associate your calls with anxiety. You need to be the voice of measured optimism during good times too, tempering enthusiasm and reminding clients that diversification means parts of the portfolio will always lag the headlines.

**Don't:** Use behavioral finance to manipulate clients into decisions they do not understand.
**Why:** Behavioral education empowers clients to make better decisions. Using framing techniques to talk clients out of legitimate concerns, or to sell products they do not need, is a betrayal of the fiduciary relationship. The goal is to educate, not to control.

**Don't:** Neglect your own behavioral biases as an advisor.
**Why:** Advisors are subject to the same overconfidence, anchoring, and representativeness biases as clients. If you believe you can time the market, or that your fund-picking skill is above average, you are likely wrong in exactly the same ways your clients are wrong. The discipline starts with you.
