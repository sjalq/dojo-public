# Wealth Management vs. Money Management

## When to Use
- A client equates the value of their advisory relationship with portfolio performance relative to a benchmark
- An advisor needs to explain why their service is different from a mutual fund or robo-advisor
- A prospect is comparing fee-only planning with brokerage services and does not understand the structural difference
- Anyone is using rules of thumb (the 4% rule, age-based allocation, risk questionnaire scores) as a substitute for actual financial planning

## Fails When
- The advisor claims to be a wealth manager but only manages money; calling yourself holistic does not make you holistic
- The advisor uses this distinction to dodge the question of whether the portfolio is performing well; investment competence is a necessary component, just not the whole picture
- The client has no complexity in their financial life (no tax planning needs, no estate considerations, no multi-goal horizon) and genuinely just needs investment management

## Core Concept

One of the most confusing issues for the public, and for many professionals, is distinguishing between money management and wealth management. They are entirely different professions with different objectives, different skill sets, and different measures of success.

A money manager is responsible for selecting individual securities within a single asset class. They have a philosophy, a style, and a benchmark. Their goal is to maximize risk-adjusted return relative to that benchmark. They are, in a meaningful sense, psychology-neutral: they do not need to know whether the client is a doctor or a candlestick maker, married or single, risk-averse or risk-seeking. The money manager's job is to beat the index, full stop. It is a focused, institutional endeavor, and there are people who do it very well.

A wealth manager bears little resemblance to this. Wealth management is comprehensive, customized, and complex. The wealth manager must know the client's dreams, goals, and fears. The wealth manager incorporates not just financial assets but implied assets: earning potential, expected retirement benefits, the character of the income stream. A government employee and an investment banker with identical account balances need radically different portfolios because their human capital, their risk exposures, and their life balance sheets are entirely different.

Success for a money manager is measured relative to a benchmark. Success for a wealth manager is measured by whether the client achieves their life goals: retirement at a chosen standard of living, funding education for children, a specific bequest, travel in retirement, maintaining purchasing power against inflation. These goals need to be time-specific and dollar-specific, and they need to be prioritized. The wealth manager does not try to maximize returns. The wealth manager designs a client-specific plan and manages toward it.

This distinction is why I reject multiple-choice risk questionnaires, age-based glide-path investing, and target-date funds as adequate planning tools. A five-question scored quiz tells you nothing about the unique human sitting across from you. A target-date fund designed for someone retiring in 2030 knows nothing about whether that person has a pension, a mortgage, a spouse who earns income, or a chronic illness that will accelerate spending. It is, as I have said many times, a one-shoe-fits-nobody strategy. It is a useful concept for a sociologist studying populations but dangerous if applied to the unique needs of individual clients.

The wealth management approach requires a fundamentally different mind-set. Individual clients are unique. Their circumstances change. Their risk tolerance shifts after a 30 percent decline in ways that no questionnaire can predict. They have multiple time horizons: a short-term need for college tuition, a medium-term need for a vacation home, a long-term need for retirement income that must keep pace with inflation for decades. Each of these requires different planning, different asset allocation, and different management.

And taxes matter enormously. Taxes affect not only which assets are appropriate but which accounts they belong in. The wealth manager thinks about after-tax wealth accumulation, not pre-tax returns. The money manager does not, because the money manager's institutional clients typically do not pay taxes.

The bottom line is that the client defines the practice. Dealing with individuals requires a very different analysis than dealing with a pension fund investment committee. If you are advising individuals with significant investable assets whose primary goal is to earn reasonable returns for the risk they are prepared to take, you are a wealth manager, whether you call yourself that or not. And the tools, training, and mind-set you need are fundamentally different from those of the money manager.

## How to Apply

1. **Start with goals, not investments.** Before touching a portfolio, quantify the client's goals: time-specific, dollar-specific, prioritized. Use financial planning software that can model multiple goals simultaneously, not a single "when do you want to retire?" question.

2. **Assess risk tolerance as the threshold of pain.** From a planning standpoint, the only definition that matters is: at what point does this client call and say "I cannot stand it, take me to cash"? That is the binding constraint. All other definitions are academically interesting but practically useless.

3. **Build the life balance sheet.** Include human capital (earning potential), implied liabilities (desired retirement lifestyle, education funding), and financial assets. Two clients with identical portfolio balances but different careers, pensions, and spending patterns need very different plans.

4. **Design the portfolio to serve the plan, not the other way around.** The asset allocation follows from the financial plan. If a 30 percent equity allocation can achieve all the client's goals, do not push them to 60 percent for the sake of higher expected returns. The question is: "Do you want to eat less well or sleep less well?"

5. **Measure success against the client's goals, not a benchmark.** The S&P 500 is irrelevant to a retiree who needs $50,000 per year in after-tax income. The correct measure is: "Are you on track to fund your goals?" If yes, the portfolio is working. If the S&P returned 25 percent and the client's diversified portfolio returned 12 percent, the client's portfolio is doing exactly what it should.

## Examples

**Situation:** A client with $3 million in savings is upset because their diversified portfolio returned 8 percent last year while the S&P 500 returned 20 percent. They are considering moving everything into an S&P 500 index fund.

**Application:** Reframe the conversation from relative returns to goal achievement. Show the client that their financial plan is fully funded at current allocation. Show what would happen to the plan if they concentrated in the S&P 500 and it experienced a 2008-style 37 percent decline. Show them the range of outcomes for both portfolios over 10 years. The diversified portfolio achieves their goals in nearly all scenarios. The concentrated portfolio achieves higher peaks but also fails catastrophically in the lower scenarios.

**Illustrative result:** The client understands that the 12-point performance gap was the cost of diversification, which is the cost of sleeping well. They stay with the plan.

---

**Situation:** A prospect is comparing a fee-only wealth management firm charging 1 percent of AUM with a robo-advisor charging 0.25 percent.

**Application:** Articulate the difference. The robo-advisor manages money: it allocates to a model portfolio based on a questionnaire, rebalances algorithmically, and harvests tax losses. It does not know the client's mortgage payoff date, their spouse's pension, their desire to fund a grandchild's education, or their plan to relocate in five years. The wealth manager integrates all of those into a financial plan and manages the portfolio as one component of the plan. The 75 basis point difference is the cost of that planning, tax optimization, behavioral coaching, and the phone call that comes before the client panics.

**Illustrative result:** The prospect understands what they are buying with the higher fee and can make an informed decision based on whether they need planning or just portfolio management.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Evaluate a wealth manager's competence solely by comparing portfolio returns to a benchmark index.
**Why:** The wealth manager's portfolio is not designed to beat the S&P 500. It is designed to fund the client's goals with the minimum necessary risk. Comparing it to a single-asset-class index is comparing bananas to oranges, and it leads clients to chase returns at the expense of their actual financial objectives.

**Don't:** Use the term "wealth management" as a marketing rebrand for what is actually a brokerage sales operation.
**Why:** Many self-proclaimed "wealth managers" are neither trained nor equipped to do comprehensive planning. The title suggests a professional, but it may mask an untrained salesperson. Clients should look past the title and ask: does this person know my goals, my taxes, my estate plan, my insurance, my human capital? If not, they are a money manager in a nicer suit.

**Don't:** Substitute a multiple-choice risk questionnaire for real client discovery.
**Why:** A 25-question scored quiz is a shortcut, and shortcuts in planning lead to generic solutions. Risk tolerance is not a quiz score; it is the point at which the client cannot tolerate further losses. That point can only be discovered through conversation, education, and scenario analysis, not by checking box C on question 14.
