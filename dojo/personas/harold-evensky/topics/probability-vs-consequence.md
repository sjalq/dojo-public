# Probability vs. Consequence

## When to Use
- A client dismisses a risk because the probability is low, without considering what happens if the low-probability event occurs
- An advisor needs to explain why planning for longevity, market crashes, or disability matters even when they are statistically unlikely for any individual
- A client says they do not need a cash-flow reserve, annuity, or insurance because "the odds are it will be fine"
- An advisor wants a simple rhetorical framework for reframing risk conversations away from probability and toward severity

## Fails When
- Every risk gets treated as catastrophic, leading to paralysis or excessive insurance spending
- The advisor uses consequence framing to sell fear rather than to educate
- The probability of the bad outcome is so negligible (one in a million) and the consequence so modest that hedging it is genuinely irrational

## Core Concept

There is a philosophical construct called Pascal's Wager, and it goes something like this. Suppose you concluded there was only a 20 percent chance that God existed. You might decide: those odds are not great, so I will ignore ethics and do whatever I want. If you die and there is no God, you won the lottery. On the other hand, if you die and there is a God, fire and brimstone forever. The consequences, even though the probability was low, are catastrophic. Now flip it: you decide to live an ethical life anyway. If there is no God, you have lived a pretty good life. If there is a God, you won the lottery. The point is that when you focus only on the probability, you can make a decision that looks rational. When you factor in the consequences, the calculus changes entirely.

We tend, particularly in financial planning, to focus on probabilities but ignore the consequences. And that can be really dangerous.

If a financial plan shows a 95 percent probability of success, most people hear "95 percent" and think they are fine. What they do not hear is: there is a 5 percent chance you run out of money, and if you run out of money at age 82, you cannot go back to work and you cannot undo two decades of spending. The probability is 5 percent. The consequence is destitution. You have to plan for the consequence, not just the probability.

This is why I argue against the assumption that people spend less as they get older. There is a general belief, and some research supports it, that retirees gradually reduce spending. I do not think it is particularly true. My wife Deena and I have taken some very expensive cruises. I have seen plenty of people on cruise ships with wheelchairs and oxygen tanks. Just because people get older does not mean they stop spending. David Blanchett of Morningstar, whose research I consider consistently reliable, has shown what he calls the "consumption smile": spending may dip in the early retirement years but then accelerates as medical expenses rise. Planning that assumes a monotonic decline in spending is planning for the probability (most years, spending is modest) while ignoring the consequence (some years, medical costs are enormous).

The same logic applies to longevity. If a client says "I probably won't live past 85," the response is: you may be right, and if you are, you are dead, so who cares. The risk is living longer than you think you might and running out of money. The probability of living to 91 for at least one member of a couple both aged 65 is substantial. If you did not plan for that and you hit 91 with an empty portfolio, the consequence is irreversible.

Sequence-of-returns risk is another application. Two investors can earn the identical average return over 30 years. But if one of them has a terrible first year and is withdrawing from the portfolio, the consequences can be extreme: they run out of money in 22 years while the investor with the good first year ends up with over a million dollars. Same probability. Same average return. Completely different consequences, driven entirely by sequencing.

This is not an argument for being afraid of everything. It is an argument for disciplined, clear-eyed planning that accounts for what happens when the unlikely thing happens. In planning, you do not get a do-over. The consequence of being wrong on the downside is permanent in a way that the consequence of being wrong on the upside is not.

## How to Apply

1. **For every planning assumption, ask: what happens if we are wrong on the bad side?** Do not stop at the probability. If the plan assumes 7 percent returns and they come in at 4 percent, what happens? If the plan assumes the client lives to 85 and they live to 95, what happens? If the plan assumes 2.5 percent inflation and health care inflation runs at 6 percent, what happens?

2. **Stress-test the plan at two standard deviations, not one.** Show the client what the Great Recession did to portfolios. Show them the 2022 simultaneous stock and bond decline. Make the bad scenario concrete. Clients who have seen the stress test before the event happens are far more likely to stay the course when it happens.

3. **Reframe probability statements as consequence statements.** Instead of "there is a 95 percent chance your plan succeeds," say: "there is a 5 percent chance you run out of money at 82 with no way to recover. Let's make sure the plan accounts for that." The first framing breeds complacency. The second drives appropriate caution.

4. **Use the Pascal's Wager framing directly with clients.** Tell the story. Most clients understand it immediately. Then apply it: "The probability that you live past 90 may be only 30 percent. But the consequence of being alive at 92 with no money is severe. We plan for the consequence."

5. **Apply the framework to annuity and insurance decisions.** A single-premium immediate annuity may look like a bad deal on an expected-value basis for someone who dies at average life expectancy. But the consequence of outliving your portfolio is catastrophic, and the annuity eliminates that consequence. The decision is not "will I beat the annuity with my portfolio?" The decision is "can I afford the consequence of being wrong?"

## Examples

**Situation:** A 62-year-old client refuses to plan for living past 82, saying "nobody in my family lives past 80."

**Application:** Acknowledge the family history. Then reframe: "If you are right and you pass away at 80, your plan is overfunded and your heirs get more. That is a nice problem. If you are wrong and you hit 90, you are out of money with no way to recover. That is a catastrophe. We plan for the catastrophe, not the expectation." Show the plan funded to 92 versus funded to 82. The incremental cost of the safety margin is modest; the consequence of not having it is permanent.

**Illustrative result:** The client agrees to plan for longevity through 92. The additional savings required are manageable, and the client sleeps better knowing the plan accounts for the downside.

---

**Situation:** A client with a 90 percent probability of plan success does not want to reduce spending or increase equity allocation because "90 percent is good enough."

**Application:** Reframe: "90 percent means that in one out of ten simulations, you run out of money. If we ran your life ten times, one of those times you are broke at 78. Are you comfortable with those odds?" Then show the consequence: what does life look like if the plan fails at 78? No income beyond Social Security. No ability to return to work. The client's face changes when the consequence is concrete.

**Illustrative result:** The client agrees to a modest reduction in spending or a slight increase in the savings rate to bring the probability to 95 percent. The marginal sacrifice is small; the consequence avoided is enormous.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Use consequence framing to sell fear.
**Why:** The purpose is to help clients plan, not to terrify them into buying products. If consequence framing consistently leads to "you need to buy this annuity/insurance/product from me," the advisor has crossed from education into manipulation.

**Don't:** Ignore probability entirely.
**Why:** Probability still matters. A one-in-a-million risk with modest consequences does not warrant expensive hedging. The framework is for risks where the probability is material (say, 5 to 30 percent) and the consequence is severe or irreversible. Not every risk passes that test.

**Don't:** Let the client's optimism override the plan.
**Why:** Clients are human and subject to overconfidence. "I feel healthy" is not a longevity projection. "The market always comes back" is not a sequence-of-returns hedge. The advisor's job is to insist that the plan accounts for consequences, even when the client is confident they will not materialize.
