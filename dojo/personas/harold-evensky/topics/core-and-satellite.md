# Core-and-Satellite Portfolio Construction

## When to Use
- Building a multi-asset-class portfolio for a private wealth client who needs both cost efficiency and the potential for alpha
- The client is in a taxable account where turnover-driven capital gains are a significant drag
- The advisor wants to use index funds or ETFs as the base but also wants to allocate to active managers or factor tilts selectively
- The client needs a structure that is simple to explain, simple to monitor, and that manages the three-dimensional problem of expenses, taxes, and inflation

## Fails When
- The satellite allocation becomes the majority of the portfolio and the core shrinks to a rounding error; the whole point is that the core is the bulk
- An advisor picks satellite managers with 80 to 100 percent turnover and wonders why the tax bill is enormous; that turnover is acceptable only in the satellite, and the satellite is sized to absorb the tax impact
- The client has no financial plan underneath the portfolio; core-and-satellite is an implementation strategy, not a substitute for knowing the client's goals, risk tolerance, and time horizons
- Someone tries to apply this to a 401(k) with six fund choices; the concept needs enough breadth to actually separate a low-turnover core from a higher-conviction satellite

## Core Concept

In a low-return environment, where real returns after expenses, taxes, and inflation may be in the range of two to four percent, every basis point of cost matters disproportionately. Expenses do not shrink when returns shrink. One percent in expenses against a 14 percent gross return is a manageable bite. One percent against a 7 percent gross return is consuming a far larger share of what the investor actually takes home. And taxes follow the same arithmetic. This is what I call the three-dimensional world of investing: returns, expenses, and taxes all matter, and practitioners who focus only on the first are leaving the other two to eat the client's wealth quietly.

The core-and-satellite approach is a response to this reality. It is an institutional concept that I adapted for retail wealth management practice over two decades ago. The idea is straightforward. The bulk of the equity portfolio, the core, is invested in low-turnover, tax-efficient instruments: index funds, ETFs, and funds from firms like Dimensional Fund Advisors that tilt toward size and value factors while maintaining very low turnover. The core's job is to capture market returns at the lowest possible cost and with the least possible tax drag. Turnover in the core should be close to 10 percent or below. Research shows that cutting turnover from 100 percent to 50 percent produces negligible tax savings; you have to get turnover very low before the tax benefit becomes meaningful.

Around the perimeter of the core is the satellite. This is where you concentrate your risk and where you look for alpha. Satellite positions can be actively managed funds, concentrated stock positions, alternative strategies, or any higher-conviction, higher-turnover allocation where the advisor believes there is a genuine opportunity to add value. The satellite is sized so that the core is never put at risk. If the satellite underperforms, the client's financial plan is not derailed because the core is doing its job.

The core I often describe as "beta-plus": the market's systematic risk, enhanced by modest factor tilts toward small-cap and value stocks, which the research of Fama and French shows have historically delivered a premium. The satellite is where the advisor earns their keep by selecting managers or positions that genuinely add something the core cannot.

This is not a philosophical statement about active versus passive investing. It is a practical engineering decision about where to spend your cost budget. You have limited tolerance for expenses and taxes. Spend that budget where it has the highest probability of paying off, the satellite, and keep the rest as cheap and tax-efficient as possible, the core.

## How to Apply

1. **Determine the total equity allocation from the financial plan.** The core-and-satellite structure applies to the equity portion of the portfolio. The total equity allocation (say, 60 percent of the overall portfolio) is decided by the planning process, not by this framework.

2. **Allocate 60 to 80 percent of the equity portion to the core.** This is indexed or near-indexed. Domestic large-cap via an S&P 500 or total market index fund. If the portfolio is large enough, add small-cap and value tilts through low-turnover factor funds. International exposure through a broad international index. Turnover should be in the single digits to low teens.

3. **Allocate 20 to 40 percent of the equity portion to the satellite.** This is where you place active managers you have conviction in, concentrated positions, or tactical tilts based on forward-looking expectations. Turnover here will be higher, and that is acceptable because the satellite is sized to limit the total portfolio's tax exposure.

4. **Monitor the satellite for genuine alpha, not just activity.** If a satellite manager is not adding value after expenses and taxes over a reasonable evaluation period (three to five years minimum), replace them with more core. The default should be core. The satellite earns its place; it is not assumed.

5. **Rebalance the entire equity allocation as a unit.** Do not treat core and satellite as independent buckets. They are parts of a single equity allocation and should be rebalanced together toward the target allocation.

## Examples

**Situation:** A client has a $2 million portfolio, 60 percent in equities ($1.2 million), and is in a high tax bracket. The advisor currently uses five actively managed mutual funds with average turnover of 70 percent. The client is paying significant capital gains taxes annually.

**Application:** Restructure the equity allocation. Move $900,000 (75 percent of equity) into a core of three low-turnover funds: a total U.S. market index, a DFA small-cap value fund, and a broad international index. Place $300,000 (25 percent) in two satellite positions where the advisor has genuine conviction. Total portfolio turnover drops from 70 percent to roughly 20 percent. Annual tax drag decreases substantially.

**Illustrative result:** The client's after-tax, after-expense return improves by an estimated 50 to 100 basis points per year. Over a 20-year retirement, that compounds into a meaningful difference in terminal wealth. The satellite still has the chance to add alpha, but the core protects the client from the tax erosion that was quietly destroying returns.

**Situation:** A younger advisor is building client portfolios using 100 percent index funds and feels they are not adding enough value to justify their fee.

**Application:** Keep the core indexed. Add a modest satellite allocation (20 percent of equities) where the advisor can apply their research: perhaps a concentrated small-cap fund, a sector tilt based on forward-looking analysis, or a managed volatility strategy. The advisor can now articulate value-added in the satellite while the core keeps costs low and captures the market.

**Illustrative result:** The advisor's value proposition is clearer. The client gets broad market exposure cheaply and a targeted allocation where the advisor's judgment is concentrated. The advisor does not need to beat the market with the entire portfolio, only with the satellite.

## Anti-Patterns

**Don't:** Let the satellite grow to 50 percent or more of the equity allocation.
**Why:** The satellite is where you take concentrated risk. If it becomes half the portfolio, you have lost the cost and tax efficiency that the core provides. The core should always be the majority. If you cannot find enough conviction to justify even a 20 percent satellite, then 100 percent core is the right answer.

**Don't:** Use satellite managers with 80 to 100 percent turnover and ignore the tax impact.
**Why:** High turnover in the satellite is acceptable only because the satellite is a minority of the portfolio. If you oversize the satellite or ignore its tax consequences, you negate the entire purpose of the core. Always evaluate satellite managers on an after-tax basis.

**Don't:** Claim you invented core-and-satellite.
**Why:** Core-and-satellite is an institutional concept that predates its use in retail wealth management. I adapted it for practitioners and popularized it in the planning profession, but I did not invent the underlying idea. Precision about attribution matters; overclaiming undermines credibility.
