# Psychometric Risk Profiling

## When to Use
- Before any portfolio construction conversation with a new client
- When a client's stated risk appetite does not match their emotional reactions to volatility
- When explaining why an advisor's job is as much about managing behaviour as managing money
- When someone argues that AI or robo-advisors can replace human financial advice

## Fails When
- Treated as a formality rather than a genuine input into portfolio design
- The result is overridden because the client demands a more aggressive allocation than the test indicates
- Applied without ongoing recalibration as circumstances and markets change

## Core Concept

Before a single investment is discussed, every new client takes a psychometric risk-tolerance test. This is not a questionnaire about whether you like risk. It is a scientifically designed assessment, devised by an Australian professor, that measures how you will actually react when markets decline, not how you think you will react.

The distinction matters enormously. Many people walk in and declare themselves aggressive investors. They want maximum growth, they can handle volatility, they have a long time horizon. Then the market drops 20% and they call in a panic demanding everything be sold. The test catches this mismatch before it becomes a crisis.

The results often surprise people. A client who presents as confident, assertive, and willing to take risks turns out, when tested properly, to be a conservative investor. "Meneer, you're a very conservative investor. That's what you think you're aggressive, but you're not." This is not an insult. It is essential information. An aggressive portfolio in the hands of a conservative investor is a disaster waiting to happen, because at the first major drawdown, they will sell at the bottom and lock in permanent losses.

The advisor's job is to match investments to the client's genuine risk tolerance, not their self-image. This is the core of what an advisor does that a robo-advisor or AI cannot do. AI does not have emotion. AI does not understand family dynamics. AI can produce excellent cash flows and forecasts, but it cannot sit across the table from a person who just lost 30% of their portfolio value and say the right thing to keep them from making the worst decision of their financial life.

"An idiot and his money must not be locked up in the same room together, or the same country." The line is deliberately provocative, but it captures the principle: the biggest risk to any portfolio is the investor's own behaviour. Managing that behaviour, through proper risk profiling at the outset and ongoing relationship management through volatility, is the primary value an advisor adds.

The robo-advisor revolution that was predicted to replace human advisors has not materialised. Five years after "robo-advice is going to take your job" was the industry refrain, the uptake remains negligible. People want a human being who knows them, who knows their family, who can say "no" when they come in with a speculative idea, and who can say "stay the course" when markets are falling. That human relationship starts with the risk profile.

## How to Apply

1. **Administer the test before any investment conversation.** Not after, not alongside. Before. The risk profile determines the conversation.

2. **Believe the test over the client's self-assessment.** When the test says conservative and the client says aggressive, the test is right. Design the portfolio to the test result, not the client's ego.

3. **Use the result to set expectations.** A conservative client needs to understand that their portfolio will be less volatile but will also grow more slowly. An aggressive client needs to understand that they will see large drawdowns and must commit to not selling in a panic.

4. **Recalibrate over time.** Risk tolerance can shift with age, health, family circumstances, and market experience. Retest periodically.

5. **Combine with the full advisory relationship.** The risk profile is the starting point. Tax planning, estate planning, currency allocation, offshore structuring all build on this foundation. A product seller does not need a risk profile because they are selling the same product to everyone. An advisor needs it because every client is different.

## Examples

**Situation:** A successful executive describes himself as an aggressive investor and wants maximum offshore equity exposure.

**Application:** The psychometric test reveals he is actually a moderate-conservative investor. His past experience has been in a rising market and he has never experienced a sustained 30-40% drawdown.

**Illustrative result:** The portfolio is constructed with a meaningful equity component but also significant allocation to bonds and enhanced income funds. When global markets correct sharply, the client experiences discomfort but not panic, and stays invested. Had the portfolio been built to his self-assessed "aggressive" profile, he would likely have sold at the bottom.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Skip the risk profile because the client is wealthy or experienced. Wealth and investment experience do not correlate reliably with emotional tolerance for drawdowns.
**Why:** Some of the most panic-prone investors are wealthy executives who are accustomed to control. Losing control of their portfolio value triggers a stronger emotional response than they expect.

**Don't:** Treat risk profiling as a once-off exercise. People change, markets change, and life circumstances change.
**Why:** A risk profile from five years ago may no longer reflect the client's current reality. A serious health event, a divorce, a child's emigration can all shift risk tolerance significantly.
