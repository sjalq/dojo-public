# Offshore Trust Structuring

## When to Use
- When a client has externalized significant capital and needs a vehicle for estate planning, asset protection, and intergenerational wealth transfer
- When a client's children have emigrated or are likely to live abroad and the family needs a structure that avoids cross-border probate nightmares
- When the objective is to "cut the link" between South African regulatory control and offshore assets
- When a client asks how to structure their offshore wealth properly once it has been moved

## Fails When
- The client has not yet externalized any meaningful capital, making the conversation about structuring premature
- Applied without proper legal, tax, and compliance advice in both the South African and offshore jurisdictions
- Treated as a scheme to evade taxes rather than a legitimate, fully compliant estate planning structure
- The client does not understand the governance obligations of a trust and treats it as a personal bank account

## Core Concept

Getting money offshore is only half the job. The other half is structuring it properly so that it serves the client's family across generations, across jurisdictions, and across whatever political risks may emerge in South Africa in the decades ahead.

The default recommendation is a Mauritius-based trust. Brenthurst Wealth has its own Mauritian trust company precisely because this structure has become central to the advice given to high-net-worth clients. The trust achieves several objectives simultaneously.

First, it "cuts the link." Once capital sits in a properly established offshore trust, it is no longer within the direct reach of South African exchange controls, Reserve Bank oversight, or potential future capital controls. The money is held by the trust in a jurisdiction with clear property rights, predictable legal framework, and a track record of respecting investor interests. Mauritius has deliberately positioned itself as a financial services hub for exactly this purpose.

Second, it solves the estate planning problem. When a South African dies with offshore assets, the winding-up of the estate can become a multi-jurisdictional nightmare involving South African Master of the High Court processes, offshore probate requirements, executor fees, and delays that can tie up capital for months or years. A trust survives the death of the settlor. The beneficiaries (typically the children and grandchildren) can access the capital without going through South African estate processes at all. If the beneficiaries are already living in London, Sydney, or Toronto, the capital is where they are, in the currency they need, without any conversion, delay, or regulatory friction.

Third, it provides flexibility. The trust can hold a diversified global portfolio: equities, bonds, property, private investments. It can make distributions to beneficiaries in any jurisdiction. It can be structured with specific conditions and protections.

The process of externalizing capital itself uses the legal allowances: R2 million per taxpayer per year via the single discretionary allowance (minimal paperwork, same-day transfer), or up to R10 million per taxpayer via the foreign investment allowance (requires tax clearance, source-of-funds documentation, but straightforward for a properly prepared application). For a married couple, the combined annual capacity is significant, and spread over two or three years, it can accommodate the full discretionary wealth of most high-net-worth families.

Modern financial advice is not one-dimensional. It encompasses tax planning in both jurisdictions, currency allocation, estate planning, wills that work across borders, and the ongoing management of the trust and its investments. An advisor who cannot handle all of these dimensions is not equipped to serve a family with cross-border wealth.

## How to Apply

1. **Start with the allowances.** Use the R2 million single discretionary allowance first (fast, minimal paperwork). Then apply for the R10 million foreign investment allowance if more capital needs to move.

2. **Establish the trust in a suitable jurisdiction.** Mauritius is the standard recommendation because of its regulatory framework, double-taxation agreements, and financial services infrastructure. The trust should be properly constituted with independent trustees, clear beneficiary designations, and appropriate governance.

3. **Build the portfolio inside the trust.** Diversified global equities, bonds, gold, possibly property. The portfolio is managed by the advisor on behalf of the trust, with the trust as the legal owner.

4. **Align wills and estate plans.** The South African will deals with South African assets. A separate offshore will or trust deed deals with the offshore assets. The two must be drafted together to avoid conflict.

5. **Review annually.** Tax laws change, family circumstances change, and political risk evolves. The structure is not set-and-forget.

## Examples

**Situation:** A client with three adult children, two living in the UK and one in South Africa, wants to ensure that offshore capital reaches the UK-based children without going through South African estate processes.

**Application:** Capital is externalized over two financial years using the foreign investment allowance. A Mauritius trust is established with all three children as beneficiaries. The portfolio is invested in global equities and bonds. The trust deed specifies distribution conditions.

**Illustrative result:** On the client's death, the UK-based children receive their inheritance from the trust directly, in pounds or dollars, without any interaction with the South African Master of the High Court. No probate delays, no currency conversion at an unfavourable rate, no risk of future exchange control changes affecting the distribution.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Wait until a crisis to start this process. The paperwork, the tax clearance, the trust establishment, all take time. If capital controls are tightened or allowances are revoked (which can happen overnight via Government Gazette), the window closes.
**Why:** The history of exchange controls in South Africa from 1960 to 1997 shows that government can and does restrict capital movement when it chooses to. Acting in advance is not paranoia; it is prudent estate planning.

**Don't:** Treat an offshore trust as a way to hide money from SARS. The trust must be fully declared, the income must be reported, and all tax obligations must be met. This is a legitimate, transparent structure, not a secrecy vehicle.
**Why:** Non-compliance turns a legal structure into a criminal liability. The value of the trust is in its legitimacy, not in secrecy.
