# Law and Order First

## When to Use
- When evaluating any country, province, or city as an investment destination
- When a client is considering property investment in a South African metro
- When screening whether a seemingly cheap asset is cheap for a reason
- When someone presents an "urban renewal" or "recovery" narrative and you need to pressure-test it

## Fails When
- Treated as a permanent verdict rather than an ongoing screen (conditions can improve, and the framework demands re-evaluation when they do)
- Applied as political commentary rather than an investment criterion
- Used to dismiss an entire country without examining whether specific regions or sectors are better governed

## Core Concept

The late Dr Simon Marais, founder of Allan Gray, was once asked what he looks for first when evaluating a country for investment. His answer: law and order, and respect for property rights. Everything else, corporate profitability, growth rates, valuations, comes after.

This answer has been adopted and extended as a standing investment screen. It is not an original framework; credit belongs to Dr Marais. But the application of it as a recurring heuristic to South African cities, municipalities, and the country as a whole has become central to the investment approach.

The logic is simple. Without law and order, property has no reliable value. Without respect for property rights, investment has no durable basis. You can have the most mineral-rich land in the world, the most favourable demographics, the most promising GDP forecasts. If the government does not enforce basic law and order, and does not respect property rights, capital will not flow in and existing capital will flow out.

This is exactly what has happened across much of South Africa outside the Western Cape. Johannesburg was once a thriving cosmopolitan city. Hillbrow and Berea were pristine, desirable neighbourhoods. Through neglect, through lack of governance, through the systematic breakdown of municipal services, those suburbs were gutted in a 10 to 15 year period. Today, parts of the Johannesburg CBD are unsafe for a visit, let alone for investment. The migration from the north to the Western Cape is not a lifestyle choice. It is a verdict on governance.

When evaluating the investment case for any South African asset, property or otherwise, this screen comes first: is law and order being maintained? Are property rights respected? If the answer to either question is no, no amount of "green shoots" narrative or political promise changes the investment case until the facts on the ground change first.

This is also why the mining industry has fled South Africa. Global mining is a comparative business. Companies compare South Africa with Canada, Australia, Chile, and China. They run the numbers on mining rights, predictability of taxes, infrastructure, rule of law, and they walk away from South Africa because it does not stack up. Expenditure on mining exploration in South Africa has dwindled to 0.6% of global exploration. The treasures in the ground will stay there because the conditions for extracting them do not exist.

## How to Apply

1. **Ask the Marais question first.** Before evaluating returns, growth, or valuation, ask: is law and order maintained in this jurisdiction? Are property rights respected?

2. **Look at the physical evidence.** Are banks and retailers leaving? Are pawnshops and debt collectors moving in? Is municipal infrastructure collapsing? These are leading indicators that property values are heading to zero.

3. **Do not confuse hope with evidence.** Political promises of urban renewal are worthless until they show up in actual governance outcomes: functioning streetlights, working water, effective policing, enforced building codes. Until then, the screen says no.

4. **Apply it to regions, not just countries.** South Africa is not monolithic. The Western Cape is better governed than Gauteng. Hermanus and Stellenbosch are functioning differently from Johannesburg and Bloemfontein. The screen can pass for one region and fail for another.

5. **Reassess when conditions change.** This is not a permanent verdict. If law and order genuinely improve, the screen flips. But the improvement must be demonstrated in outcomes, not promised in speeches.

## Examples

**Situation:** A client asks whether Adrian Gore buying Discovery's headquarters in Sandton signals a Johannesburg recovery.

**Application:** The law and order screen. Yes, Sandton's commercial heart still functions. But the broader Johannesburg picture is one of collapsed municipal administration (the metro building was vacated overnight and never reoccupied), hijacked buildings in Bryanston, mafia-controlled taxi routes, and a police service that is itself a source of fear rather than safety.

**Illustrative result:** One corporate purchase does not reverse a decade of governance collapse. Law and order number one. Until that changes, the investment case for Johannesburg property remains deeply impaired. The advice remains: if you are in a town where it is falling apart, take whatever money you can get and move.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Present this as an original framework. It belongs to Dr Simon Marais of Allan Gray, who articulated it clearly and concisely. It is used here as an adopted and extended heuristic, always with attribution.
**Why:** Intellectual honesty matters. The power of the framework is in its simplicity and its source. Claiming it diminishes both.

**Don't:** Use law and order as a political club rather than an investment screen. The question is not "who is at fault?" but "are the conditions present for capital to be safe?"
**Why:** An investment advisor is not a political commentator. The screen is applied to countries, cities, and jurisdictions regardless of politics. Canada and Australia pass the screen not because of their politics but because of their governance outcomes.
