# Offshore Diversification Thesis

## When to Use
- When a South African investor asks where to put long-term discretionary capital
- When someone is weighing JSE performance against global alternatives
- When a client has the legal capacity to externalize capital (single discretionary allowance, foreign investment allowance) and needs a framework to decide whether to use it
- When someone is being told "local is lekker" and wants a data-driven counter-view

## Fails When
- Applied as dogma without looking at the client's actual situation, risk tolerance, and timeline
- Used to justify moving money offshore at the worst possible exchange rate in a panic
- Treated as a permanent-bear position that ignores tactical shifts (the thesis has a strategic layer that stays constant and a tactical layer that adapts to evidence)
- The client has near-term rand-denominated liabilities that would be poorly served by currency mismatch

## Core Concept

The central conviction is captured in a single sentence: wealth is priced in dollars, not in rands.

South Africa is a small economy, currently less than 0.5% of global GDP and about 0.37% of the emerging market universe. The rand has been in structural decline for decades. In 1980, you got $1.40 for one rand. Today a rand is worth roughly 5.8 US cents. That depreciation has not been a series of accidents. It reflects a shrinking share of global economic activity, persistent policy risk, commodity-cycle dependence, and governance failures that have compounded over administrations.

The practical consequence for a South African investor is that returns measured in rands are misleading. A fund reporting 10% annual returns in rand while the rand depreciates 6% per year is delivering 4% in real global purchasing power. Strip out that currency effect, and the JSE has been a poor place to grow wealth over the last 15 years. Convert a Johannesburg house price to dollars over two decades and the picture is often flat or negative. Convert a portfolio of global equities over the same period and you see genuine wealth creation.

This is not a prediction that South Africa will collapse. It is the observation that structural underperformance versus global markets, combined with a depreciating currency and elevated political risk, makes concentrated local investment a bad bet for anyone whose time horizon extends beyond a few years. The first rule of investing is diversification: do not have all your eggs in one basket. South African investors already have massive local exposure through their property, their businesses, their pension funds (capped by Regulation 28), and the fact that they earn and spend in rands. Discretionary capital is the one pool they can choose to protect.

Since 1 April 2015, when Treasury raised the offshore allowance to R10 million per taxpayer per year (plus R1 million, now R2 million, in the single discretionary allowance), South Africans have legally externalized an estimated R1.2 trillion or more. This is not capital flight driven by panic. It is a rational response to the data, and every large financial institution in the country now offers offshore products. The thesis that was heresy ten years ago is now mainstream.

The strategic thesis stays constant: maintain heavy offshore diversification, do not trust "local is lekker" narratives at face value, and think globally while living locally. The tactical read adjusts. When the Government of National Unity formed in mid-2024, there was a brief period where things appeared to be improving and selective local exposure made sense. When the improvement proved narrow (commodity-luck-driven, not fundamentals-driven), the tactical read reverted. The strategic thesis never changed.

The house view: offshore for equities, local for bond/income investments for local spending needs. Bonds and enhanced income funds in South Africa have delivered 15% year after year with low volatility, which is excellent for retirees needing predictable rand income. But long-term growth capital belongs where the growth is.

## How to Apply

1. **Convert everything to dollars before judging it.** House price, salary, fund returns. If it hasn't grown in dollar terms, it hasn't grown. This is the single most important mental shift.

2. **Use the legal allowances.** The R2 million single discretionary allowance per taxpayer requires minimal paperwork and can be moved in a day. The R10 million foreign investment allowance requires tax clearance and more documentation but is straightforward for a properly structured application. Use them.

3. **Build a diversified global portfolio.** The S&P 500 and NASDAQ have been the primary growth engines, but do not ignore Japan (the Orbis Japan Equity Fund has delivered 16% per annum in dollar terms over ten years), European equities (the so-called "GRANOLAS" companies), gold (which has been in a structural bull market since 2001), and frontier markets in Africa-ex-SA.

4. **Keep your local allocation purpose-built.** Local enhanced income funds, money market, and bond funds for near-term spending needs and retirees who need predictable rand income. Do not put growth capital into the JSE just because you live here.

5. **Do not wait for the perfect exchange rate.** Time in the market beats timing the market. If you wait for the rand to strengthen before moving money offshore, you are speculating on a currency with a 40-year track record of depreciation.

6. **Separate your lifestyle from your nest egg.** Think globally, live locally. You can enjoy South Africa's quality of life while your long-term wealth is protected in global markets. The offshore nest egg and the local lifestyle are two separate, deliberately decoupled things.

## Examples

**Situation:** A family held four residential blocks worth R60 million in a declining South African city in 2014/15, generating 3% returns on capital. Magnus advised them to sell and externalize.

**Application:** They sold, used the foreign investment allowances over two financial years to move the capital offshore, and invested in three diversified global portfolios.

**Illustrative result:** That R60 million grew to approximately $9 million (R145 million at current exchange rates), even after withdrawals. One family member retired in France. Another has nearly $5 million in an offshore trust for the next generation. The buyer of the original buildings later tried to sell them back at R30 million, half the original price. The difference in wealth outcomes is almost impossible to overstate.

**Situation:** A listed company CEO had R52 million in a preservation fund restricted by Regulation 28's offshore limits, earning sub-optimal returns.

**Application:** Magnus advised him to withdraw the capital, pay the R17 million in taxes, and move the remaining R33 million offshore in two tranches.

**Illustrative result:** That capital is now worth $5.2 million offshore. The client broke the shackles of Regulation 28, freed his capital from Reserve Bank controls, and created optionality for himself and his family. Ironically, with his money safely offshore, he no longer feels the need to emigrate.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Take advice from someone who has a vested interest in keeping your money in South Africa. Fund managers must construct funds within their mandate; they cannot recommend you move your assets elsewhere. An independent advisor is paid by the client, not by product providers.
**Why:** The "local is lekker" narrative has historically been pushed hardest by exactly the institutions that benefit from local asset flows. Their interests and yours are not aligned.

**Don't:** Treat the offshore thesis as an all-or-nothing permanent-bear position. It is a diversification framework, not a prediction of collapse.
**Why:** There are clients who should not go offshore because their needs are short-term, they cannot tolerate volatility, or their spending is entirely rand-denominated. The thesis is about long-term discretionary capital, not about moving every rand you have.

**Don't:** Wait for a crisis to act. By the time capital controls tighten or exchange rates blow out, the window has closed. The allowances are just that: allowances. They can be repealed overnight by a simple regulation published in the Government Gazette.
**Why:** The history of South Africa from 1960 to 1997 is a 37-year period when offshore investing was illegal and people went to jail for trying. Freedom to externalize capital is not guaranteed to last.
