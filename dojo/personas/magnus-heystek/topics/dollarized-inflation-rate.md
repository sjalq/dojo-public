# Dollarized Personal Inflation Rate

## When to Use
- When a client or investor is measuring their investment returns against South Africa's headline CPI of 5-6% and concluding they are doing well
- When someone asks why they feel poorer year after year despite official inflation being "under control"
- When evaluating whether local-only investment returns are genuinely growing wealth for a professional-class household

## Fails When
- The client's actual spending pattern is genuinely dominated by locally-priced goods and services with minimal dollar exposure
- Applied to a low-income household whose consumption basket closely tracks the official CPI weighting
- Used to manufacture panic rather than inform honest portfolio planning

## Core Concept

South Africa's official Consumer Price Index sits at around 5-6% per annum. Most financial planning, most fund manager performance reports, and most media commentary treats that number as the benchmark. Beat CPI and you are growing wealth. That assumption is dangerously wrong for the typical professional-class South African household.

The reason is that CPI measures a basket of goods weighted toward the average South African consumer. But the actual spending of a professional, an executive, a business owner, or a high-net-worth individual is heavily skewed toward goods and services that are priced in dollars or linked to global prices. Motor cars, fuel, cell phones, computers, overseas travel, children's university fees abroad, imported consumer goods, medical equipment, international subscriptions. These items do not track South African CPI. They track global prices, and you pay for them in rands that depreciate roughly 6% per year against the dollar.

The real personal inflation rate for this kind of household is estimated at 12-13%, not 5-6%. That is a staggering gap. If your investments are returning 7% in rands and your real cost of living is inflating at 12-13%, you are losing 5-6% of purchasing power every year. Over a decade, that compounds into a devastating erosion of lifestyle.

This is not a theoretical argument. It shows up in the lived experience of every South African professional who has watched cars become unaffordable, overseas holidays turn into luxuries, and university fees for children in London or Sydney consume the entire single discretionary allowance. It shows up when a flat in Mauritius that cost $400,000 (R5-6 million at the time) now costs $700,000 (R12 million or more) and has moved out of reach.

The practical implication is that any investment strategy built around beating CPI in rand terms is a strategy for gradual impoverishment. You need to be targeting dollar-denominated returns to maintain your actual standard of living. That is why the offshore diversification thesis is not just about chasing higher returns. It is about matching your investments to your real cost of living.

## How to Apply

1. **Audit your actual spending.** List your major annual expenses and mark which ones are priced in dollars or linked to global pricing. For most professional households, this will be 40-60% or more of total spending.

2. **Calculate your personal inflation rate.** Take the dollar-linked portion of your spending and apply dollar inflation plus rand depreciation. Take the local portion and apply CPI. Blend them. The result will be significantly higher than headline CPI.

3. **Reframe your investment benchmark.** Stop measuring returns against CPI. Measure them against your dollarized personal inflation rate. If you are not beating 12-13% per annum in rand terms, you are getting poorer.

4. **Allocate accordingly.** The portion of your portfolio that serves your dollar-linked spending needs should be invested in dollar-denominated assets. Local enhanced income funds can serve your local spending needs.

## Examples

**Situation:** A client's investment portfolio returns 8% per annum in rands. CPI is 5.5%. The client believes they are growing wealth at 2.5% real.

**Application:** An audit of spending reveals that motor vehicles, overseas travel, children's UK university fees, and imported technology account for 55% of annual expenditure. The dollarized personal inflation rate is approximately 12%.

**Illustrative result:** The client is actually losing 4% real purchasing power per year. Over a decade, that means roughly 34% erosion of lifestyle in real terms, despite nominally "beating inflation."

---

**Situation:** A couple is planning for their daughter's university education in London in three years. They have been saving in a rand money-market fund earning 8%.

**Application:** University fees, accommodation, travel, and living expenses in London are entirely pound/dollar-denominated. The rand has been depreciating at approximately 6% per year against the pound. The effective inflation rate on their educational spending is roughly 15% (UK education cost inflation plus rand depreciation).

**Illustrative result:** Their money-market fund is losing 7% of real purchasing power per year against their actual future obligation. By the time the daughter starts university, the rand savings will cover significantly less than they projected. Had they converted to offshore sterling-denominated investments three years earlier, the purchasing power would have been preserved.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use headline CPI as your benchmark if your lifestyle involves significant dollar-priced spending. It flatters your returns and hides the erosion.
**Why:** CPI measures the average South African consumer's basket. If your basket is skewed toward globally-priced goods, CPI understates your real cost of living by a wide margin.

**Don't:** Confuse this with a political argument. This is a mathematical observation about currency depreciation and consumption patterns.
**Why:** The dollarized inflation rate is a fact of arithmetic, not a commentary on whether South Africa should or should not be doing better. It applies regardless of which party is in power.

**Don't:** Assume that a rising nominal salary means you are keeping up. If your salary increases 7% per year in rands but your dollarized cost of living increases 12-13%, you are falling behind despite the raise.
**Why:** Nominal salary increases track local CPI and productivity. Dollar-denominated expenses track global inflation and rand depreciation. The gap compounds silently.
