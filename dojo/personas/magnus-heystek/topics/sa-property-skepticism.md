# South African Property Skepticism

## When to Use
- When a client believes their South African property is a core investment asset without having priced it in real or dollar terms
- When someone is considering buying property in a municipality with deteriorating governance
- When evaluating whether the middle class's largest "asset" is actually performing as an investment
- When a client asks whether to hold or sell an investment property outside the Western Cape

## Fails When
- Applied to Western Cape markets where governance has been maintained and property has genuinely appreciated
- The property serves an irreplaceable lifestyle function (primary residence in a desirable area) and the client is not treating it primarily as an investment
- Used to dismiss offshore property as an asset class (Mauritius, European property can be excellent investments)

## Core Concept

Residential property in South Africa, excluding the Western Cape, has been a 10-year bear market in real terms. In inflation-adjusted terms, the average return on South African residential property since 2007 is negative 30%. In dollar terms, it is negative 60% or worse. The typical middle-class family that considers their house their biggest asset is sitting on a wasting investment without knowing it.

The numbers are brutal and simple. A house purchased in a Johannesburg northern suburb 20 years ago, in inflation-adjusted terms, would be lucky to fetch the same price today. Convert that price to dollars and the loss is devastating. Meanwhile, a comparable property investment in Hermanus, Franschhoek, or Stellenbosch has doubled and doubled again. The difference is governance.

The myth that "property always goes up" persists because people look at nominal rand prices without adjusting for inflation or currency depreciation. A house that cost R2 million 15 years ago and is now "worth" R3 million appears to have appreciated 50%. Adjusted for inflation, it has lost value. Adjusted for the dollar, it has lost half its value or more.

For investment properties (not primary residences), the calculus is even more stark. A portfolio of residential blocks in a declining South African city might generate a nominal income, but the return on capital is often 3% or less. The capital itself is declining in real value. And the risk of further municipal collapse, of tenants who cannot be removed, of rates and taxes that are stolen rather than invested in services, makes the investment profile deeply unattractive.

The practical advice: consider selling any investment properties outside the Western Cape very carefully. Take whatever price you can get. If you wait, the price will likely be lower. Redeploy the capital offshore where it can earn genuine real returns. For primary residences, the decision involves lifestyle and emotional factors, but understand clearly that a house in a collapsing municipality is not an investment. It is a lifestyle expense that happens to have a declining residual value.

Consider future property purchases in the Western Cape only, where governance has maintained property markets. Alternatively, consider offshore property (Mauritius, Portugal, other jurisdictions) where the purchase also provides residency optionality for the family.

Never buy vacant land in South Africa as a speculative investment. Land that is not generating income is pure speculation in a jurisdiction with EWC risk. It is the highest-risk property play in the current environment.

## How to Apply

1. **Price your property in dollars.** Not in rands. A house that appears stable in rand terms has likely lost 50-60% of its dollar value over the last 15 years.

2. **Calculate the real return on capital.** For investment properties, divide the net annual income by the current market value. If the number is below 5%, the property is underperforming basic income funds. If the market value is declining in real terms, the total return is likely negative.

3. **Evaluate the municipality.** Is it well-governed? Are services functioning? Is the population growing or shrinking? Is capital flowing in or out? The municipality determines the property's medium-term fate.

4. **Sell investment properties in declining municipalities.** Do not hold hoping for a recovery. Take whatever price is available and redeploy. Time is working against you.

5. **If buying, buy in well-governed areas only.** Western Cape. Hermanus. Stellenbosch. Franschhoek. Offshore. These are the only markets where property has demonstrated real appreciation in recent years.

## Examples

**Situation:** A client has owned a home in Dainfern, Johannesburg, for 20 years. They believe it is a major asset.

**Application:** In inflation-adjusted terms, the property has not appreciated 0.01 cents in 20 years. In dollar terms, the loss is severe. Meanwhile, the client's offshore portfolio has doubled or trebled.

**Illustrative result:** The client understands that their "biggest asset" is actually their offshore portfolio, not their house. The house is a lifestyle expense, not an investment. They begin planning a move to the Western Cape, where property has appreciated and governance is functional.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Hold property in a declining municipality because selling feels like "giving it away." The alternative is holding an asset that continues to depreciate toward zero.
**Why:** Emotional attachment to a purchase price is sunk-cost fallacy applied to property. The question is not what you paid. It is what the property will be worth in five years given the trajectory of the municipality.

**Don't:** Buy vacant land in South Africa outside the Western Cape. Land that is not generating income is pure exposure to municipal governance risk and EWC risk with no offsetting return.
**Why:** Vacant land is the single most vulnerable property class to expropriation without compensation. It generates no income, cannot be defended through productive use, and its value depends entirely on governance conditions that are deteriorating.
