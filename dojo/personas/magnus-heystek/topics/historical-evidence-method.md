# Historical Evidence-Based Conviction

## When to Use
- When an investment argument is being made on the basis of narrative, forecast, or sentiment rather than data
- When comparing JSE performance to global markets over meaningful time horizons
- When someone cites one good year as evidence that local investing has turned a corner
- When a fund manager's bullish call needs to be tested against their track record

## Fails When
- Starting-point bias is ignored (the comparison period materially changes the conclusion and this is not acknowledged)
- Presented as a guarantee of future performance rather than a strong historical pattern
- Used to cherry-pick a flattering time period without showing what happens when the period shifts

## Core Concept

The investment approach is built on historical evidence, not narrative, not forecasting, and not theory. Every claim is backed by specific, decade-plus index comparisons: JSE ALSI versus NASDAQ, S&P 500, MSCI World, in both rand and dollar terms. The data is the argument.

The standard reference for very long-term comparative returns is the Dimson-Marsh-Staunton Credit Suisse/UBS Global Investment Returns Yearbook, which provides data going back to 1900. This dataset is widely respected and widely cited. Notably, it is also cited by Piet Viljoen, the friendly rival on the other side of the local-vs-offshore debate, to argue that the JSE has been one of the best-performing markets over the full 120+ year period. That is true. But the relevant period for anyone investing today is not 1900-2025. It is the last 15 years, where the JSE has structurally underperformed global markets in both rand and dollar terms.

The core data points are straightforward and consistently cited:

R1 million invested in the JSE 10 years ago would be worth approximately R2.2 million. The same R1 million in the MSCI World Index would be worth R4.4 million. In the S&P 500, R5.6 million. In the NASDAQ, R9 million. These are not projections. They are historical fact.

Over every time period from 6 months to 10 years (with occasional exceptions during strong commodity years), offshore has outperformed local. The JSE's best years have been driven by specific catalysts: the commodity boom of 2002-2008 (China's WTO accession), and the gold/commodity rally of 2024-2025. Strip out those commodity-driven periods and the structural underperformance is even more stark.

Starting-point bias must be acknowledged honestly. If you start the comparison in 2000, the JSE looks competitive because Wall Street was at a massive high (the dot-com peak) while emerging markets were at a cyclical low after the 1998 crisis. Some fund managers still use this chart, starting at 1 January 2000, to argue the case for the JSE. "That is mischievous." Starting points make a difference. The honest comparison uses multiple starting points and acknowledges where each side has an advantage.

The method also extends to testing fund manager predictions against reality. When Old Mutual predicted in March 2018 that South African equities would outperform global averages over the next five years, the prediction was recorded. Five years later, the Old Mutual Investors Fund returned approximately 4% per annum, compared to 28% for the NASDAQ, 21% for the S&P 500, and 18% for the MSCI World. That is not a marginal miss. It is a structural failure of the prediction, and the data is right there for anyone to check.

## How to Apply

1. **Always compare in both rand and dollar terms.** Rand returns flatter local performance because the rand depreciates. Dollar returns show actual wealth creation. Both perspectives are relevant, but dollar terms reveal the real picture.

2. **Use decade-plus comparisons, not single years.** Any market can have a good year. The JSE's strong 2024, driven by gold and Naspers/Prosus, was impressive but does not overturn 15 years of structural underperformance. Commodity rallies are cyclical, not structural.

3. **Acknowledge starting-point bias.** Be transparent about which periods favour which argument. The honest position is that the JSE outperformed from 2000-2010 (commodity boom) and has underperformed since. Both facts are true. Only presenting one is misleading.

4. **Test predictions against outcomes.** When a fund manager or commentator makes a bullish call, record it. Check it five years later. The track record of bullish local calls over the last 15 years is poor.

5. **Cite the data source.** The Dimson-Marsh-Staunton yearbook, Bloomberg data on capital flows, specific index returns with dates. Specificity builds credibility and prevents arguments from becoming opinion-versus-opinion.

## Examples

**Situation:** A client's existing advisor argues that the JSE has had a fantastic year and offshore is overrated.

**Application:** One good year does not change a structural trend. The JSE's 2024 rally was driven primarily by gold (the gold price doubled), Naspers/Prosus, and commodity shares. Strip those out and the broader market is flat. Compare 5-year, 10-year, and 15-year performance of the JSE ALSI against the S&P 500 and MSCI World in dollar terms.

**Illustrative result:** The data shows offshore outperformance of 2x to 4x over every meaningful long-term period. The single strong JSE year, while welcome, is commodity-driven (cyclical, not structural) and still did not match the NASDAQ or S&P 500 in the same period.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use a single time period that flatters your conclusion without acknowledging what other periods show. This is the exact move that "local is lekker" proponents make with the 2000-2010 data.
**Why:** Cherry-picking destroys credibility. Present the full picture, including the periods where local outperformed, and explain why the structural conditions have changed.

**Don't:** Present illustrative numbers as precise client results. The specific returns cited are index-level comparisons, not individual portfolio outcomes.
**Why:** Individual client returns depend on timing, fees, allocation, and currency conversion dates. The index comparison is the evidence for the structural thesis, not a promise of specific returns.
