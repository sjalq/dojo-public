# Risk Allocation Precedes Asset Allocation

## When to Use
- The investor or advisor is selecting securities, funds, or managers before defining what each tranche of capital is for
- Someone asks "should I be in stocks or bonds" before identifying their goals and the three risk dimensions
- Portfolio construction is driven by market outlook rather than by the investor's life situation and objectives

## Fails When
- The investor has genuinely simple needs that do not require three distinct risk dimensions (rare but possible for very small portfolios with a single short-term goal)
- Risk allocation has already been done and the question is legitimately about implementation within a bucket

## Core Concept

The standard industry practice is to begin with asset allocation: how much in equities, how much in fixed income, how much in alternatives. Risk tolerance is assessed through a questionnaire, a target return is set, and then an optimizer finds the portfolio on the efficient frontier that matches.

This gets the sequence backwards. Asset allocation is the answer to a question that has not yet been asked. The question that must come first is: what is this money for?

In order to achieve an appropriate allocation of wealth for the individual investor, risk allocation must precede asset allocation. Risk allocation means deciding how much of your total resources (not just market investments, but all assets and liabilities including home, mortgage, insurance, and human capital) belongs in each of the three risk dimensions: personal risk (safety), market risk (diversification and market-rate return), and aspirational risk (concentrated, leveraged bets with genuine human alpha).

Only after you have sized the three buckets, a decision driven entirely by your goals, your life stage, your distance from the danger zone, and your appetite for aspirational outcomes, do you move to asset allocation within each bucket. The asset choices inside the personal-risk bucket (cash, Treasuries, TIPS, fixed-rate mortgage, insurance) are fundamentally different from the asset choices inside the market bucket (diversified equities, bonds, alternatives), which are fundamentally different from the aspirational bucket (concentrated stock, business equity, non-recourse leveraged real estate).

Both the type of security and the purpose it serves in the portfolio determine placement. The same security can belong in different buckets for different individuals. A broadly diversified equity ETF is a market-risk asset. A concentrated position in your employer's stock is an aspirational-risk asset. A principal-protected note is a personal-risk asset. Placement depends on purpose, not on the label.

The practical consequence is that the traditional risk questionnaire, which typically produces a single number ("you are a 60/40 investor"), is insufficient. It captures only the market-risk dimension. It ignores the investor's need for safety (which may require a large personal-risk bucket regardless of their market-risk tolerance) and their capacity for aspirational risk (which depends on human capital, expertise, and business ownership, not on how much volatility they can stomach).

## How to Apply

1. **Start with goals, not markets.** Ask what the money is for. Classify goals as essential, important, or aspirational. Assign each goal to the bucket whose risk profile matches the required level of certainty.

2. **Size the three buckets.** The sizing is driven by the investor's circumstances, not by market conditions. A conservative investor near retirement with no business interests might allocate 60% personal / 30-40% market / 0-10% aspirational. An affluent entrepreneur with a growing business might allocate 20% personal / 40% market / 40% aspirational. The sizing is the risk allocation decision.

3. **Only then select assets within each bucket.** The personal-risk bucket gets cash-like instruments, insurance, and inflation protection. The market bucket gets a diversified portfolio, indexed or actively managed. The aspirational bucket gets the concentrated positions, the business equity, the areas of genuine expertise.

4. **Set benchmarks per bucket.** The personal bucket is measured against inflation. The market bucket against a diversified index. The aspirational bucket against absolute return or the wealth-mobility goal. Never apply a single benchmark across all three.

## Examples

**Situation:** A financial advisor asks a client to fill out a risk-tolerance questionnaire. The result says "moderate aggressive." The advisor builds a 70/30 equity/bond portfolio.

**Application:** The questionnaire answered the wrong question. The right sequence: First, what are the client's goals? They need $80K/year in retirement income (essential), want to fund a beach house (important), and are considering investing in their sister's startup (aspirational). Now size the buckets. The retirement income requires a large personal-risk allocation (cash, TIPS, perhaps an annuity). The market bucket handles the beach house and general wealth maintenance. The startup investment, if the client has genuine edge (they know the industry, they understand the business), goes in the aspirational bucket. Asset allocation within each bucket follows.

**Illustrative result:** The client ends up with a portfolio structured by purpose rather than by a single risk number. When markets crash, the retirement income is protected regardless. When markets boom, the market bucket participates. The startup bet is sized as a deliberate aspirational allocation, not as an afterthought or an emotional impulse.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Start with "what should I invest in?" Start with "what is this money for?"
**Why:** Asset selection without risk allocation is how people end up with 100% equities and no safety net, or 100% cash and no growth, or a scattered collection of investments with no coherent purpose.

**Don't:** Use a single risk-tolerance score to drive the entire portfolio.
**Why:** A single score collapses three distinct dimensions (safety need, market participation, aspirational capacity) into one number. An investor can be extremely risk-averse about their essential goals and simultaneously aggressive about their aspirational goals. The three-bucket framework accommodates this duality; a single score cannot.
