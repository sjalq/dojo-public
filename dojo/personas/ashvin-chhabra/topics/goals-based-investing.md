# Goals-Based Investing (Essential / Important / Aspirational Goal Tiers)

## When to Use
- The investor is measuring success by whether they beat the S&P 500 instead of whether they achieved their life objectives
- Multiple goals with different time horizons, risk tolerances, and non-negotiability levels need to be organized into a coherent strategy
- An advisor needs to explain why a client's portfolio can be "underperforming the benchmark" and still be exactly on track
- The investor's goals span from absolute safety (retirement income, education) to aspirational outcomes (starting a business, making an impact)

## Fails When
- The investor has only one goal and a fixed time horizon; full goal tiering may be unnecessary
- The investor refuses to articulate goals and insists on optimizing purely for return; the framework requires goal identification as input

## Core Concept

The best measure of investor success is to ask: what is the purpose of the money you are investing? If you achieved it, you were successful. If you did not, you were not. If you didn't achieve your goal, it doesn't matter whether you beat the benchmark.

Nobody is on their deathbed and says, "I had a successful life because I beat the S&P by 2% a year." Success in life, and therefore success in investing, is measured by whether you took care of your family, funded your children's education, maintained your standard of living, pursued the things that gave your life meaning, and had the freedom to act on your aspirations.

Goals-based investing begins by identifying and classifying the investor's goals into three tiers:

**Essential goals.** Non-negotiable. These must be achieved with near-certainty regardless of market conditions. Examples: retirement income to cover basic living expenses, children's education, mortgage payments, healthcare costs. These goals map to the personal-risk bucket and require assets with very low volatility: cash, short-duration Treasuries, TIPS, annuities, insurance. For those things you must do, the emphasis is on lowering the risk of not achieving these goals.

**Important goals.** Strongly desired but adjustable. These maintain the investor's standard of living and social standing: a comfortable home, travel, charitable giving at current levels, maintaining lifestyle parity with peers. These map primarily to the market-risk bucket. A broadly diversified portfolio that earns market returns over time will fund these goals in most scenarios, though the investor may need to adjust their expectations in prolonged downturns.

**Aspirational goals.** Things the investor would like to achieve that require above-market returns or concentrated bets: starting a business, making an impact through philanthropy, achieving a significant jump in wealth, pursuing a passion project. These goals map to the aspirational-risk bucket. The goals you aspire to may require a somewhat higher level of risk. Failure to achieve aspirational goals is disappointing but not catastrophic, because the essential and important goals are already protected.

The critical insight is that each goal tier has a different required probability of success and therefore connects to a different risk bucket. This is not a single risk-tolerance score. It is a multi-dimensional mapping of goals to risk dimensions.

While market investments are crucial, a comprehensive goals-based approach that takes into account all of your assets and liabilities, including your human capital, and connects them with their underlying goal or purpose will create a more complete investment process. Investing is not about the markets; investing is about you.

## How to Apply

1. **List all goals explicitly.** Write them down. Include both financial goals (retirement income, education, home) and non-financial goals (time freedom, business ownership, philanthropic impact). Do not skip this step; it is the foundation.

2. **Classify each goal as essential, important, or aspirational.** Be honest about which goals are truly non-negotiable (essential), which are strongly desired but adjustable (important), and which are aspirational (would be wonderful but survivable without).

3. **Assign each goal a cost and time horizon.** Essential goals: $X/year for Y years with near-certainty. Important goals: $X/year for Y years with high probability. Aspirational goals: $X needed with some probability of success.

4. **Map goals to risk buckets.** Essential goals connect to the personal-risk bucket. Important goals connect to the market-risk bucket. Aspirational goals connect to the aspirational-risk bucket. This mapping drives the risk allocation, which drives the asset allocation.

5. **Measure success by goal achievement, not by benchmark.** Did the essential goals get funded? Did the market bucket maintain the important goals? Did the aspirational bucket give the investor a genuine shot at their aspirational goals? If all three answers are yes, the portfolio was successful, regardless of what the S&P 500 did.

## Examples

**Situation:** A couple in their 50s has $3M in total assets. Their goals: fund retirement at $120K/year for 30 years (essential), maintain their current home and travel habits (important), and fund their daughter's nonprofit startup with $200K (aspirational).

**Application:** Essential: $120K/year for 30 years requires approximately $2.5M in present-value terms (depending on assumed returns and inflation). This is the dominant goal and maps to the personal-risk bucket (annuities, TIPS ladder, cash) plus the market bucket (diversified equities for growth over the 30-year horizon). Important: maintaining the home and travel requires the market bucket to grow at least at the rate of inflation. Aspirational: the $200K for the daughter's startup comes from the aspirational bucket and is sized as money they can afford to lose entirely. Goal classification drives the allocation: approximately 40% personal risk, 50% market, 10% aspirational.

**Illustrative result:** If the market drops 30%, the essential goal is protected by the personal-risk bucket. The important goals may need temporary adjustment (one fewer trip per year) but are not destroyed. The aspirational allocation was sized as expendable, so its loss does not cascade into the essential or important goals.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Define success as beating the benchmark.
**Why:** Benchmark-relative performance tells you nothing about whether the investor's life goals were achieved. A portfolio that returned 12% while the market returned 15% may be fully on track for all essential and important goals. A portfolio that beat the market by 3% but failed to fund retirement (because it was too volatile at the wrong time) is a failure by any measure that matters.

**Don't:** Treat all goals as equally important.
**Why:** Treating aspirational goals with the same urgency as essential goals leads to either excessive risk-taking (over-funding aspirational goals at the expense of safety) or excessive conservatism (under-funding aspirational goals because safety absorbs everything). The tiered classification is the mechanism that prevents both errors.
