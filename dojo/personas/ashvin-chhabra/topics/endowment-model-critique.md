# The Endowment Model Critique

## When to Use
- An institution, family office, or individual is considering adopting the endowment model because "Yale did well with it"
- Someone asks whether adding private equity, hedge funds, venture, and timber to a portfolio is the path to superior returns
- An advisor needs to evaluate whether the endowment model is appropriate for a specific institution given its size, governance, and non-financial resources
- Someone is puzzled by why the endowment model failed for many institutions in 2008

## Fails When
- The institution genuinely has Yale-like advantages: brand access to top managers, capacity constraints that favor it, a non-financial safety net (tuition, donations), and a multi-decade time horizon with no near-term spending crises
- The discussion is about a single alternative investment rather than the model as a whole

## Core Concept

The endowment model, pioneered by David Swensen at Yale, is widely admired for its long-term track record. It shifted university endowments from a traditional stock-and-bond portfolio toward heavy allocations to private equity, venture capital, hedge funds, real estate, timber, and other illiquid alternatives. The model produced outstanding returns for Yale over multiple decades.

But when analyzed through the lens of the Wealth Allocation Framework, the endowment model reveals itself as something quite specific: a well-constructed market portfolio. Not a diversified portfolio across multiple risk dimensions. A market portfolio.

When you look at Swensen's portfolio at Yale, there is not a lot of fixed income any longer because rates have been very low. There is a little bit of cash. So it is really a beta-one portfolio, maybe slightly more than beta-one. All the alternative investments, the private equity, the venture capital, the hedge funds, are just different risks within the market. For the LP (the limited partner, which is the endowment), a hedge fund is not an aspirational investment. It is definitely an aspirational investment for the GP (the general partner, who manages the fund and collects fees on the upside with limited downside). Similarly, private equity is aspirational for the people who own the firm.

The endowment model is no more than just taking cash, bonds, and equity and adding other toppings to the pizza. It is still competing with Vanguard. It may be a superior market portfolio, but it is a market portfolio.

The test of this analysis is what happens in a real crash. When the market crashes and correlations go to one, this portfolio goes down like the equity market. And that is exactly what happened in 2008. The endowment model was supposed to be diversified, but in the crisis, it fell just as much as the market, and worse, it faced capital calls from private equity at the worst possible time.

So the question becomes: is this appropriate for Yale? The answer is yes, because Yale has a non-financial safety bucket. Its safety net is students and tuition. If Yale needs money urgently, it can raise tuition by 25% and still fill every seat. Yale's aspirational bucket is its alumni network, its human capital, its sphere of influence. The endowment only needs to be the market bucket because the other two buckets are already covered by non-financial assets.

The problem arises when other institutions copy Yale. If you are some very small university in the middle of the country that is already having trouble getting students each year, the endowment model is dangerous, because you do not have Yale's implicit safety net. A 40% drawdown with illiquid alternatives and capital calls can be existential. Similarly, a family office copying the endowment model without a non-financial safety net (business income, other liquid assets) is running a leveraged market portfolio without a personal-risk bucket.

## How to Apply

1. **Before adopting the endowment model, ask: where is my safety bucket?** If your safety bucket is non-financial (tuition, business income, guaranteed cash flows from another source), the endowment model may be appropriate for your financial portfolio. If your safety bucket must come from the same financial portfolio, you need to size it explicitly before allocating the remainder to market-like alternatives.

2. **Recognize alternatives for what they are in your portfolio.** For the LP, private equity, hedge funds, and venture are variations of market risk with higher fees, illiquidity, and (sometimes) higher returns. They are not a distinct risk dimension. Do not count them as "diversification" unless their correlation to your market bucket is genuinely low in stress scenarios (it usually is not).

3. **Evaluate the fee and governance structure.** The fees in the endowment model are high and asymmetric: the GP captures a large share of the upside while the LP bears the downside. The governance required to select, monitor, and exit managers is significant. Institutions without the capacity for this governance may be better served by a simpler, lower-fee market portfolio.

4. **Stress-test with correlations at one.** In a genuine crisis, all risky assets tend to correlate. Run your portfolio with the assumption that everything in the market bucket (including alternatives) drops 40-50% simultaneously. If that scenario is survivable given your non-financial safety net and spending needs, the allocation is appropriate. If it is not, you have too little in the personal-risk bucket.

## Examples

**Situation:** A small liberal arts college with a $200M endowment and declining enrollment is considering shifting 60% of the endowment into private equity, venture, and hedge funds to "match Yale's returns."

**Application:** Under the Wealth Allocation Framework, this institution has no non-financial safety bucket. It cannot raise tuition without losing students. Its endowment must serve as both the safety bucket and the market bucket. Allocating 60% to illiquid alternatives eliminates the ability to access capital in a crisis and exposes the institution to capital calls at the worst time. The appropriate allocation is a significant personal-risk bucket (20-30% in cash and short-duration bonds to cover 3-5 years of operating expenses), a diversified market bucket (50-60%), and little or no aspirational allocation.

**Illustrative result:** The college preserves its ability to function through a multi-year market downturn without forced selling or program cuts. It accepts lower expected returns than Yale in exchange for the survivability that Yale gets for free from its tuition and alumni base.

**Situation:** A family office managing $500M from a technology exit is evaluating whether to adopt the endowment model.

**Application:** The family's "business" is no longer operating; the exit produced a large lump sum but no ongoing income stream. There is no non-financial safety bucket. The endowment model, which assumes a non-financial safety net exists elsewhere, is structurally inappropriate as a complete strategy. The family needs to carve out a personal-risk bucket first (sized to cover 5-10 years of living expenses plus insurance and estate planning), then allocate the market bucket to a diversified portfolio (which may include some alternatives for return enhancement), and finally assess whether any aspirational investments are warranted (perhaps a new venture where the family has genuine expertise from the technology business).

**Illustrative result:** The family ends up with a three-bucket allocation rather than a single endowment-model-style market portfolio, reflecting the reality that they have no non-financial safety net.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Copy Yale's allocation without Yale's structural advantages.
**Why:** Yale's model works because Yale has a non-financial safety bucket (tuition, alumni network) and an implicit aspirational bucket (human capital, brand). Copying the allocation without those structural advantages produces a leveraged market portfolio with no safety net.

**Don't:** Count alternative investments as diversification in a crisis.
**Why:** In 2008, all risky assets correlated. The "diversification" that alternatives provided in normal markets vanished when it was needed most. Alternatives may improve risk-adjusted returns in normal conditions, but they are not a substitute for a genuine personal-risk bucket that holds its value in extremis.
