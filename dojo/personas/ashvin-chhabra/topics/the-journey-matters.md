# The Journey Matters: Path-Dependence and Personal Risk

## When to Use
- An investor is focused exclusively on expected return and ignoring the volatility of the path
- Someone argues that a higher-return portfolio is always better because "it works out in the long run"
- The investor faces near-term cash flow needs, liabilities, or life events that make the path as important as the destination
- An advisor needs to explain why two portfolios with the same average return can have radically different outcomes for the same person

## Fails When
- The investor genuinely has no near-term needs and can ride out any drawdown; the path matters less when the time horizon is very long and cash flow is stable
- The discussion is about institutional portfolios with stable, long-duration liabilities and no near-term spending shocks

## Core Concept

The efficient frontier approach optimizes for a portfolio's outcome at the end of a single predetermined time period. There are at least two problems with this: investors need to maintain their lifestyle and meet their financial obligations during that time period regardless of market conditions, and we do not always know at the outset what the specified time period should be.

A stream may have an average depth of five feet, but a traveler wading through it will not make it to the other side if its midpoint is ten feet deep. Similarly, an overly volatile investing strategy may sink an investor before she gets to reap its anticipated rewards.

This is the core of personal risk, the dimension that Modern Portfolio Theory does not capture. Personal risk is path-dependent. It is not enough to know that a portfolio is expected to return 8% over 20 years. You need to know whether the portfolio can survive the sequence of returns along the way without forcing the investor into catastrophic decisions: selling the home, pulling children from college, depleting reserves in a downturn and never recovering.

The emphasis of the efficient frontier approach using market risks, without consideration for its effect on the portfolio holder, is fundamentally unsatisfactory. The journey matters because individual investors are not statistical aggregates. The same 40% market decline is a nuisance for a billionaire and a life-altering catastrophe for a family with 80% of their net worth in equities and a child starting college next year.

Personal risk has four components:

**Cash flow.** Is the investor a net saver or net spender? A net spender in a drawdown is forced to sell at the worst time, turning a paper loss into a permanent one. The cash-flow profile determines how much of the portfolio must be shielded from market volatility.

**Lifecycle stage.** Someone at the peak of earning capacity can absorb more market risk than someone approaching retirement. Human capital, the present value of future earnings, is a large but invisible asset early in life and a depleted one later. The portfolio must compensate.

**Ability to weather shortfalls.** How does one protect against outliving one's assets? Living 10 to 15 years longer than life expectancy is a real risk. The personal-risk bucket, including annuities and longevity insurance, addresses this directly.

**Event risk.** Job loss, major health problems, disability, the need to support an additional family member, lawsuits, macro events like market crashes or sovereign defaults. These are singular, unpredictable events that can destroy a portfolio if the personal-risk bucket is too small. The Wealth Allocation Framework addresses this by maintaining a safety allocation that provides certainty of protection regardless of market conditions.

## How to Apply

1. **Assess the investor's cash-flow profile.** Is the investor accumulating (working years, positive savings rate) or distributing (retirement, drawing down)? Distribution-phase investors are far more vulnerable to path-dependence.

2. **Map near-term liabilities and potential events.** College tuition in 3 years, mortgage payments, a parent who may need care, potential job loss. Each near-term liability that cannot be deferred is a claim on the personal-risk bucket.

3. **Size the personal-risk bucket to survive realistic adverse scenarios.** If a 40% market decline combined with a job loss would force the investor into distressed selling, the personal-risk bucket is too small. Size it so that essential goals can be met even in a severe downturn.

4. **Separate the path from the destination in performance evaluation.** Do not judge a safety allocation by its return. Its purpose is to make the journey survivable, not to maximize the endpoint. A zero-real-return safety allocation that prevents a catastrophic forced sale during a crash is producing enormous value, even though it drags the portfolio's average return down.

## Examples

**Situation:** Two investors each hold $1M in a diversified equity portfolio. Investor A is 35, has stable income, and is saving $50K/year. Investor B is 62, retired, and drawing $60K/year from the portfolio.

**Application:** Both portfolios are expected to return 8% over 20 years. But a 40% drawdown in year one is survivable for Investor A (who keeps contributing and buys at lower prices) and potentially catastrophic for Investor B (who must sell $60K/year from a $600K portfolio, locking in losses and accelerating depletion). The journey matters far more for Investor B. Her portfolio needs a large personal-risk bucket (cash, short-duration bonds, perhaps an annuity) to fund several years of spending without touching the market bucket during a downturn.

**Illustrative result:** Investor A can hold a small personal-risk bucket and weight toward market and aspirational risk. Investor B needs 40-60% in personal risk, with the market bucket sized to recover during her remaining horizon and no aspirational risk unless she is already wealthy enough that essential goals are fully funded.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Evaluate a retiree's portfolio solely by its average return.
**Why:** A high-average-return portfolio with high volatility can destroy a retiree through sequence-of-returns risk. The path to the average is what determines whether the retiree runs out of money. Average depth does not help the traveler who drowns at the midpoint.

**Don't:** Assume that time heals all wounds in investing.
**Why:** For accumulators with stable income, time does help because contributions buy more shares in downturns. For distributors and anyone with near-term liabilities, time without cash flow is the enemy. The personal-risk bucket exists precisely to buy the time that the market bucket needs to recover.
