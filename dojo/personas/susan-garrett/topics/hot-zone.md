# Hot Zone

## When to Use
- Dog needs a designated location they can go to, settle in, and hold until released
- Building duration and relaxation without adding the word "stay"
- Working multiple dogs in rotation — one works, one waits in hot zone
- Training in high-distraction environments and needing a stable home base for the dog

## Fails When
- Dog is consistently released from a distance, building a habit of watching and waiting on high alert rather than actually relaxing
- Dog leaves hot zone and handler cues "hop it up" to get them back, inadvertently reinforcing the act of leaving
- Duration is extended too aggressively without using the release itself as a reinforcer
- Physical boundary is too ambiguous — a flat towel gives the dog no clear information about where correct and incorrect are

## Core Concept

The hot zone starts with crate games and graduates to a raised dog bed or a bed with a bumper wall around it. The physical design is not arbitrary. When a dog is on a raised bed or one with a rounded boundary, there is a clear distinction between four paws in and any paw leaving. One paw on the floor is unambiguously wrong, and the dog has to make a deliberate effort to step up and over a physical boundary if they are going to leave. That response cost matters. It makes the correct behavior easy to identify and easy to hold. A flat towel gives you none of that. You can get it, but it is a much harder concept for the dog to capture and maintain.

The nuance that Susan observed in two students training on back-to-back days, doing the same thing, is one of those details that looks fine from the outside and is actually quietly undoing the behavior you are trying to build. They would put their dog on the hot zone, move around setting up training, and then call the dog out from across the room to start working. The dog would launch off and they would look like a great team. But what is being reinforced? The release is a massive reinforcement. The thing just before the release is what builds up value. If you consistently walk away from the hot zone, move around the room, and then call the dog across to work with you, the dog learns: every time they move around, something amazing is about to happen. I need to watch every move. The dog who was relaxing will stop relaxing. The dog who might have fallen asleep will stop falling asleep. They are on high alert every time you step away, asking themselves is this going to be the time?

The solution is simple: the thing before the thing. When Susan is going to release a dog from the hot zone to work, she walks back to the hot zone, touches the dog, often gives them a reward for staying there, picks up her toy, and releases them right there with her. They play together as they move out to the training area. The signal to "get ready, something exciting is about to happen" is now her footsteps coming toward the hot zone, not her footsteps moving away from it. Her dogs can lie down, relax, and even fall asleep on the hot zone because they know the game does not start from a distance. It starts when she comes to them.

Duration behaviors are not simply about extending time in one direction. There is a tendency to train them like climbing stairs: five seconds, then ten, then twenty, then thirty. The problem is this puts increasing pressure on the dog without using what is actually your most powerful reinforcer for duration: the release itself. Susan's approach is to release frequently and early, then invite the dog to come back. The dog discovers that releases are plentiful, that getting back on the hot zone leads to more reinforcement, and that the hot zone is a place where good things happen rather than a holding cell. The release builds the behavior rather than just ending it.

## How to Apply

1. Start with crate games. The hot zone is an evolution of crate games, not a replacement for them. The dog must already understand the release word clearly and have a positive association with staying in a controlled position before you move to an open hot zone. Crate games give you all of that, plus a physical boundary that makes the concept easy. When the dog is solid in the crate, move to a raised bed or a bed with a bumper that gives them similar boundary clarity.

2. Choose the right physical setup. A raised dog bed, or a dog bed with walls or a bumper around the edge, is Susan's strong preference. The physical boundary gives the dog clear information: four paws in is correct, any paw out is incorrect. There is no ambiguity. A flat towel or bath mat makes the dog responsible for staying on something that has no natural edges, which is harder for them to understand and easier for them to drift off of without realizing.

3. Build in the rule: go to the dog before releasing them to work. This is the single nuance that protects relaxation on the hot zone. If you are about to start a training session and your dog is on the hot zone, walk to them. Touch them. Maybe feed them there. Then release them with you standing right next to them. That footstep pattern — you coming close means the session is about to start — preserves the dog's ability to actually rest when you are not coming close.

4. Use the release as a reinforcer for duration, not just as an ending. Instead of asking the dog to hold the hot zone for ten seconds, then twenty, then thirty, ask for five seconds and give a release that feels like a party. Let the dog run off, let them settle, invite them back, mark and feed for getting back on. The value of the hot zone grows because it is associated with engagement and reinforcement. The release teaches the dog that holding longer is worth it because something great is coming. Do not make duration a grind.

5. If the dog leaves the hot zone uninvited, quietly take them by the collar and return them. No "hop it up." No frustration in your voice. No repeated cueing. The collar grab is a game they already know from recall work, so it is not punishing. Just a quiet, matter-of-fact return. If this is happening repeatedly within a session, ask yourself about the environment: is the distraction level too high? Is the distance you have moved too far? Is there something in the environment that has pushed past the dog's threshold for holding duration? Adjust the environment before you conclude the dog is being defiant.

## Examples

**Situation:** A handler wants their dog to wait on their mat while they answer the door. The dog holds for about ten seconds but then drifts off the mat as the door interaction continues.

**Application:** The hot zone duration is not yet solid at the level of distraction that a doorbell and visitor create. Susan would walk the hot zone back to near-zero distraction environments and use the release as the main reinforcer, giving many short releases and returns before gradually building the duration. Crate games are used to proof against the distraction of the door specifically, and the bumper bed replaces the flat mat so the physical boundary does the work.

**Illustrative result:** Over weeks of short successful holds rewarded with enthusiastic releases, the dog's history on the hot zone becomes very positive. The behavior builds because it is being reinforced, not because the dog is white-knuckling through an extended stay under pressure. The visitor context gets added after duration is solid in low-distraction settings.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Cue the dog back onto the hot zone every time they leave — saying "hop it up, hop it up" each time the dog walks off.

**Why:** Cues you give the dog are reinforcement. "Hop it up" is the cue that means go to that amazing place where good things happen. If the dog leaves the hot zone and you immediately say "hop it up," you have just reinforced leaving the hot zone. The more you say it, the more the dog learns: leave the hot zone, hear my favorite cue, get back on the hot zone. You have accidentally turned leaving into the behavior that earns the cue. Quietly and without drama, take the dog by the collar and return them. Then ask yourself what needs to change in the training environment, not in the dog.
