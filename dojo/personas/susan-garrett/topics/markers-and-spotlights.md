# Markers and Spotlights

## When to Use
- Dog is confused about where to go after a marker word, losing position to find the reward
- General marker words like "good" or "yes" have become dulled through casual use
- Handler wants to use toys, activities, and instinctive drives as reinforcement, not just food
- Handler wants to build different emotional states into their cues (calm vs. high-drive)

## Fails When
- Spotlights are used inconsistently or casually, destroying the conditioned emotional response — they become just another word the dog hears
- Handler uses a known cue the dog loves (like "swim" or "fetch") while the dog is in an undesired state, accidentally reinforcing that state
- Handler uses "okay" as a release word — that word is saturated in everyday conversation and will be ignored or miscategorized

## Core Concept

Most dog owners use general marker words: good, yes, excellent. The problem is those words show up constantly in everyday conversation, usually without anything meaningful following them. Over time, a word like "good" that started with some training value gets used so casually — "that's so good, yes, good boy" — that the dog stops brighting up when they hear it. The conditioned emotional response fades. What you have left is a word in the background noise of your household.

Susan trained under the Say Yes banner for years, and yes was her general marker. She has since rebranded what she considers the far more powerful alternative: spotlights. The formal term is location-specific reinforcement markers, but spotlights does what it says — it shines a light of clarity on exactly what the dog is earning and exactly where they will earn it. A spotlight accomplishes three things: it tells the dog what reinforcement is coming, it tells the dog where that reinforcement will be delivered, and it triggers a specific emotional response. That third element is the one that most people miss, and it is what separates a spotlight from a general marker.

Think about what this means practically. When you train a puppy with a calm, cooed "good" that only ever appears during relaxation exercises, followed by a soft, easy-to-swallow reward, the word good becomes a predictor of calm and ease. The dog's emotional response is quiet and still. But if you then use that same word in an agility ring to send a dog to a jump, you have just triggered the wrong emotional state. Equally, if you use your high-drive, exciting marker in a nail-trimming session, you have a puppy throwing their head up looking for a toy. Spotlights let you calibrate the emotional channel your training occupies, not just the behavior. As Brene Brown says, clear is kind. And anytime we can amplify that kind of clarity in our dog training, we know we are on the right track.

One more thing about spotlights that makes them categorically different from general markers: spotlights are both markers and cues that prompt behavior. A general marker is not. When you use a word the dog knows and loves, you are telling the dog two things simultaneously: I love what you are doing right now, and here is what comes next. That double nature means you have to be conscious of exactly what the dog is doing when you use a spotlight, because you are reinforcing whatever behavior is happening at that moment. Use "swim" when the dog is dragging on leash, and you have reinforced leash dragging. Use "jump" at the start line while the dog is creeping forward, and you have reinforced creeping.

## How to Apply

1. Audit your current marker words. Are "good" and "yes" appearing in everyday household conversation without reinforcement following? If so, they have been diluted. Decide which ones to retire from casual use. The ones you want to function as training markers should only appear in training contexts, always followed by exactly the reinforcement they predict.

2. Build one spotlight at a time. Start with the reinforcement you use most often. If you train primarily with food, build a spotlight that means "hold position, I am bringing food to you" and one that means "you can leave position and search for food on the ground." Introduce them in low-distraction environments, pair them consistently with the exact reinforcement they predict, and confirm the conditioned emotional response before moving to the next one.

3. Understand the food-related spotlights Susan uses. Her "good" (soft coo, only during relaxation) means stay calm, a small soft food reward is coming. "Cook" means hold your current position, food is being delivered to you. "Search" means you may leave position and look for food thrown on the ground. "Cow" means there is food in a bowl on the ground and you may run to it. "Treat" means come running to me and get food from my hand. Each of these tells the dog something different about what they are earning and where to go for it.

4. Extend to toys and activities. "Strike" means spin back toward me, grab the tug, game on. "Bring" means leave position, fly out and grab that toy, bring it back. "Chase" means charge after me, there is a toy coming. "Swim," if your dog loves swimming, can be a spotlight that marks a behavior and sends them to the water as reinforcement. Activities are legitimate, powerful reinforcers. Building spotlights for them makes them usable precisely in training.

5. Never use "okay" as a release word. It falls into every conversation. Your dog has heard "okay" hundreds of times with nothing following it. It becomes a word that gets ignored, or worse, one the dog is unsure of. Susan retired "okay" the day she was on a phone call laughing and saying "okay, okay" while her dogs were waiting on their mat — they released and ran upstairs covered in mud. Use a word you genuinely do not use in everyday speech. Her choice is "break."

## Examples

**Situation:** A handler uses "yes" as their marker but notices the dog leaves a sit-stay the instant they hear it, running toward the handler to collect the food.

**Application:** The word "yes" is acting as a general marker with no position information baked in. Susan would introduce "cook" as the spotlight for this context: condition it specifically to mean hold your position, food is coming to you. Once the dog understands "cook" predicts food delivered at their location, the sit holds. "Search" gets built separately for times when the handler wants the dog to actively hunt for food on the ground, so the dog now has two different predictions and two different behaviors called up by two different words.

**Illustrative result:** The dog holds the sit-stay every time they hear "cook" because the history of that word is clear: stay where you are, something wonderful arrives. The confusion about whether to run to the handler disappears because the information was already in the marker word.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use an exciting, high-value spotlight word to end or reward a calm or low-arousal behavior.

**Why:** Spotlights carry emotional charge. If "chase" triggers a predatory arousal response and you say it while conditioning a puppy to tolerate nail trims, you have just blown up the calmness you were building. Every spotlight creates a predictive emotional state, and those states have to be matched to the context in which they appear. The word "good" softly cooed during a massage conditions calm. "Strike" shouted in an agility ring triggers drive. They are not interchangeable. The power of spotlights comes precisely from this specificity, and diluting it by mixing contexts destroys the emotional precision you built.
