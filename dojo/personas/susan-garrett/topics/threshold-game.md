# The Threshold Game

## When to Use
- Dog loses its mind at any predictable entry point: front door, back gate, park entrance, training building, car exit
- Arousal is escalating well before the actual trigger — getting out of the car is crazy, the whole drive is crazy, getting the leash is crazy
- Handler wants a proactive game to teach the dog that thresholds mean "orient to me" rather than "charge forward"
- Dog is reactive near certain thresholds and handler wants a trained, happy alternative behavior pattern

## Fails When
- Handler tries to run the game while the dog is mid-cascade, barking and pulling — no learning can happen in that state
- Handler practices it once or twice and then goes to the real high-stakes threshold without enough repetitions at home
- Criteria are unclear or inconsistently applied — letting the dog through when it was almost calm undermines the game quickly
- Jumping to the test move (same-side direction change) too early, before the dog has deep value for the inward turn

## Core Concept

A threshold is any point that moves your dog from one emotional state into another. It could be the front door, the entrance to a park, the gate to your yard, the car door, a competition ring entrance, even the edge of the driveway. Thresholds are not neutral — they predict things. And because dogs are brilliant at reading what predicts what, a threshold that has reliably led to exciting experiences develops what I call a conditioned emotional response. The dog is not being naughty when they flip out at the park gate. They have learned, over many repetitions, that that gate is the thing before the amazing thing. Their brain is running the exact same transfer-of-value process that makes all good training tick — except right now, all that value is sitting on the other side of the door, and none of it is sitting with you.

Here is what I want you to understand about how it escalates. You might start out with a puppy who gets a little excited at the park gate. You find it kind of cute. Over time that gate excitement becomes absolute certainty that incredible things are about to happen. The dog is not just excited at the gate anymore — they are excited in the car, getting out of the car, walking up to the gate, getting the leash, hearing the word "park." The thing before the thing before the thing before the thing has all taken on the same emotional charge. That is the antecedent-behavior-consequence chain running on autopilot. The consequence — getting enveloped by everything exciting on the other side — reinforces every single piece of pulling, spinning, barking, and leaping that came before it. And often the handler is adding attention on top of all that: "Sparky! Sparky, stop it! Sparky, what are you doing?" That is not helping.

So the Threshold Game is my solution. Instead of telling the dog "no" to all of that excitement, we teach the dog a very specific thing: when a door handle gets touched, something amazing happens right here with me. We transfer the value from the other side of the door to the relationship on this side. The door handle touch becomes the new cue — but instead of cueing "charge forward," it cues "orient back to my handler."

Before you play the game anywhere important, you need three simple elements in place. First, the cue "cook," which means I am going to deliver a reinforcer directly to where you are standing — you do not need to move toward me. Second, the cue "search," which means I have just thrown something on the ground and you go find it. Third, genuine value for reinforcement zone — that is the spot at my hip, on either side, where my dog knows that good things happen. I always touch my hip before the food goes to the dog's mouth when they are in that position, so the connection is from my hip to the dog's mouth, not from my treat bag to the dog's mouth.

There are also two movement mechanics you need to practice without your dog first. The inward turn — where your dog is on your left and you turn into them, the dog follows the turn, and you end up going in the opposite direction with the dog now on your right. In agility we call this a front cross. This is your primary tool because the turn itself draws the dog's attention back to you; they are almost forced to reorient. The second move is the same-side turn, where your dog is on the left and you simply turn around and go back the way you came, with the dog on the outside of the turn traveling further. This is harder for the dog — they can easily drift off toward the distraction — so it is a test move, not a teaching move.

## How to Apply

1. **Build the game at home on a low-stakes threshold first.** Pick one interior door in your house. Walk up to it, touch the handle, do an inward turn toward your dog, say "search," and toss a cookie behind you on the ground. Do this two or three times. Then: touch the handle, say "cook," touch your hip, feed your dog in reinforcement zone. Repeat until touching that handle reliably gets your dog orienting to you.

2. **Add the door opening gradually.** Once the dog loves the handle touch, open the door a few inches while doing the inward turn and feeding in reinforcement zone. Grow this to opening the door fully, taking one step through, doing an inward turn, and walking back out while feeding. Mix between "search" (throw cookie behind you) and "cook" (feed at your hip) so the dog finds both rewarding.

3. **Vary the amount of penetration into the space.** Sometimes take one step in and reward. Sometimes take three steps in and reward. Sometimes walk in and out twice before rewarding. Sometimes give one cookie, sometimes two. The dog should start to find the threshold itself exciting because it reliably predicts the game, not because it predicts the other side.

4. **Grow environments methodically.** Once your dog is reliably orienting to you at interior thresholds, move to exterior thresholds — back door, front door, backyard gate. Then go to the neighbor's yard. Then a park when it is empty. Work up to thresholds near real distractions only after the game is deeply established in at least six different home environments.

5. **Use the same-side turn as a test.** When you think your dog truly values the threshold game, try the same-side turn — you walk in and turn around the same way without your body creating the reorientation. If your dog drifts toward the distraction, go back to the inward turn. If your dog comes right back to you, you have real value for this game.

## Examples

**Situation:** A Border Collie puppy comes to a training building. By eight months, she is barking, spinning, and pulling on leash as soon as she gets out of the car — before they even reach the door. Her handler is frustrated and embarrassed.

**Application:** The handler stops trying to manage the dog at the training building door and instead builds the Threshold Game at home: handle touch, inward turn, cookie on the ground or in reinforcement zone. After a few weeks of daily practice at multiple home thresholds, she takes the game to a quiet version of the training building entrance when no other dogs are around. The handle touch triggers the inward turn, the game fires, the dog orients back.

**Illustrative result:** Within a single ten-minute session at the training building, the puppy is orienting back to the handler every time the door handle is touched, even with another dog playing inside the building. The handler still uses the same-side turn occasionally as a check — and the puppy passes, choosing the handler over the distraction. The chaos that used to start in the car has dissipated because the threshold has been given new meaning.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Try to train or correct the dog mid-cascade at the real threshold.
**Why:** Once arousal has escalated to the point of barking, spinning, and pulling, the dog is in a state where learning cannot happen. This is not stubbornness. No animal can process new information at that level of arousal. Trying to teach the game in that moment is like a basketball coach yelling "drop your elbow" while the player is mid-game under full defensive pressure. Build the game in a green-zone environment first, get it rock-solid, then bring it to the challenging threshold. The game has to be conditioned before the pressure arrives.

**Don't:** Let the criteria slip because you feel bad for the dog or you are in a hurry.
**Why:** If the dog gets through the door while still spinning or if "almost calm" counts, you have just told the dog that "almost calm" is the new criteria. They will push to that line every time and you will find yourself in an ever-escalating game of what is the minimum I have to do to get what I want. The criteria you hold is the behavior you train. Be kind, be patient — but be clear.
