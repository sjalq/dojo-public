# Reinforcement

## When to Use
- When trying to understand why a reinforcement-based approach appears not to be working
- When a dog is reactive to motion, other dogs, strangers, or environmental triggers and the cookies seem to stop working
- When building a management structure that keeps reinforcement working for you rather than against you
- When training any behavior that needs to hold up under real-world distraction and arousal

## Fails When
- Reinforcement presented in the middle of a dog that's over threshold — you are masking emotional state, not training
- Counter-conditioning attempted without desensitization first — high-value food in the presence of a fear trigger can reverse into an aversive association
- Operant conditioning (ABC choices) demanded before the dog feels safe — dog cannot make thoughtful choices when they're not in a thinking brain
- Training lumped: jumping to complex environments without the layers of foundational games that build genuine confidence and relationship

## Core Concept

I've heard it said more times than I can count: reinforcement-based dog training doesn't work. To quote my mentor Bob Bailey, "Don't blame the methodology for one person's application of it." Reinforcement-based training is in its infancy in many ways — and yet I myself have been doing nothing but this kind of training since the early to mid-90s, across many different breeds of dogs, in many different sports and life situations. The science goes back to B.F. Skinner and operant conditioning: reinforcement builds behavior. That principle is as reliable as gravity. What changes is how skillfully it gets applied.

Here is the core truth that changes everything: reinforcement is always happening. It does not switch on when you pull out a cookie and switch off when you put it away. Every interaction your dog has with the environment, with you, and with the world is producing consequences, and those consequences are building the dog in front of you right now. That means reinforcement is either working for you or against you, depending on whether you're being intentional about it. I tell the story of a dog trainer colleague who couldn't figure out why her nine-month-old Border Collie woke her up every single night between two and three in the morning. The dog would whine, the trainer would take her outside, the dog would pee, and then the dog would be so full of energy that the trainer would throw a Frisbee with her for a few minutes until she tired out. Every single night. The trainer had created that entire behavior chain without realizing it, because the dog had learned exactly what action got her that incredible payoff of off-leash Frisbee at two in the morning. Dogs are far more brilliant than we are at picking up patterns of reinforcement. Don't feel bad if you've created patterns you didn't intend — just become aware of them.

Management is the biggest part of getting success with reinforcement-based dog training. If you don't manage, then you'll end up reacting to unwanted behavior instead of preventing it. What I call a "gated community" — a crate with an x-pen, or a managed room — creates an environment where the dog cannot rehearse wrong choices. You're not saying "ah-ah" or using magical tools that stop unwanted behavior. You're setting up the environment so the behaviors that get reinforced are the ones you actually want. From that foundation, you build confidence in small, deliberate increments: your training den first, then another room, then the front porch where a car goes by. Not from the training den straight to the dog park. Micro challenges, not jumps.

Now, when reinforcement-based training appears to not be working — especially around triggers — there's a three-step process that explains why and what to do. The first step is desensitization. My Border Collie Encore was very triggered by motion at six months old — dogs running, bicycles, anything moving activated her. Before I could do any training with her around those things, I had to find the distance at which she could see the trigger but remain in an emotional state where she could actually think. Sometimes that meant sitting on a hill a football field away from an agility trial, letting her watch at a distance where she was interested but not frantic. That's desensitization: getting the dog to a place where the trigger exists in their awareness without hijacking their nervous system.

The second step is counter-conditioning: creating a new relationship between the trigger and something the dog loves. Crucially, counter-conditioning is not dog training. It is building an emotional association. It comes after desensitization has put the dog in a calm enough state that the good thing (food, tug, car ride) can actually register positively. Many people attempt counter-conditioning in the middle of full emotional arousal — holding liver in front of a dog that's over threshold, or giving rapid-fire cookies to a dog that's already reacting. That isn't counter-conditioning. That's masking: temporarily overriding the emotional state with something overwhelming. And there's a real risk: if you present high-value food consistently at the wrong moment, you can reverse-condition it, turning that food into a signal for stress.

The third step is operant conditioning: the ABCs. Antecedent — the cue or trigger. Behavior — the dog's choice. Consequence — what happens as a result. This is where the dog gets to make decisions and earn reinforcement for good ones. But this step requires that the dog feel safe and feel confident. You cannot layer operant choices onto a dog that is still in a fear or arousal state. The behavior in the B of the ABCs is the dog's choice — we don't force it. Once Encore was desensitized and counter-conditioned, I could present the trigger and wait for her to check back in with me. She would look at the dogs at ringside, then look up at me. I'd mark that, give her a little tug game, move to a slightly safer distance, and repeat. That is reinforcement-based training actually working.

One thing I see often that looks like reinforcement training but isn't: pre-run cookie stuffing at agility trials — giving rapid, continuous cookies before a dog goes into the ring, presumably to keep them focused or motivated. But what is each cookie for? Is it desensitizing anything? Counter-conditioning anything? The consequence for a specific choice? When cookies come so fast the dog has no chance to make a choice, you aren't training. You're snacking. The dogs that leave the ring to sniff or visit the judge haven't been trained with a solid reinforcement history for staying engaged with their handler — the foundational games weren't there. The lumping happened.

## How to Apply

1. **Become aware of every reinforcement the dog is getting — not just the ones you intend.** Ask yourself: what was that cookie for? What was that attention for? What did the dog just get from the environment? Awareness is the first step in making reinforcement work for you.

2. **Use management to prevent rehearsal of unwanted behaviors.** Gated community, supervision, thoughtful use of confinement — these are not restrictions, they're the foundation of success. A dog who can't practice wrong choices is a dog who can learn right ones.

3. **For triggered/reactive dogs, work the three-step process in order.** Desensitize first (find the threshold distance, let the dog observe calmly). Counter-condition second (pair the trigger with something the dog loves, at that safe distance, with good timing). Operant training third (give the dog choices and reinforce the right ones with games, tug, or high-value food). Do not skip to step three.

4. **Build confidence in micro challenges.** Go from the training den to one other room to the front porch to the end of the driveway. Each step is a tiny reach past the comfort zone, followed by success and reinforcement. Bridging those gaps is what makes behavior reliable under real distraction.

5. **Identify permissions as reinforcement.** Access to running with another dog, going for a swim, chasing a ball — these are powerful reinforcements that cost you nothing. Using them intentionally and contingently grows the behaviors you want without ever pulling out a cookie.

## Examples

**Situation:** A dog is reactive to other dogs on walks. The owner has been giving cookies but nothing is improving.

**Application:** You stop counter-conditioning mid-reaction. You go back to the distance at which the dog can see another dog but remain in a thinking state — probably much further than the owner has been trying. At that distance, you sit with the dog, let them observe, and give cookies only when the dog is calm and offering attention. Over multiple sessions, you very gradually decrease the distance, always watching the dog's emotional state. When the dog looks at another dog and then looks back at you, that check-in gets marked and rewarded.

**Illustrative result:** Over several weeks, the dog begins to understand that other dogs at a distance predict good things, and checking back in with the handler is the most rewarding option. The reactive threshold shifts because the emotional state has shifted first.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use food to "manage through" a trigger by holding it near the dog's face or feeding constantly during exposure.
**Why:** This is masking, not training. You are using an overwhelming stimulus to temporarily override the dog's emotional response to the trigger. The dog isn't learning anything about the trigger. In fact, if you repeat this enough, the food itself can become associated with the stress of the trigger rather than with calm. Real counter-conditioning happens at a distance where the dog is already under threshold, with good timing, so that the trigger predicts the good thing — not the other way around.
