# Crate Games

## When to Use
- With any new puppy, adult dog, or rescue from day one
- When a dog needs to be comfortable in a crate for veterinary procedures, boarding, travel, or emergency evacuation
- When the owner needs a management tool that keeps the dog safe and prevents the destructive behaviors that damage the human-dog relationship
- When building a dog that can be alone, calm, and confident in confinement — not because they've been forced, but because they genuinely love their crate

## Fails When
- Door shut before the dog has sufficient value in the crate — the dog has no reason to stay and every reason to try to get out
- Dog with separation anxiety is locked in too soon — this creates a panic loop that makes the anxiety worse, not better
- Crate used as a punishment or time-out space — the crate needs to be the dog's most positive place, not associated with something the owner is upset about
- Dog placed in an isolated location like a garage or basement — dogs bond to humans and need to be where the family is

## Core Concept

When most people hear "crate," they hear "cage." But if you truly love your dog, I want to flip that around. A dog that loves their crate is a dog that is safe during car travel, calm during veterinary overnight stays, settled in a hotel room, and manageable when you have to evacuate your home in an emergency. These things happen. The question isn't whether your dog will ever need to be confined — the question is whether that confinement is a source of panic and stress, or a familiar place they've been trained to love.

Here's what gets me: I have heard the story too many times. Someone takes their dog in for a dental cleaning or a spay, and the dog comes home and drinks water like it hasn't had any in a week. The owner thinks the vet didn't offer water. Almost always, the truth is that dog spent the entire time in a crate it has never learned to accept, stress-panting and barking and running through its whole emotional emergency kit. That is unnecessary suffering. A dog that has been through Crate Games properly would go: "Yeah, I know how to be in a crate. I know my owner comes back. I'm good." Every dog I have ever owned has loved their crate, and every dog I own today still chooses to be in one. That's not a coincidence.

The goal is not to teach a dog to tolerate confinement. The goal is to build such a strong positive association with the crate that the dog actively wants to be in it. The way you get there is through games, not through throwing cookies in and closing the door. Throwing a cookie in is not the same as building value. The dog takes the cookie, comes back out, and the crate is neutral again. Games create a relationship with the crate. The dog starts to think: "When I'm in that crate, good things happen." Over time, when the crate door is open and no one is asking anything, a well-trained dog will often choose to walk in on their own. That's what you're building toward.

The "gated community" concept goes alongside this: for young puppies or rescue dogs learning the household, I create a crate with an x-pen around it. When I can supervise, the dog has more room to be near me. When I can't supervise, they're safely in the crate. This setup lives where I live — my office, my kitchen, my bedroom. Not the garage. Not the basement. Dogs are social animals. They bond to humans. You want the crate to be part of family life, not a banishment.

A word about separation anxiety: if your dog has genuine separation anxiety, Crate Games is absolutely something you should do — but do not close and lock that door until you've done a lot of work building up to it. Start with the games open-ended, build value, and only begin adding duration in tiny, calm increments. A dog with separation anxiety in a locked crate gets more anxious, not less.

## How to Apply

1. **The In/Out Game.** Get the dog in the crate and toss a treat to send them back out. Then send them back in with a treat. You're reinforcing both going in and coming out, at your request — not theirs. Keep it super casual. Don't point, don't coerce, don't make it a big deal. You want the dog to think this is completely unremarkable. The door is not closed yet. This is just the beginning of building a positive history with going into the crate on cue.

2. **The Door Game (building IYC at the threshold).** Send the dog into the crate. Begin to close the door slowly. If the dog tries to push forward and come out before you've released them, close the door. The door closing is a natural consequence of trying to come out uninvited. When the dog is still inside and the door is shut, give a calm release word — "okay" — toss the treat inside, and immediately reopen the door. You are not luring them in and you are not trapping them. You are building the understanding that the release word means it's your turn to come out, and nothing else does.

3. **The Place Game in the Crate.** If the dog has a place or mat cue, use it with the crate door open. Cue them to go in, take a step or two away, then return and reward them while they're still inside. Gradually extend the distance you move away before you return. Then try with the door shut. You're building duration and spatial distance from you while the dog stays calm in the crate.

4. **Meals in a Food Toy.** Put the dog's meal inside a rag with treats tied in, or in a food toy. Set the dog up in the crate with it and settle yourself just outside — reading a book, ignoring the dog. Let them work at it. When they're finished, open the door but do not release until you give the release word. Even after a long, pleasant meal, they don't come out on their own terms. This builds duration in the crate without any drama, because the dog had a wonderful time and is naturally settling.

5. **Random Surprise Cookies.** Throughout the day, when the dog isn't looking, leave a treat in the open crate. The dog wanders in on their own, finds something great, and wanders out. You're not there for this one — it just happens. The crate becomes a place where good things show up randomly. Over time, the dog will choose to investigate the crate often on their own. That is exactly what you want.

## Examples

**Situation:** A new puppy is resisting going into the crate at night and cries when the door is closed.

**Application:** You haven't started closing the door yet. You spend three days doing only the In/Out game and the Meal game, keeping the door open the entire time. You are in the room. The puppy is building a strong positive history with going into the crate and settling there. Only after the puppy is voluntarily spending time near the crate and going in willingly do you begin the Door game — and even then, you start with two seconds of door-closed before you open it again.

**Illustrative result:** By the end of the week, the puppy is walking into the crate on their own when they're tired, because the crate has become associated with good things and calm. Overnight, they settle because the crate is familiar rather than frightening.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Rush to close and lock the crate door the first time the dog voluntarily goes inside.
**Why:** That first voluntary entry is a gift — the dog is telling you they're beginning to see the crate as a positive space. Locking the door immediately can shatter that association, especially in a dog who hasn't yet built up duration and trust. Reward the entry, let the dog come back out on their own terms, do it again. The door closing is a layer that comes after the value of going in has been thoroughly built. Invest in that foundation and the door will never be a battle.
