# Social Pressure Zones

## When to Use
- Dog has any form of heightened sensitivity to other dogs or unfamiliar people on leash
- Handler wants a clear system for reading their dog's arousal state during outings and responding appropriately
- Building a rehabilitation plan for a dog who lunges, barks, growls, or shuts down around social triggers
- Handler wants to know what they can teach, what they can only manage, and what recovery looks like

## Fails When
- Attempting to teach or correct in the yellow or red zone — a dog in that state literally cannot learn
- The code yellow protocol has never been practiced in a green zone, so the dog has no idea what you are asking when you need it most
- Letting the dog creep into yellow while staring at a trigger, thinking "they're okay" — and then being blindsided when they tip into red
- Skipping the foundational work at home and trying to grow confidence directly in triggering environments
- Not addressing potential medical contributors: thyroid imbalances, pain, or diet-related inflammation can all drive social sensitivity and cannot be trained away

## Core Concept

I want to be clear about something from the start: if you have a dog who is showing what the world calls reactivity, please do not feel shame about that. Your dog is a combination of genetics, early experiences, and everything your mentorship has shaped in them. This is a journey. My entire team is passionate about two things — helping every dog live a better life tomorrow than they are living today, and helping every owner get there alongside their dog.

Now, the dog that people most often bring to me in distress is what I call the socially sensitive dog. There are three versions. The first is the dog who sees another dog and shrinks — backs behind your leg, peeks around at the world, body language saying "I am not sure about any of this." That dog gets met with empathy. The second type gets invigorated — off leash they might start spinning in circles, running laps, going low and then springing away. That dog gets met with laughter. The third type goes proactive: they puff up, show teeth, growl, lunge, air snap. That dog gets met with judgment. But here is the thing — the dog who shrinks and the dog who gets invigorated are often on the way to becoming the dog who goes proactive. They are all on the same spectrum of social sensitivity, and the same three phases of work will help all of them.

Before I give you those phases, I have to say it plainly: before you go any further into dog training, rule out the obvious medical contributors. Get a full thyroid panel done by your veterinarian — even a dog showing on the low end of "normal" can benefit from a small dose of thyroid support, and it can make a meaningful difference to social sensitivity. Check for pain: get your dog seen by a veterinarian, a canine chiropractor, or a body worker. Diet matters too. My own young dog This! showed dramatic improvement in her social sensitivity when I adjusted her food — lowered the protein, added more varied fruits and vegetables and leafy greens. Gut health and brain behavior are deeply connected.

With that said, here is the framework I use. Think of your dog's arousal state in terms of three zones.

The **green zone** is where your dog will always engage with you. They will always take food. They will always play a game. They will always do a food puzzle, settle on a mat, take a bone, or fall asleep. This is where learning happens. Where is your dog's green zone? It might be your living room, your backyard, your car. It might be outside a flyball tournament but not inside it. Know your dog's green zones precisely.

The **yellow zone** is where the dog is a little concerned. They notice something, look, maybe freeze for a second, but they can come back. They will still eat. They will still play a game if you ask. They might glance toward a trigger and then look back at you. This is the zone of heightened awareness, not crisis. No degree of yellow is acceptable as a destination state. Your goal is to stay in green and use yellow as data about what needs more work.

The **red zone** has degrees. There is the early "I see a dog, I think I see a dog" and there is full-out screaming, lunging, and losing control. Any redness is a signal: you have let your dog get too close to a trigger, or a trigger has appeared too quickly. There is no code red protocol. The code red protocol is the smack to your own forehead and the acknowledgment that you let it get away from you.

The **code yellow protocol** is a set of responses you have prepared for when your dog begins to show yellow. Things like: pivot and run in the opposite direction. Pivot and retreat at a walk if you can see it far enough away. Ask your dog to go between your legs — what I call Home Zone — so they can feel protected while the trigger passes at a distance. You are not teaching in yellow. You are not correcting in yellow. You are managing and protecting. The critical thing to understand: you must practice your code yellow protocol *in green*. A basketball coach does not yell technique corrections mid-game. You teach in practice. Practice the pivot and retreat in your backyard. Practice the Home Zone in your living room. Get it to first-rep right in six environments before you ever need it in the field.

## How to Apply

**Phase 1: Grow Confidence — Passive and Active**

Passive confidence comes from the relaxation protocol: teaching your dog to go to a mat or bed and genuinely relax on cue. Start in one room of your house, get it reliable there, then grow it room by room, then to your backyard, your deck, your front yard, a friend's yard. The goal is a dog who can choose to relax in any environment, on any surface. That dog is not on high alert. That dog has an alternative to vigilance.

Active confidence comes from games your dog loves. The foundational ones are: hand targets (nose to palm), the search game (dog finds a treat you threw on the ground), perch work and pivots (dog moves their rear end to find reinforcement zone on both your left side and your right side), Home Zone (dog goes between your legs into a sit), and the pivot-and-leave and pivot-and-run. All of these need to reach first-rep right in six locations: at minimum three rooms inside your house and three outside. The reason for both sides is critical with the socially sensitive dog — you need the ability to say "switch" and have your dog move from left to right so you can put yourself between them and an oncoming trigger.

**Phase 2: Practice Your Code Yellow Protocol in Green**

Pick the games your dog loves most and practice the code yellow moves — pivot and run, pivot and retreat, Home Zone — in places where there is no social pressure. Do a pivot and run in your front yard for fun. Ask for Home Zone in the kitchen, in the driveway, at a quiet park. Build so much value and repetition into these moves that when your dog shows yellow on a walk, the moves fire cleanly because they have a huge reinforcement history behind them.

**Phase 3: Reflect and Recharge**

After every outing, evaluate. Three things that went well. One thing you would do differently. If your dog had any time in yellow or red, they have accumulated stress. Recharge: give them sniff time, a food puzzle, a body massage. I have had a professional give my dogs a full body massage once a week for over ten years. At a minimum, find a good canine massage therapist or kinesiologist and get your dog worked on at least once a month. Stress accumulates in the body and we need to help it out.

## Examples

**Situation:** A dog who lunges and barks at every dog on the street. Handler is mortified. Everyone they pass judges them.

**Application:** Before working near other dogs, the handler spends two weeks building relaxation protocol and five game behaviors in three rooms of the house. They practice pivot-and-run and Home Zone daily in the house, the backyard, and the front yard. Then they take the dog to a large park at a time when dogs are not present and run those same games. When they finally walk the dog near other dogs, they stay at whatever distance keeps the dog solidly in green, practice the pivot and retreat every time a dog appears in the distance, and feed generously in reinforcement zone throughout. They do not try to get closer until the games are first-rep right at that current distance.

**Illustrative result:** Over several weeks, the handler builds a reliable code yellow response — dog sees another dog in the distance, handler pivots, dog comes with them immediately and orients to reinforcement zone. The lunging at close-range encounters still needs work, but the handler now has a strategy and a way to keep the dog under threshold for the vast majority of every walk.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Let someone tell you to correct a reacting dog in the moment or flood them by "letting them get used to it."
**Why:** Any animal in fight-or-flight cannot process information. If someone is following you down a dark alley, getting faster, and they lean in and ask you what the square root of 64 is — you cannot answer. Your dog cannot learn while another dog is lunging at them, or while they are lunging at another dog. Corrections in that state do not teach the dog that other dogs are safe. They add aversive experience to an already aversive emotional state. Train in green. Manage in yellow. Never let it go red.
