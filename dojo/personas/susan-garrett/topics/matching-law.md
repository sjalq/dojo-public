# Matching Law

## When to Use
- Dog listens perfectly at home but disappears mentally at the park
- Handler is surprised that higher-value treats are not working around squirrels, other dogs, or exciting stimuli
- Someone is considering punishment-based tools because "cookies aren't working"
- Discussing why a dog's behavior history in the environment is working against the handler

## Fails When
- Treated as a static ratio to overcome through sheer repetition of rewards — the law is about value and recency, not just count
- Used as justification to never ask the dog to work around distractions — the solution is management plus deliberate transfer of value
- Handler attempts to compete with a full predatory-sequence trigger in the moment — matching law tells you that is the wrong time to train

## Core Concept

The Matching Law is a principle from behavioral science, first described by psychologist Richard Herrnstein. In its original form it states that organisms distribute their behavior across available options in proportion to the reinforcement available from each option. Susan Garrett applies this directly to dog training to explain something every dog owner has experienced: the dog who is 100% attentive in the kitchen and invisible to you the moment a squirrel appears.

The way Susan frames it is this: the probability of your dog choosing one behavior over another is entirely dependent on the reinforcement history of each of those behaviors. The trap people fall into is thinking the math is simple. If my dog has chased a rabbit five times, I just need to give him a cookie six times and we've tipped the scales, right? Not even close. What the Matching Law is really talking about is that all reinforcement is not equal. You have to weigh three things: the value of each reinforcement, the recency of each, and the rate of reinforcement for each behavior. A cookie and the experience of a full predatory chase are not the same unit of value. Treating them as though they are is where handlers go wrong.

Value is where it gets really interesting. Susan connects the Matching Law directly to the predatory sequence that exists in domesticated dogs: orient, eye, stalk, chase, bite to grab, bite to kill, eviscerate, consume. Different breeds have different parts of that sequence amplified through selective breeding. Her border collies have a powerful orient, eye, stalk, and chase. A terrier might have a very strong grab and eviscerate. When a dog gets to practice a behavior that taps directly into one of those hard-wired internal drives, the reinforcement value is not just strong, it can be outrageous compared to anything you could pull out of your treat pouch. Add recency to that: a dog who was allowed to rehearse chasing behaviors repeatedly as a puppy has a reinforcement history on the side of the environment. The cookie may never win that contest in the moment.

## How to Apply

1. Map the reinforcement landscape honestly. What does your dog find reinforcing? What does the environment offer that taps directly into their instinctive drives? A beagle getting on the trail of an animal, a whippet chasing something moving, a retriever grabbing and carrying. Name those instinctive reinforcers because they are the ones that will beat your treat pouch unless you manage access to them.

2. Control the rehearsal history. The Matching Law compounds over time. Every time a dog self-reinforces in the environment, they are adding to the reinforcement history of that behavior. Puppies and new dogs go outside on leash precisely so they do not build up a bank of rehearsals you will later have to compete against. You cannot undo those rehearsals with cookies. You can only manage them, reduce their recency, and build a competing history through deliberate training.

3. Never try to out-compete matching law in the moment. If your dog is in a high-drive state, orienting hard at something that taps their predatory sequence, this is not the time to test your recall or ask for a sit-stay. You will lose that competition. The correct response is management: change the environment, increase distance, reduce arousal. Then build your reinforcement history systematically through the three stages of any behavior: identify value, transfer value, and test value in gradually increasing distraction levels.

4. Use the predatory drive for you, not against you. Once you have management in place and the dog is not self-reinforcing in the environment, you can begin to make high-drive activities available through you. Instinctive reinforcers are the most powerful ones you have access to. A dog who can only chase a toy when you throw it, only gets to swim when you release them, only gets to run when you signal it, now has a reinforcement history building on your side of the equation.

## Examples

**Situation:** A handler has a rescue dog who chased down the neighbor's cat twice in the first week. Now they want to walk the dog off-leash and are frustrated that their treat pouch is being ignored whenever a cat appears.

**Application:** Susan would point to the reinforcement history: two high-value rehearsals of chase have stacked up, and a bag of kibble is not going to tip those scales in the moment. The prescription is management first. The dog goes on leash or long-term house line while a reinforcement history is being built through games like It's Your Choice and controlled exposure to low-level distractions with high-value reinforcement. You are slowly changing the ratio, not trying to win a single moment with a bigger cookie.

**Illustrative result:** Over months of managed rehearsal and deliberate value transfer, the dog learns that cats in the environment are a signal that good things come through the handler, because they have never had recent unmanaged access to the chase. The reinforcement history has shifted. The cookie is not competing with the cat; you are.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Escalate treat value indefinitely trying to out-compete a full predatory response in real time.

**Why:** You are not going to win that competition in the moment. The Matching Law is not a real-time negotiation you can win with a better offer. It is a historical ratio. Attempting it trains the dog that you are irrelevant when excitement is high, erodes confidence in your own training, and often leads handlers to conclude that positive reinforcement "doesn't work" for their dog, pushing them toward corrections. The solution is not a better treat in that moment. It is better management across weeks and months so the reinforcement history shifts. As Susan puts it, we understand matching law, so we understand that in that state we aren't going to try to get that dog to overcome anything — and we're not going to turn to punishment either.
