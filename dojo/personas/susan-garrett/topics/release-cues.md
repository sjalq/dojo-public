# Release Cues

## When to Use
- Any time a dog is being asked to hold a control position: sit, down, stand, mat, crate
- Puppy or new dog is beginning training and the handler wants to build clarity from the start
- Dog breaks position inconsistently and the handler is reaching for "stay" to patch it
- Handler wants to use multiple release words to give a dog specific information about what comes next

## Fails When
- Release word is something the dog hears constantly in conversation, so it loses its predictive power
- Handler relies on body movement to signal release, meaning the dog is watching feet and body rather than listening for a word
- "Stay" gets added as an extension of "sit" after both have already been partially eroded, compounding the confusion rather than solving it
- Release is only taught in easy contexts and never proofed against distraction

## Core Concept

The word "stay" is redundant. That is the position Susan holds, and she holds it firmly. When you ask a dog to sit, there is no automatic expiration on that sit. Sit does not mean sit for five seconds and then check back in. It means sit there until you give another cue. If the dog is allowed to break the sit whenever they feel like it as long as you have not said stay, then you have a problem with the sit, not with the absence of stay. Adding the word stay does not solve a broken sit. It adds another word that then also gets eroded. Now you have eroded both of them.

The more fundamental point is about sequencing. Susan teaches the release word before the control word. That is backwards from how most people learn to train: you teach sit, sit, sit, sit, and then okay means you can move. The problem with that sequence is the dog learns to watch for movement, not for a word. They see you step toward them or shift your weight and they release. Movement teaches the dog to move. What you want is a dog who is listening for a specific word, not reading your body. And you cannot build that if you layered sit on top of itself before you ever gave the dog the clarity of what releases them.

In crate games, Susan never says the word sit. She shapes the dog to sit through the mechanics of the game — touching the door triggers sitting because cookies come high and at the back of the crate. The dog learns the concept of sitting without the word ever being introduced. What does get introduced early and meaningfully is the release word. The most important word in the dog's life. For Susan's dogs, that word is break. Not okay. Okay is a word that falls into everyday conversation constantly. You say it on the phone. You say it when you agree with someone. You say it when you just want a pause. A dog hears "okay" dozens of times a day and nothing predictable follows it. It ends up in the category of words the dog ignores, or worse, the category of words the dog finds confusing. One of Susan's early dogs was waiting on a mat and she was laughing on the phone saying okay, okay, and all her dogs came bounding up the stairs covered in mud. That day she switched to break.

Control position words — sit, down, stand, hop it up — are sacred. They mean go to that position and remain there until you receive a new cue. The new cue might be a spotlight that sends you somewhere to collect a reward. It might be a recall. It might be a cue to shift position. But none of those happen until the handler says so, and "stay" is not one of the things the handler needs to say. The release word is the ying and yang of every control behavior. You cannot have one without the other. The release makes the hold meaningful.

## How to Apply

1. Choose your release word before you teach any control positions. It must be a word you do not use in regular conversation. "Break" is Susan's choice. "Free" is common but conflicts with certain program games. The word should be short, distinct in sound from your control words, and something you can say with a consistent tone that your dog will learn to track.

2. Build the release word through crate games before introducing control words. The dog learns that being in the crate or in position has tremendous value, and that the release word is the signal that now they may move. The dog who does not yet understand the release word will not understand the control word either. Sequence matters.

3. Never use movement as the release signal during formal training sessions. If you step toward your dog or shift your body and they break position, stop. Proof the verbal release specifically: give the release word without moving, and reward the dog for responding to the word rather than to your body. Susan walks over to the dog and touches them before releasing rather than calling them across the room, partly for this reason and partly to keep the waiting dog relaxed rather than on high alert.

4. Use spotlights as release words. The cue "search" means you can leave position and look for food on the ground. The cue "bring" means leave position and go grab that toy. "Strike" means spin back toward me and let's tug. Every one of these is a release from position that also tells the dog exactly what is coming. The dog has multiple release words, each predictive, each specific. This is more clarity, not more confusion.

5. If you have two dogs and need to release only one, pair the release word with the individual dog's name: "This, break" releases only that dog. The other dog learns their name was not called so they stay. Build this in crate games first, individually, before you ever introduce both dogs together in a control position context.

## Examples

**Situation:** A handler's dog holds a sit for about three seconds and then wanders off if not immediately rewarded. The handler has been saying "sit, stay, stay, STAY" with increasing intensity.

**Application:** The word "stay" is doing nothing here because "sit" itself has not been built with a clear release word. Susan would take it back to the foundation: introduce a release word the dog has never heard (not "okay"), build massive value for the release through crate games, and shape the sit again with the understanding that sit means hold until you hear the release word, not hold until you wander off. The repetition of "stay" gets retired entirely.

**Illustrative result:** Once the dog has a release word they genuinely understand and love, the sit holds because the dog is waiting for that word. The wait is not anxiety or obedience under pressure. It is a dog who knows exactly when the party starts and has learned that the word they love comes after a period of holding position.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Teach "sit" and "down" before your dog fully understands the release word.

**Why:** Without a clear, well-conditioned release word, the dog has no anchor for what hold position actually means. They learn to read environmental and body cues to guess when they are free to move. As distractions increase, those guesses become more frequent. Adding "stay" on top of an already-eroding "sit" does not reinforce the concept. It adds another word to erode. The sequence should always be: release word first, deeply conditioned, and only then do control position words go in behind it with the full meaning of "hold until you hear the release."
