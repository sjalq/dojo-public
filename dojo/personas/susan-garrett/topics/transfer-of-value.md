# Transfer of Value

## When to Use
- Dog ignores the handler in exciting environments despite "knowing" the behavior at home
- Handler is relying on food lures and noticing motivation plateauing over time
- Dog only works when a specific reinforcer is visible or physically present
- Handler wants to build genuine drive for training games, cues, or the relationship itself
- Preparing to use the Premack principle to channel a dog's excitement into cooperation

## Fails When
- Repeating the same reinforcer at the same value every rep — the dog predicts it, it becomes neutral, dopamine production flatlines
- Using a food lure from the very first repetition — the lure is always expected, so the dog's brain has nowhere to spike
- Dog is dealing with chronic stress, illness, or gut problems — dopamine production is suppressed and the dog lacks the motivation resources to seek out reward
- Contingencies are applied inconsistently — if "sort of this gets that," the predictive chain collapses and with it the transfer

## Core Concept

There is a neurotransmitter in your dog's brain called dopamine, and it has been misunderstood for a long time. Most people think dopamine is the feel-good chemical, the thing that floods the brain when the dog gets the cookie. But research in the last fifteen to twenty years has completely rewritten that story. Dopamine is less about happiness and more about the *drive to seek out* happiness. It is motivation, not reward. And when you understand that, everything about how you structure your training changes.

Here is what actually happens. Dopamine gets released when the reward your dog receives is *greater* than what they expected. Scientists call that a positive prediction error. If the dog expected nothing and got something wonderful, spike. If the dog expected something wonderful and got something even better, spike. If what they got was exactly what they expected, the dopamine response is flat. And if they expected something and got less, dopamine production shuts down briefly. So the only way to keep your dog genuinely motivated and seeking is to keep creating that gap between expectation and outcome.

Now here is where transfer of value becomes so powerful. In a landmark series of experiments by Dr. Schultz, researchers measured exactly when dopamine spiked in monkeys learning a task. The assumption was that the spike would happen when the reward arrived. But that is not what happened. As soon as a light came on — the cue that meant "game on, press the lever, the reward is coming" — the dopamine spike happened right then, at the cue, and stayed elevated throughout the work. It only dropped when the animal actually received the reward. The signal transferred backward through the chain. The *predictor* of reward became as neurologically potent as the reward itself. Dr. Mark Humphries, a neuroscientist at the University of Nottingham, described it this way: dopamine neurons do not just signal the difference between reward received and reward expected; they also transfer that signal to unexpected things that predict rewards.

This is what I have been calling transfer of value for years. When you pull out your clicker, your dog's dopamine spikes. When you pick up a specific leash, it spikes. When you say a particular word, it spikes. The thing before the thing has taken on the value of the thing itself. And you can chain this. First the cue, then the behavior, then the reinforcement — and each preceding element in that chain can acquire genuine motivating power. That is how training becomes *addictive* to your dog, not just palatable.

The flip side of this is why food-lure training has a ceiling. The very first time a dog tastes a piece of food they love, they get a spike. But from repetition two onward, if it is the same food in the same hand in the same position, it is now predicted. It is expected. The positive prediction error is zero, and the dopamine production is flat. I asked Dr. Steven Lindsay this exact question back in 2012 right after he presented Dr. Schultz's original work at the University of Guelph. I asked him: does this mean dogs trained with lures are not going to get the same frequency and magnitude of dopamine spikes as dogs whose behaviors are shaped? He said, in his opinion, one hundred percent. When the lure is always there, the brain has no prediction error to work with.

This connects directly to the Premack principle — developed by psychologist David Premack — which says that the opportunity to engage in a high-probability behavior can be used to reinforce participation in a lower-probability behavior. Put simply: if you sit, then you get to swim. What I want you to see is that the value of swimming does not stay with swimming when you apply this consistently. The value of swimming gets put *into* the sit. The value gets put into the cue that precedes the sit. It eventually even gets put into you, because you are the one controlling access to the swim. I watched this happen with my dog Buzz, who was absolutely wild about water. I started asking for a simple sit at the pond's edge before I gave him the cue to go for a swim. Very quickly he was offering that sit without being asked, staring at the water, and then looking at me to give the green light. The value of swimming transferred through the sit and through the cue and into our relationship. He went from wanting to stay in the water for forty-five minutes to voluntarily coming back out within a minute or two, because the act of coming back and checking in with me had borrowed all that swimming value.

## How to Apply

1. **Build a hierarchy of reinforcement before you need it.** Know what your dog considers low, medium, and high value — food, toys, activities, and social interaction. You need variety so you can create genuine surprise and outperform the dog's expectations at key moments.

2. **Start shaping rather than luring.** Allow the dog to offer behaviors and then mark and reward them. The dog who is searching for the right answer, not following a cookie, is a dog whose brain is in dopamine-seeking mode for the entire session. Every correct guess that produces an unexpected reward is a spike. That is addictive training.

3. **Use the Premack structure for anything your dog goes crazy over.** Identify the high-probability behavior — the thing your dog cannot stop thinking about. Establish a simple, achievable behavior the dog already knows well in many environments. Apply the if/then contingency: if behavior one, then you access behavior two. Be completely clear about the criteria. Do not re-cue. Do not force. If the dog cannot do behavior one, you are too close to the exciting thing — back away until they can. Apply the contingency consistently every single time.

4. **Vary the value of your reinforcement unpredictably.** Use a trail-mix approach: mostly moderate value, occasional low, and random high-value drops that the dog cannot predict. Predictable reward value kills the dopamine spike. Unpredictable value sustains it.

5. **Watch for and protect dopamine suppression.** If your dog has been sick, is in pain, is dealing with gut issues, or has been under chronic stress, their dopamine production is compromised. Be more conservative with your training demands during those times. Negative prediction errors — expecting a reward and not getting one — are particularly costly when dopamine reserves are low.

## Examples

**Situation:** A Border Collie goes completely wild at the park entrance, barking, spinning, and pulling, making it impossible to get through the gate calmly.

**Application:** Back far enough away from the gate that the dog can offer a simple behavior they know well — a sit or a hand touch. Deliver that, then give a clear permission cue to go through the gate and run. Repeat: gate approach, sit, permission. Keep the sit criteria crisp. If the dog cannot sit because the gate is too exciting, back up further. Over several sessions, the sit — and then the approach to the gate — begins accumulating the value of everything that happens on the other side.

**Illustrative result:** Within a few weeks the dog starts offering the sit as soon as the handler moves toward the gate, because the sit has acquired transfer of value from every exciting thing that follows it. The chaos at the gate disappears not because it was punished but because the approach to the gate now reliably predicts the best game in the world.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use the same treat, same quantity, same timing every single repetition and expect continued enthusiasm.
**Why:** You have removed all positive prediction error from your training. The dog's brain correctly learns that every rep will produce the same thing, dopamine production settles into baseline, and you get a dog who will work when it is easy and opt out the moment the environment offers anything more interesting. Vary value, vary timing, vary which behaviors earn the jackpot.

**Don't:** Produce the lure before the dog has offered any behavior.
**Why:** The moment the lure appears before the behavior, it becomes the cue — and a cue that is always present is never surprising. You get one initial dopamine hit from novelty and then a flatline. Shape the behavior first, add the cue after, and let the reinforcer arrive as a consequence of correct behavior, not as a bribe to get the behavior started.
