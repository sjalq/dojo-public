# Hand Target

## When to Use
- Owner wants one foundational game that does many things at once — focus, engagement, position work, redirect
- Dog needs something simple and reinforcing to orient toward in distracting environments
- Owner wants to teach a complex behavior like lining up at the side or agility start line, and the direct approach isn't working
- The concept of game within a game is being used for the first time

## Fails When
- Reinforcement history with the hand target is thin — the dog doesn't value it enough to choose it over interesting things nearby
- The hand target is only practiced in sterile, non-distracting settings and never tested where it's actually needed
- Owner telegraphs the hand target so obviously that the dog only responds when set up, rather than as a spontaneous orienting behavior

## Core Concept

A game within a game is where the primary reason for playing the game is disguised within the context of a much simpler game. The hand target is one of the best examples of this I can give you. On the surface, it's a simple game: I put my hand out, the dog touches their nose to it, they get reinforced. That's it. It takes about five minutes to teach. But inside that simple game, if you build it correctly, is a tool that can do dozens of things — get a dog to line up at your side, redirect focus in a distraction, serve as a check-in behavior on walks, set an agility start line, create inspired focus without a single command.

Think about the 2010 Karate Kid remake. Jaden Smith's character Dre comes to Mr. Han to learn kung fu. Instead of teaching him kung fu, Mr. Han gives him this task: put your jacket on in a specific way, drop it, pick it up, hang it on a hook. Over and over, thousands of times. Dre thinks he's learning about respect. He eventually has a meltdown — he says Mr. Han doesn't even know kung fu. And then Mr. Han calls him over, tells him to pick the coat up, and as he moves through the motion Mr. Han grabs his arm. Suddenly Dre sees what he's built. He can block. He can duck. He learned all of it through picking up the coat. He just didn't know it. That is the game within a game. For our dogs, unlike Dre, we make sure the game within the game is a lot of fun — because we're transferring the value from the reinforcement into the game itself.

Once that value is built into the hand target, you can put your hand out and my dogs will touch it 40, 50 times. I've actually counted once and stopped my dog at 100 touches. They just kept touching. Sometimes they'd glance at me as if to say — do you see what I'm doing here? — and then touch again. That's because the value of the reinforcement has moved into the game. The game itself has become reinforcing, independent of what prize might or might not appear. That's the point you're working toward.

## How to Apply

1. **Build the behavior.** Present your hand flat, at nose height. Dog investigates and touches their nose to it. Mark (click or yes) and deliver a cookie. Repeat. In one session of a few minutes, most dogs are offering confident, repeatable nose-to-hand contact. You don't need a verbal cue right away. Let the behavior become fluent and enthusiastic first.

2. **Build the reinforcement history.** This is the most important step and the one most people skip too quickly. You need many, many repetitions in low-distraction environments where the hand touch is reliably reinforced. The more reinforcement history behind the hand, the more the value of the food transfers into the game. When you walk into a distraction later and put that hand out, you're calling on all of those banked repetitions.

3. **Use it as a game within a game.** Here is the lining-up example: you want your dog at your left side. Put your feet together. Plant your right foot. Step your left foot back, in line with where you were standing. Extend your left hand back for the dog to touch. They touch it, you drop a cookie back there — placing reinforcement in the exact location you want them to be. Then step forward. As the dog steps forward, quickly step back again before they can swing their nose around in front of you. Hand back again. Touch. Cookie back there again. Do that three or four times, then bring your feet together, look left, and say sit. The dog was behind you expecting a cookie but now they're at your side getting the cookie in a sit. They thought they were playing hand targets. You were teaching start line position.

4. **Use it as a focus and redirect tool.** When the dog is being drawn toward a distraction, put your hand out. Don't say the dog's name first. Don't say touch. Just put the hand out. A dog with a strong hand target history will glance over eventually — how long has that been out? — and then move toward it. As the hand target becomes more fluent, you can back away from a distraction as you present the hand, giving the dog a moment to disengage and come to you. The hand target becomes the on-switch for engagement. Combine this with It's Your Choice — put a cookie on the ground and put your hand out — and you have the single most powerful focus exercise I know: the dog looks at the thing they want on the ground, looks at your hand, and chooses the hand. That is inspired focus. That is a dog choosing to work with you.

## Examples

**Situation:** Dog has learned a strong hand target indoors. Owner wants to use it outside at the park where the dog tends to disengage. Dog is at the end of a long line, looking at other dogs playing in the distance.

**Application:** Owner waits. Puts hand out behind their back, then presents it calmly to the side. Dog glances. Owner does not call the name. Just holds the hand available. Dog checks back in, touches the hand. Owner celebrates, plays tug, gives three cookies. Puts hand away. Dog returns to watching the field. Owner puts hand out again. Dog checks in faster. Over several sessions this builds a habit of checking in when the hand appears, even in the presence of other dogs.

**Illustrative result:** After weeks of hand target practice in increasingly distracting environments, the dog begins offering voluntary check-ins on walks without the hand even being presented — looking up at the owner to see what game might be next.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Always pair the hand target with saying the dog's name first.
**Why:** The whole power of the hand target as a focus tool is that it works silently. If you teach the dog to expect their name before the hand target, you've made the hand target dependent on an auditory cue. You want the visual of the hand to produce spontaneous re-engagement on its own. Name plus hand target is less useful than hand target alone in the situations where you need it most.

**Don't:** Introduce the hand target in distracting environments before the reinforcement history is built.
**Why:** Calling on a behavior before it has strong value is drawing on a nearly empty bank account. The behavior doesn't have the weight to compete with whatever the distraction is. Build the repetitions at home first, hundreds of them, until the game itself is reinforcing. Then take it out into the world where it needs to work.
