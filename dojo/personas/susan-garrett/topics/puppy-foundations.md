# Puppy Foundations

## When to Use
- New puppy is home and the owner is making dozens of small decisions each day that will compound
- Owner is defaulting to kindness and permissiveness without realizing those decisions have a long lens
- Puppy is sleeping through training games in its crate when the crate door is never closed
- Owner is using the puppy's name repeatedly with no result, or naming behaviors the puppy doesn't understand yet
- Owner wants to know the five foundational games that start everything off right

## Fails When
- Owner thinks "positive is not permissive" means they need to be strict or harsh — it means structure, not punishment
- Cue words are added before the behavior is fluent, diluting their meaning from week one
- Owner allows too much free roaming in big environments before the relationship bank has any deposits in it
- Puppy's name is used repeatedly over and over, teaching the puppy to tune it out

## Core Concept

Here is the thing I want everyone to know: you start training your puppy the moment it comes into your home. Not when you start formal lessons. Not when you enroll in a class. The moment that puppy walks through the door, the environment you set up, the small decisions you make, the things you allow and don't allow — all of that is training. And most puppy challenges do not start out as problems. They start out as small, kind decisions made multiple times throughout the day. The puppy is a sponge. They are constantly learning through the experiment of choice and the outcomes of that choice.

Probably 30 years ago I coined the phrase: positive is not permissive. Just because we train with positive reinforcement doesn't mean we allow the puppy to do what they want when they want. I have friends who are raising a new golden doodle, and they are leading with kindness, which is beautiful to see. But they never closed the crate door. So after night one, the puppy chose to come out at 2 a.m. and pee on the floor. And then they set alarms to take him out in the night. Ten days later they were still doing it, telling me his little bladder can't hold on. I said to them: if someone woke me up at 2 a.m., I promise you I would have a pee. But if they let me sleep until 6 a.m., I would be completely fine. Structure builds confidence and behaviors much faster than kindness alone ever could. Management is not restrictive. Management is proactive. The one thing you do not want when your puppy is a year old is a long list of regrets.

Think with the long lens. Everything you do now, ask yourself: what am I doing right now that might have repercussions I don't want to deal with later? Things like greeting the dog by letting them jump all over you when they're eight weeks old. When they're an adult, is that okay for them to greet other people like that? The behaviors you grow are the behaviors you get. What you grow is what you get. And giving a puppy the keys to the Lamborghini — free roaming with all the freedom and no skills — means poor choices are coming. Good leadership guides decisions in a way that the puppy thinks it was their idea, and looks forward to making another good choice in the future.

## How to Apply

1. **Set up the environment before the puppy arrives.** A gated pen with clear panels so you can see the puppy from across the room, a crate inside that pen, and a clear understanding that the crate door gets closed from night one. Not as punishment. As structure that builds security. A puppy who is conditioned to love their crate and to understand the crate door closes has tools for regulating themselves that a puppy with full free roam simply doesn't have.

2. **Start with the five foundational games.** These need almost no vocabulary. They're games, not lessons.
   - Game one: You are a Pez dispenser. High rate of reinforcement. Throw cookies into the crate bottom. Puppy learns — this person is fun, good things happen around them, training is a good time. That's the entire lesson.
   - Game two: Name game. Say the puppy's name once as they're leaving the crate after getting a cookie. They come back. Jackpot. The name means one thing: something amazing is about to happen. It is sacred. Never use it over and over. Every time the name gets ignored is a deposit into ignoring the name in the future, and a puppy ignoring their name is potentially dangerous.
   - Game three: I love this place. Build value for a specific spot, a bed or crate. Cookie goes in, puppy goes in, say their name as they return, they get another cookie. Then start adding the release word — break, followed by the permission word for cookies on the floor — so the puppy learns that good things come from their place and that they can be released from it.
   - Game four: The release word. Break. Not okay. Okay is used too often in everyday conversation and will become white noise. Break is a word with a clear, consistent meaning: you may now move.
   - Game five: Four on the floor. Puppy sits or has four paws on the ground to earn anything they want. Not jumping up. You don't get to steal what you want. You earn it by doing what earns it.

3. **Condition before you name.** Do not rush vocabulary. A puppy does not need to know the word "sit" in week one. They need to have a sit conditioned into specific contexts — when they're in the crate and the door opens, when someone arrives to greet them. The puppy learns that a certain situation always leads to sitting which always leads to good things. Once you can predict with certainty that the behavior is about to happen, and it's happening with joy and speed, then you add the word. My dogs know about 100 cues by 18 months, and they know them all with clarity, confidence, and joy. That's the pace.

4. **Never repeat a cue as praise.** If you say "good sit" to a dog who is already sitting, do you know what they'll do? They'll pop their butt and sit again. Sit is a verb. It means take action. When you use it as praise, you are teaching the dog that sit is a word they can sometimes ignore. You dilute the effectiveness of the cue. Praise the behavior. Say good dog, good boy, use genuine warmth. But don't use the behavior's cue word as the praise word.

## Examples

**Situation:** New puppy has been home for a week. The owners have been letting him roam freely in the living room, saying his name constantly, and naming behaviors like sit and down. He doesn't come when called and ignores his name.

**Application:** Pause the free roaming. Go back to the gated pen with a crate inside. Start with game one — high rate of reinforcement, crate bottom, just teaching the puppy that this person is a great Pez dispenser. Then introduce name game with strict one-utterance-only rule. Each time the name is called, there is an obvious and certain path to reinforcement. Stop naming sit and down — just condition those body positions in specific meaningful contexts. Give the relationship bank time to accumulate deposits before asking for anything.

**Illustrative result:** Within two to three weeks, puppy offers a head whip when name is said once. Sits automatically before greetings without being asked. Chooses to spend time near the owner rather than independently exploring.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Let the puppy free roam in big environments before the relationship foundation is built.
**Why:** The environment will always have something more interesting than an owner the puppy barely knows. Every reinforcement the puppy finds on their own — sniffing, exploring, playing solo — is a deposit into the environment bank instead of the relationship bank. This is the exact dynamic that makes dogs distracted and disengaged later. Start small. Build value for you first.

**Don't:** Name behaviors before they are fluent, consistent, and joyful.
**Why:** Fluency of behavior in a puppy comes long before vocabulary. If you name a behavior the puppy only half understands, you have now taught the puppy that the word applies to both the thing they did well and the wobbly version they did when they were uncertain. You haven't named a behavior. You've named confusion. Condition first, name later, and then the name will be bright and shiny and mean something crystal clear.
