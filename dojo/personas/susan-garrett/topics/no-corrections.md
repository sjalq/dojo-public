# No Corrections

## When to Use
- Owner is using "no," "ah-ah," "hey," or other corrective words and finding them ineffective
- Owner wants to understand why Susan doesn't use the word "no" and what she does instead
- Cue words like "okay," "come," or "stay" have lost their meaning through overuse or inconsistent application
- Owner wants to understand the five categories every word in a dog's world falls into
- Owner is wondering whether corrections are necessary for real-world reliability

## Fails When
- Removing corrections without building alternatives — the dog needs a clear taught behavior to offer when the don't arises
- Confusing no-corrections with no-structure or permissiveness — structure and leadership are still essential
- Words like "break" or a trained recall are introduced but immediately overused and slipped into category two: words the dog ignores

## Core Concept

Someone asked me once: do I believe there's a place in dog training for the word "no"? I did a little experiment. I turned on my phone, pointed it at my dogs, and I said no. I pointed my finger and yelled no. Do you know what happened? Their ears stayed up high on their heads. Their tails wagged. They were like — we don't know what you're saying, but usually when you pay attention to us, something amazing is about to happen. That's how much the word "no" means to my dogs. And that's intentional.

I'm not saying anyone who uses the word "no" is wrong. I'm going to share why I don't. I believe the word "no" is saying you should know better. And if we truly believe our dogs are doing the best they can with the information we've given them in the environment we're asking them to be in, then no is just a judgment. Are they really wrong? Or did we set them up to make a choice we don't like? If we set them up to make a choice we didn't like, does that make them wrong? Possibly it's us that's wrong. All of our dogs are always doing the best they can with the education we've given them. If I want different behavior, I need to change the education or the environment. Not tell them they're bad for doing what the education and environment taught them to do.

Here is what I use instead of no: an unbelievable, automatic, sacred head-whip reaction to the dog's name. I don't care where you are. I don't care what you're doing. If I say your name, stop what you're doing and orient to me. That's what the name means. Not a judgment. Not a punishment. A redirect. Stop and orient. And then we go from there — maybe come here, maybe let's go do something else. The name is sacred. It could save a life. Every time it's used and ignored is a deposit into ignoring it in the future, so I never use it in ways that aren't heading somewhere reinforcing.

## How to Apply

1. **Understand the five categories dogs put words into.** Category one: words they respond to always, no matter what. This is where you want sit, down, the recall, the release word. Category two: words they ignore. Every dog has words in here, often because those words were used too casually. Category three: words that are confusing or they're unsure about. Category four: words that make them happy. Tail up, ears forward, expectation of something good. Category five: words that make them afraid or stressed. You want category five to be as close to empty as possible. When you choose cue words and evaluate how you're using them, you're managing where they land.

2. **Drop "okay" as a release word.** Okay is one of the five most commonly used words in dog training. It is also one of the most commonly used words in everyday human conversation. Those two things are incompatible. I learned this the hard way. I had all my dogs on a mat by the door after a muddy walk, and I got on the phone, started laughing with a friend, said okay — and all the dirty dogs came running upstairs. You said okay. They did what okay means. The problem isn't the dogs. The problem is the word. Replace okay with break, or free, or any word you genuinely don't use in regular speech. I use break. It's clean, it's unusual, and it means one thing: you may now move.

3. **Drop "stay" as a separate cue.** Stay is redundant. When I say sit, that doesn't mean sit for five seconds and then decide you're done. Sit means sit there until I give you a release word. There is no automatic time expiration on the behavior. Adding stay as a second word actually creates confusion — it implies that sometimes sit means just sit briefly, and stay means actually stay. That ambiguity costs you. Sit means sit. Down means down. The release word ends it.

4. **Replace corrective interrupts with name plus redirect.** When Tater was peeing on my flowers, I didn't say no. I called his name. He stopped, oriented. I walked him to the tree where he's allowed to mark — the pee tree. I didn't train him not to mark. I managed it. And that's an important distinction to hold: is this something I want to train, or is it something I can manage? Not everything needs to be a training project. Use the MRI formula when you do want to train it, and manage proactively when you don't need to make it a project right now.

## Examples

**Situation:** Owner has been using "okay" as a release word for two years. The dog sometimes releases on it, sometimes ignores it, sometimes releases when the owner just says "okay" in conversation. The sit is unreliable.

**Application:** Begin pairing "break" with "okay." Ask dog to sit. Say "break" — dog shouldn't move, they don't know what it means yet. Then say "okay" — dog moves. Reward. Repeat about five times, then do something else entirely. Come back in an hour. Do it again. Dogs are brilliant pattern-learners and they will learn that "break" predicts "okay," and eventually they'll start moving on "break" before "okay" arrives. Once they're consistently releasing on "break," drop saying "okay." You now have a clean, specific release word. It might take a few sessions or it might take a week depending on how strong the okay history is.

**Illustrative result:** Within a week of consistent pairing, the dog begins moving on "break" alone. The sit becomes more reliable because the release word now has a single unambiguous meaning and isn't being triggered accidentally mid-conversation.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use corrective words or sounds (no, ah-ah, hey, uh-uh) as your first response when the dog does something you don't like.
**Why:** At best, these words fall into category three — confusing or unclear — and have no effect. At worst, they fall into category five — words the dog fears — and you've introduced tension and stress into a moment that needed calm redirection. Neither gets you closer to the behavior you want. The name plus redirect is cleaner, more precise, and builds the relationship rather than eroding it.

**Don't:** Let the name slip into category two — words the dog ignores — by using it over and over in situations where the dog is not going to respond.
**Why:** Every ignored name is training the dog that the name is optional. If your dog is 200 meters away, deeply in the grip of a distraction they've never been trained to disengage from at that distance, and you call their name five times with no result, you have run five training trials that all produced the wrong behavior. The name should be said once. And when it's said, there should be an obvious, reliable path to reinforcement at the end of it.
