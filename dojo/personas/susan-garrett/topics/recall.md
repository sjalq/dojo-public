# Recall

## When to Use
- Puppy or new dog has no recall history
- Current recall cue has been poisoned through inconsistent use
- Dog is reliable at home or on leash but disappears mentally off leash or with distractions
- Handler wants to build a recall that does not depend on tools

## Fails When
- Distraction level is too high before the value transfer is complete — the dog physically cannot choose you when the environment is winning
- Recall cue has been repeated: if you've had to say it twice, it's been poisoned and needs a fresh start
- Reinforcement is misused as a lure: always needing "something better" than the distraction is an arms race you will eventually lose
- Long line or e-collar provides false confidence — tools can fail, and relying on them means the actual training never happens

## Core Concept

A recall is like anything else we shape. It comes down to three parts: identifying what is valuable to your dog, transferring that value to the recall behavior, and then testing that value in progressively challenging environments. That is the whole thing. Simple in principle, but the reason so many people struggle is they have made one or both of two major mistakes before they even realize they needed a strategy.

The first mistake is rehearsal. Every time a dog is allowed to find reinforcement in the environment on their own — chasing squirrels through a dog door, following their nose off leash at the park, being the one who decides when to leave and when to engage — they are building a reinforcement history on the side of the environment. That history is working against you when you want a reliable recall. A puppy who goes outside only on leash for the first several months of life never builds that bank of unmanaged rehearsals. That is not punishing the puppy. That is protecting the recall. If you have a rescue dog and you do not know what their history contains, the answer is not hopelessness — it is management from today forward and deliberate rebuilding.

The second mistake lives in the foundation. People who train with reinforcement instead of correction sometimes develop a dangerous mindset: I just need something my dog likes better than the distraction. That thinking will eventually collapse. There will always come a moment when what the environment offers is more reinforcing than anything you can pull out of your treat pouch. The answer is not to find a bigger treat. The answer is to transfer value so completely that your recall word itself, and coming to you as a behavior, becomes one of the most predictively exciting events in your dog's world. Similarly, tools like long lines and e-collars create a sense of false security. The long line lets you stop the dog when the training would have told you something. E-collar batteries fail. Remotes get left at home. If you never need these tools, they can never fail you.

## How to Apply

1. Identify what is truly valuable to your dog. Food is a starting point, but the most powerful reinforcers are instinctive ones: for a beagle, the scent trail. For a high-prey-drive dog, the chase. For a retriever, carry and deliver. List every food, toy, and activity your dog finds reinforcing, and rate them. Know your high-value items because those are going in the training. But also identify what the dog finds reinforcing instinctively, because those are the ones you need to bring under your control through you, not compete with.

2. Choose a fresh recall cue. If you have used "come" or your dog's name as the recall and the dog has ever looked at you and not come, or come slowly while scanning for other options, that cue is poisoned. The recall cue should produce a head-whip-and-sprint, not a thoughtful glance. Pick a sound you do not use in everyday conversation. Something that will never get casually spoken around the house. It should stand out from your normal tone. Once you have that sound, condition it exactly like a clicker: use it three or four times in a zero-distraction environment, pair it with something amazing, and end the session. Do not overuse it or you will build a fixated stare instead of a reflexive sprint.

3. Play the foundation games in order, starting with It's Your Choice and Crate Games. These are not optional warm-ups. They build the dog's fundamental understanding that access to good things comes through you, and that waiting for a cue is what earns the reinforcement. From there, play Call Once: one person calls once, the dog comes, the dog gets something great, repeat a handful of times, stop. Add the collar grab: before presenting food, reach for the collar. The hand approaching from above should predict something wonderful, not trigger avoidance. Restraint recalls build drive and urgency toward the recall word.

4. Test value in layers, with great compassion. Zero to very low distraction for the first couple of weeks. A distraction-free environment might be a bathroom or bedroom. Know what zero looks like for your dog and know what a ten looks like. Never start in a ten. When the dog does not come after you have used the cue, that is information: you jumped layers. You do not get angry. You do not repeat the cue. You make a mental note, go back to where you had success, and rebuild. The failure belongs to you, not the dog.

## Examples

**Situation:** A handler has a two-year-old dog who comes reliably in the yard but ignores all recall attempts at the off-leash park. They have been using the word "come" since puppyhood and have noticed the dog looks at them before wandering away.

**Application:** The word "come" is likely poisoned given the dog has rehearsed ignoring it. Susan would recommend retiring that word entirely and starting fresh with a new, unusual sound that has no history. All recall training goes back to near-zero distraction environments while the new cue gets conditioned with high-value reinforcement. The off-leash park is off the table as a training venue until value is solid. The handler works through Call Once, collar grab, and Restraint Recalls before incrementally adding distraction.

**Illustrative result:** After weeks of building value in low-distraction settings and extending into moderate distraction, the new recall word produces the head-whip response reliably because it has never been poisoned. The off-leash park is introduced gradually, with distance and management, until it too becomes just another distraction layer the dog has learned to override.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use the recall cue as a conversational word, a general attention-getter, or a test of whether the dog is listening today.

**Why:** Every time you use the recall word and the dog's response is anything less than a sprint toward you, you are adding a layer of "sometimes this word doesn't mean anything urgent." The recall word should be reserved for situations where you actually need the dog to come, and in training, you should only use it when the environment is set up for success. The moment you repeat it twice in a single session, it is on its way to becoming background noise. Susan puts it plainly: if you have ever had to say your recall word more than once, the cue is already poisoned, because the word should mean head-whip and chase, nothing less.
