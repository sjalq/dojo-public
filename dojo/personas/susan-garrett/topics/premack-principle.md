# Premack Principle

## When to Use
- When a dog has one or more activities they love so intensely that it overrides their training around those things
- When building a recall or reinforcement zone behavior that holds up off-leash in exciting environments
- When the trainer wants that love, that drive, that desire the dog has for something — to get channeled into the relationship
- When the dog won't take food or toys near the thing they want most

## Fails When
- Behavior 1 (the prerequisite) is beyond what the dog can genuinely do in front of the distraction — asking for too much too soon breaks the contingency before it forms
- Coercion is used when the dog can't comply — force is not an option here, only adjusting distance to make success achievable
- The rule wavers — one day it applies, the next day you let it slide — and the dog learns there are exceptions
- Transfer of value is expected immediately — this process builds over time through consistent application

## Core Concept

The Premack principle was identified by psychologist David Premack. Sometimes called "Grandma's Law" — eat your vegetables and then you can have dessert — it describes a powerful contingency: the opportunity to engage in a high-probability behavior can be used to reinforce an animal participating in a lower-probability behavior. In plain terms: if you do the thing I'm asking, then you get to do the thing you love. This is not a trick or a gimmick. It is one of the most powerful tools in building a dog that genuinely wants to work with you.

The scientific terms are "if" and "then." If you sit at the door, then I will open it and you can run outside with the other dogs. But there is criteria — you have to hold that sit while I open the door, while the other dogs go out first. If you can hold your sit, then you get to go. The word "if" carries all the training, and the word "then" carries all the motivation.

Here is the part that is almost magical: when you apply this consistently, the value of the high-drive activity actually transfers into the behavior you ask beforehand. I had a dog named Buzz — he lived to be almost 18 years old and I wrote a book about him called Shaping Success, which won dog training book of the year in 2005. Buzz was absolutely crazy about swimming. When he was near water he couldn't think; all he wanted was to get in. What I started doing with him at the pond was simple. I would ask him to sit at the edge of the water. When he sat, I would click my clicker and say "go for a swim." He'd dive in. After one or two seconds — as soon as the splash settled — I'd call him back out, and he'd come swimming right over, jump out, and sit beside me. After one or two repetitions he wasn't even waiting to be told to sit. He'd come out and plant himself, staring at the water, ready.

What happened over time was extraordinary. The value of swimming got transferred into the cue "go for a swim." Instead of wanting to stay in the pond for 45 minutes, he would swim for a minute or two and come out on his own. And even more beautifully — the value went through me. The chance to do the thing he loved needed me. Our relationship became the access point to what he wanted most. That's the really, really cool thing about applying Premack consistently.

There are five things you need to get right for this to work.

**One: Behavior 1 has to be achievable.** The thing you're asking the dog to do before the high-value activity has to be easy for that dog in that environment. I started by asking Buzz to sit. He'd done it thousands of times. It was completely achievable. You cannot start with a complex behavior in front of the most exciting thing in the dog's life. Begin with a hand touch, a sit, one step in reinforcement zone — something the dog genuinely can do there. You grow the criteria over time, but you start small.

**Two: Criteria has to be clear — for you and for the dog.** If I asked Buzz to sit and his butt touched the ground and then slowly crept back up as he inched toward the water, and I said "go for a swim" anyway, I'd have muddied everything. Brene Brown says it and I love it: clear is kind. You need to know exactly what correct looks like, and you need to hold to it. Creeping doesn't count. A sit is a sit.

**Three: Behavior 1 needs a history of success across many different environments and distractions.** Before you ask a behavior right in front of the thing the dog wants most, that behavior needs to have been trained and reinforced in many different locations, under many different distractions. It has to be a deep, reliable behavior — not a fragile one. If your recall has only ever been practiced in the backyard, do not go to the edge of the dog park and expect it to hold.

**Four: You cannot force Behavior 1, and you cannot re-cue it.** If the dog doesn't sit, you don't pull up on the collar. You don't say "sit, sit, SIT." If the dog can't comply, the only thing you do is lower the intensity of the high-value distraction — back away from the pond, get more distance from the other dogs, move further from the gate. Make success possible again, then ask. If you repeat the cue multiple times, you are teaching the dog that the criteria is actually "sit after you've heard it four times." That's not what you want.

**Five: The contingency must be applied consistently.** This is not something you do sometimes. The "if this then that" has to be a rule you apply every time, not a guideline that bends when you're in a hurry or when the dog gives you sad eyes. If you're patient, persistent, and consistent, the transfer of value happens. If you're inconsistent, the dog learns that the rule has loopholes.

## How to Apply

1. **Make a list of the things your dog goes absolutely crazy for.** Swimming, running with other dogs, chasing birds, going for car rides, getting to go through the front door — anything that causes your dog to lose their mind with excitement. These are your high-probability behaviors.

2. **Make a list of simple, fluent behaviors your dog can do reliably.** A sit, a hand touch, one step in reinforcement zone. These are your low-probability Behavior 1 options. Choose something the dog genuinely knows cold and has done in many environments.

3. **Start in a low-intensity version of the exciting situation.** At a distance from the pond, not at the edge. With one dog visible far away, not in a pack. Ask Behavior 1. If the dog can do it, release them with a clear cue — "go for a swim," "go play," whatever signals permission. If the dog cannot do it, you have too much distraction. Increase your distance.

4. **Use ping-pong to grow the criteria.** Ask the behavior, release to the activity, call back, ask the behavior, release again. Then ask for a slightly harder version: not just a sit but a sit plus one step in reinforcement zone. Then go back and ask for the easy version. Then try two steps. You're building the bank account of success in this environment.

5. **If the dog is not checking back in with you, build a game around it.** When I was building this with my nine-month-old puppy — who would completely ignore me when outside with the other dogs — I created a game I started calling "I think you must be lost." I'd let her run ahead, then I'd change direction. Not recall her, just change direction and keep walking. As she ran further ahead and noticed I'd gone a different way, she'd come screaming back. When she got beside me, I'd change direction again. What I was looking for was the moment she slowed down when she came by me. The first time I played this, it took about 25 direction changes before she stopped near me and looked at me like "what on earth is going on?" That moment got a click and a release to go play. Over time it took fewer and fewer changes, until finally, as soon as I changed direction, she came right into reinforcement zone and walked one step with me before I'd click and send her off. That one step in reinforcement zone was all I needed to start. The Premack principle built it from there.

## Examples

**Situation:** A dog goes insane when other dogs are nearby and won't take food or even look at the handler.

**Application:** The cookies are irrelevant here — the dog is too aroused to take them, which tells you the high-value activity (being near other dogs) is exactly your Premack tool. You get enough distance from the other dogs that the dog can perform a hand touch. You ask for it, they give it, and you immediately release them toward the other dogs. You recall after a short time, ask for another hand touch, release again. You're not competing with the other dogs as a reinforcer — you're using the other dogs as the reinforcer.

**Illustrative result:** After several sessions, the dog begins to turn back toward the handler on their own — not because they've been recalled, but because checking in has become the most reliable path to getting access to the thing they love. The value of running with other dogs has been partially transferred into the handler.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Re-cue the behavior if the dog doesn't respond the first time.
**Why:** Repeating the cue teaches the dog that your first ask is not real — the criteria is actually "comply after the third time I say it." You have not trained a reliable behavior, you've trained a dog who waits for your escalation. If the dog doesn't respond on the first cue, the environment is too intense for their current skill level. Back away, lower the excitement, make it achievable again — then ask once. That is clear. That is kind. And that is what builds the reliable response you're looking for.
