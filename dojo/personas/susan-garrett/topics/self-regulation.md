# Self-Regulation

## When to Use
- Dog is physically expressing an internal emotional struggle — barking, spinning, lunging, humping, digging, or any repetitive arousal behavior
- Handler wants to understand whether they are managing their dog or genuinely building the dog's capacity to manage itself
- Handler has been co-regulating (moving away from triggers, using toys or food to distract) and wants to know how to move past that
- Building a strategic long-term plan for a dog who currently cannot function in exciting or stressful environments

## Fails When
- The foundational physical and environmental needs are not met — a dog running on poor nutrition, no exercise, and a sleep-deprived schedule does not have the resources to regulate
- Handler keeps co-regulating without a plan to progress — crossing the street to avoid every cat will never build the dog's ability to walk past one
- Handler expects the behavior to resolve itself with time or age
- The dog has no history of choice-based games — they have never practiced the experience of feeling an impulse and choosing a different behavior
- The handler is emotionally escalated around the dog's known triggers — the dog reads that and confirms that the trigger is worth being concerned about

## Core Concept

There is a spectrum I want you to understand, and I want you to be honest about where your dog currently sits on it. At one end you have disregulation — that is when a dog feels an emotion and acts on it fully, with no filter. At the other end you have habituation — the thing that used to trigger a response has become white noise, and the dog does not even need to regulate because there is simply nothing to regulate. Between those two poles are co-regulation and self-regulation, and understanding the difference between those two middle stages is everything.

Disregulation is not a character flaw in your dog. It is not stubbornness. It is not dominance. It is simply the inability to manage an emotion in the moment. It will show up in any number of ways: spinning on leash, barking hysterically at a bicycle, humping the houseguest, digging like the garden owes them money, or yes — in more serious cases — biting. When you see that behavior, what you are seeing is an outward physical representation of an inward mental or emotional struggle. Behavior is communication. The dog is not blowing you off. They are showing you something.

Co-regulation is when you, the handler, take action to bring the dog's arousal down. You cross the street to get away from the cat. You pull out a tug toy to redirect the dog past the jogger. You do a U-turn before the dog sees the other dog. All of that is co-regulation and it is 100% appropriate as a short-term tool. The problem I see constantly is people who co-regulate brilliantly — they genuinely care, they move away from triggers, they always have a toy ready — but they have no plan for how to move from "I need to manage this for my dog" to "my dog can manage this for themselves." Without that plan, you end up with a lifelong project. You can never walk your dog past a cat. You can never leave your dog alone in a new place. The trigger always has power because the dog has never been given the tools to override it themselves.

Self-regulation is when your dog feels the emotion — they see the cat, they feel the pull — and they do not act on it. They look at the cat, go "yeah, there's a cat," and look back at you. That is not suppression. That is a dog who has been given enough history with good choices that the pull of the trigger no longer overrides everything else. And beyond that is habituation: the other dog walking past on the path has become white noise because the dog has processed it so many times in positive conditions that it carries no charge anymore. That is Nirvana. Not every dog will get there, but I have seen dogs with some genuinely alarming histories move to a place that is dramatically better. The goal is improvement, not perfection.

Here is the thing about self-regulation that often gets missed: it depends on whether the dog's needs are being met. When your physical, mental, and emotional needs are not met, your own filter for impulse control erodes. The same is completely true for your dog. A dog on poor nutrition, no exercise, sleeping poorly in an overstimulating environment, with no enrichment and no predictability — that dog is running on empty. They do not have the resources to regulate. We have to build the bank account before we can ask the dog to spend from it.

I have ten things I work through when I am looking at a dog's ability to self-regulate. Nutrition first — the gut-brain connection is real, and the quality of food affects behavior in ways that people consistently underestimate. Exercise second, and I mean real exercise: a minimum of two twenty-minute walks daily, with at least one off-leash opportunity to sniff, explore, and run. A healthy environment third — remove artificial fragrances from the home, be thoughtful about chemical exposures, and be aware that the electromagnetic environment our dogs live in today is very different from twenty years ago. Relaxation protocol fourth — I want every dog, regardless of whether they are anxious or hyper, to have a behavior where they have learned how good it feels to just be calm. Choice-based games fifth, and this is the biggest one.

Every dog I have ever owned has won a national or World Championship in at least one dog sport. Every single one. And all of them were raised on choice-based, game-based training. When you give your dog repeated practice in making difficult decisions — when they feel the impulse to do one thing and choose to do another and get reinforced for that choice — you are building the neurological pathway for self-regulation directly. Positive is not permissive. My dogs live with extreme clarity and rich reinforcement. They are not running amok; they are making great choices because they have a long history of great choices being rewarded. That is number five.

Then sleep — dogs need real downtime and not every dog is getting enough of it. Strategic alone time, because a dog who panics the second you are not entertaining them has not learned the difference between dog time and your time. Cooperative teamwork, where the dog is allowed to tell you "I am not comfortable with this" through their behavior and you listen. Journaling, because patterns in disregulation almost always have contributing factors that are invisible until you write them down. And finally, video your training and follow a proven strategic plan rather than gathering random games from random sources.

## How to Apply

1. **Assess honestly where your dog sits right now.** Are you in disregulation? Co-regulation without a plan? Or are you actively building toward self-regulation? This assessment changes what you do next.

2. **Address the foundational needs first.** Before adding more training, look at nutrition, exercise, sleep, and environment. A dog whose gut microbiome is compromised, who is not getting adequate exercise, and who lives in a stimulating but unpredictable environment does not have the physical resources to regulate. These are not optional extras.

3. **Build a relaxation protocol.** Teach the dog to go to a specific position — I use a mat — and to genuinely relax there. Grow this across multiple environments before you ever try it anywhere near a known trigger. A dog who can choose to relax has an enormous advantage when arousal starts rising.

4. **Run choice-based games consistently.** The specific games matter less than the principle: the dog is constantly practicing "I felt an impulse, I weighed my options, I made a good choice, and something great happened." Games like It's Your Choice — where the dog learns that grabbing does not work and waiting does — are building the exact mental muscle needed for self-regulation in harder situations.

5. **Build your co-regulation into a progression plan.** Every time you move away from a trigger, that is fine. But ask: what is the plan to shrink that distance over time? What am I practicing in green that will eventually allow my dog to handle yellow independently? Co-regulation with a destination is strategy. Co-regulation without a destination is indefinite management.

6. **Journal meticulously.** Write down the time, what happened, what preceded it, what else occurred that day. A dog who had a great week and then had a meltdown on Tuesday often had something notable happen Monday: a grooming appointment, a change in food, not enough sleep, a stressful car ride. The pattern is in the data.

## Examples

**Situation:** A dog who goes completely wild every time they see a cat on a walk — spinning, barking, and with zero response to the handler calling their name. The handler has learned to cross the street to avoid cats, but the problem has not improved over two years of this approach.

**Application:** The handler recognizes they have been co-regulating without a plan. They commit to working through the ten pillars: switch to higher-quality food, establish two real daily walks with sniff time, introduce a relaxation protocol in the house, and begin choice-based games daily. They practice pivot-and-run, reinforcement zone, and retrieve at home until all three are first-rep right in five environments. Then they start walking near cats at whatever distance keeps the dog fully in green — initially that might be 200 feet. They play stimulating games at that distance. When the dog voluntarily disengages from the cat and looks at the handler, that is the moment they reinforce.

**Illustrative result:** Over several months, the required distance shrinks. The dog begins to show a look-back at the handler when they first notice a cat — because that look-back has been reinforced so many times. The handler no longer needs to cross the street for every cat. The dog still notices cats; it has not habituated. But the dog has developed enough self-regulation to process the cat and choose the handler instead.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Assume that telling the dog "no" or "leave it" during a moment of disregulation is building self-regulation.
**Why:** You are co-regulating at best — you are the external source of the interruption. The dog has not made a choice; you have overridden them. Self-regulation requires the dog to feel the emotional pull and choose differently. That choice can only be built through a long history of being reinforced for it in gradually increasing levels of difficulty. The correction stops one behavior in one moment. The game history builds a skill that travels everywhere.
