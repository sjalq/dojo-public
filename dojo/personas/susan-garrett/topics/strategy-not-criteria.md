# Strategy, Not Criteria

## When to Use
- Training has stalled and something needs to change, but the goal hasn't changed
- Dog is failing repeatedly and showing frustration, anxiety, or frantic trial-and-error behavior
- Owner is tempted to reward a lesser version of the behavior to get something reinforced
- Owner is unsure whether what they're reinforcing actually communicates what they want to the dog

## Fails When
- The criteria was never clearly defined — if the owner can't describe exactly what they're reinforcing, neither can the dog learn it
- The strategy changes still leave the dog confused because anxiety is the real problem and needs addressing first
- The owner changes strategy but never analyzes why the previous strategy failed, so they repeat the same mechanical errors in a new format

## Core Concept

One common mistake I see when things don't go well in training is that people change their criteria in the middle of the session to make it easier for the dog to be successful. You might think, hey, that makes perfect sense. I'm going to tell you why that is a huge mistake and what to do instead.

Think about going to Grandma's house for Christmas. You head out and the road is closed. You don't say to the kids, "Well, I guess we're going to a different grandma's house." You don't knock on a stranger's door and say this lady will do. No. You just have to change your strategy. Take a different road. Park and hike. Take a boat. Take an Uber. There are a hundred strategies to get to Grandma's house, but Grandma is still Grandma. We never change our goal. We change the strategy to get to that goal.

The very first thing I do when I'm watching a student train their dog is look at the training through the eyes of the dog. Ask yourself: can I see something consistent that the dog is being reinforced for? If I can't see it, I promise you your dog can't see it either. Dogs don't understand trying. They understand behavior. I did this. I got that. I'll do more of this. If you're rewarding a dog for trying, you've created a slot machine. The dog keeps playing, but the rules keep shifting, and that is going to lead to either a frantic dog throwing everything at the wall or a shut-down dog who has stopped believing reinforcement is available. Both outcomes are on us, not on the dog. Our dogs are always doing the best they can with the education we've given them in the environment we've put them in. When it's not working, we need to change the education or the environment. Not the standard we're holding.

Before you change anything, ask yourself one question. It cuts through almost everything: If I reinforce this, does it make it easier for my dog to understand criteria moving forward? Yes or no. If the answer is yes, reinforce it. If the answer is no — if what you're about to reward would actually teach the dog that a lesser version is acceptable, making it harder to get the real thing later — then don't. Change the strategy instead so you can find a version of the behavior that the dog can succeed at while you hold the actual goal firm.

## How to Apply

1. **Eliminate anxiety first.** Check whether the dog is showing signs of anxiety: feet paddling, spinning, vocalizing, heavy panting. A frantic dog cannot think clearly, and a lot of frantic behavior in training comes from too much failure or unclear criteria. Before trying a new strategy for the behavior itself, go back to calm confidence. Something as simple as crate games or a hot zone game where the rules are crystal clear and reinforcement is abundant can reset a dog's emotional state. Once they're calm and thinking again, you can introduce the new strategy.

2. **Eliminate competing reinforcement in the environment.** The best place to teach anything new is the quiet of your own home. If there are other dogs running around, crumbs on the floor, horse poop in the field — those are competing reinforcers and they will dilute your teaching. Even a windowless bathroom is a legitimate training environment because there is genuinely nothing else for the dog to look at. Get your first solid reps in a place where it's easy to be correct.

3. **Split the behavior or grow what is correct.** Before lowering the criteria, ask whether you can find a smaller version of the behavior that preserves the essential quality. Weave poles are a classic example: if the dog is skipping poles in a full set of 12, don't start rewarding them for weaving through 11. Put two poles out and build success, drive, and confidence with just two. That is not changing the criteria. That is splitting the behavior into a teachable piece while the goal stays exactly the same. Alternatively, grow what is correct — make it easier for the right thing to happen. If the dog is missing a back-foot target because the target is small, make the target bigger. Success now, refine the detail later.

4. **Change the strategy, keep the destination.** There may be multiple routes to the same behavior. Teaching backup? You could use spatial pressure, shape it from scratch with a clicker, use furniture to prevent the dog from turning around, or teach a back-foot target and fade it. If one strategy isn't working, pick a different one. The backup is still a backup. The sit is still a sit. What does not change is what correct looks like. What changes is how you help the dog understand and get there.

## Examples

**Situation:** Owner is teaching their dog to weave poles in agility. The dog is going fast but skipping a pole here and there. The owner has been rewarding the dog anyway for "trying hard" and "mostly getting it right."

**Application:** Ask the key question: does rewarding a run with a skipped pole make it easier for the dog to understand the criteria in the future? No — it teaches that skipping is acceptable sometimes, which will make clean weave poles harder to establish, not easier. Change the strategy: go back to two poles. The dog runs through two poles successfully almost every time, gets reinforced heavily, builds confidence and drive, and the neural pattern of entering and exiting correctly begins to solidify. Then add pairs of poles progressively. The destination is a clean full set. The strategy has changed; the destination hasn't.

**Illustrative result:** Dog who was skipping every third or fourth pole shows clean independent weave pole performance once the split strategy builds confidence and clarity without any change to the final criteria.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Reward the dog for "trying" or "being close."
**Why:** Dogs don't generalize from trying to understanding. They learn from the pattern of what gets reinforced. If close enough gets reinforced, close enough is what they'll offer. You'll spend months unraveling that later, trying to sharpen a behavior the dog has every reason to believe they already have nailed.

**Don't:** Change the criteria and call it changing the strategy.
**Why:** Changing the criteria means you've accepted a new, lesser destination. Changing the strategy means you've found a different road to the same destination. They feel similar in the moment — both involve making things easier — but they are completely different in outcome. One says to the dog "the bar is here." The other says "let me help you get over the bar that hasn't moved." Only one of those builds the relationship and the behavior you actually want.
