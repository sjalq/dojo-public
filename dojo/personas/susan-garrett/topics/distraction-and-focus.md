# Distraction and Focus

## When to Use
- Dog ignores owner in favor of smells, other dogs, squirrels, or anything moving
- Owner keeps calling the dog's name with diminishing results
- Owner has said "I don't mind" to small environment-reinforced behaviors that have now escalated
- Dog treats the owner as one option among many, rather than the preferred option
- Owner is starting fresh with a puppy or rescue and wants to build the right foundation

## Fails When
- Forcing focus through nagging commands ("watch me," "heads up," "no sniff") — this creates compliance theater, not an engaged partner
- Continuing to let the dog roam freely and earn reinforcement away from you while simultaneously trying to build focus on you
- Reacting only after the dog is already deeply in the grip of a distraction, rather than preventing the pattern from building in the first place

## Core Concept

In Las Vegas they say the house always wins. In dog training, people say the environment always wins. I'm here to tell you that doesn't have to be the truth. But to change it, you have to understand why it becomes true in the first place. What happens is the dog's love for the environment grows over time, and it grows because we inadvertently feed it. Think about the dishwasher. The dog licks a plate. You say "I don't mind, it's just the dishwasher." Then they sniff around the kitchen baseboard. Nothing there, nothing there, nothing there — and then the 21st time, boom, a big chunk of cheese you didn't even know fell. That is a negative prediction error, which is one of the most powerful reinforcers there is. The dog learns: I should check out every environment because manna from heaven might appear. And you didn't even know you were training that.

Layers of learning that happen intentionally through us are powerful. But layers of learning that happen accidentally through the environment are equally powerful — and they're working against us. Every time your dog does barn hunt, lure coursing, agility, flyball, or chases a squirrel and has the time of their life, you have to ask yourself: what is the thing before the thing? What is the dog doing in the moment before all that joy begins? If the answer is screaming, pulling, clawing on leash — and then you release them to go do the thing they love — congratulations, you just reinforced screaming and pulling. The environment has set up a beautiful system that builds value away from you.

Focus is an expression of priorities — yours and your dog's. When I got my puppy Prophet at 11 weeks old, his top priorities were other dogs, food, sticks, and poop. I was way down the list, and I had no right to expect anything different. Relationships don't start with "I'm the boss, listen to me." They grow because I make my priorities worth having. When Prophet's priorities started to morph toward mine — when I became number one on that list, tied only briefly with other dogs before pulling ahead — that's when we had a harmonious relationship. That shift happened in about five weeks. Not through commands. Through games. Through me being consistently, chaotically, surprisingly reinforcing. Through inspired focus, not forced focus.

## How to Apply

1. **Write your priority lists.** On day one — which is today, whatever day you're reading this — open your journal and write down what a harmonious relationship with your dog looks like. What could you do together that you can't do now? Then write your dog's current priority list. Be honest. If squirrels are number one and you're number seven, that's your starting point. Every 30 days, review. Is the needle moving?

2. **Eliminate the hidden reinforcements.** Walk through your daily routine and find where your dog is earning reinforcement away from you. Kitchen scraps, free roaming in the backyard, releasing them to play with other dogs after they've been pulling on leash — those are all deposits into the environment bank. Stop making those deposits. When you go outside, play games. Let them sniff, sure, but then call them away and play tug or scatter a handful of cookies. You mix it up. You are not a predictable Pez dispenser. You are chaotically, surprisingly reinforcing.

3. **Increase the good habits — the games — daily.** Games are not optional extras. They are the entire mechanism. Hand targets. It's Your Choice. Recall games. These only need a few minutes a day to work. The dog learns that engaging with you leads to outcomes they love. They start checking in — glancing at you mid-walk to see if you're still there — because they want to know what you might have planned. That is the beginning of inspired focus. Nobody tells them to look. They want to.

4. **Combine hand target and It's Your Choice.** Once both games are solid, put a cookie on the ground beside you and put your hand out. The dog has to choose: cookie on the ground or hand target? If they can't handle that level of distraction, move the cookie further away, or take a step back from it. The moment the dog looks at that cookie on the ground and turns back to touch your hand instead — that is the first time your dog has been looking at something they want and said, "I will focus on you." Build from there, layer by layer, until distractions in the world produce a check-in rather than a disappearance.

## Examples

**Situation:** Owner's dog bolts off leash to greet other dogs at the park and cannot be called back. Owner has been saying the dog's name repeatedly with no result.

**Application:** Stop calling the name — each ignored call is a deposit into "ignore the name" territory. Begin rebuilding from scratch with recall games at home, then in low-distraction outdoor spaces. Start putting the dog on a long-line in the park so the environment cannot fully win while the relationship bank is being rebuilt. Every time the dog checks back in voluntarily, that is the opportunity to reinforce massively — not just a cookie, but tug, a game, running together. The dog learns that choosing you leads to something even better than chasing over there.

**Illustrative result:** Within several weeks of daily game play and removing free environmental access, the dog starts offering check-ins without being called. The owner reports the dog looked at a squirrel, then turned back and made eye contact, waiting to see what was next.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Nag the dog into focus with "watch me," "heads up," or repeated name calls.
**Why:** You are not building a dog who wants to focus on you. You are building a dog who has learned that your voice is background noise. Forced focus is compliance. Inspired focus is a relationship. The difference shows up the moment the squirrel runs.

**Don't:** Allow small environment-reinforced behaviors while telling yourself "I don't mind."
**Why:** "I don't mind" said out loud is three words that will come back to bite you. Every unchecked reinforcement in the environment is a training session you didn't intend to run. It compounds. The dog who licks the floor today is the dog who surfs the counter next year.
