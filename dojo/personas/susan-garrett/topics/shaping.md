# Shaping

## When to Use
- Teaching any new behavior from scratch: tricks, sport skills, practical behaviors
- When a free shaping session has stalled and the dog is either guessing frantically or shutting down
- When moving away from lure-based training toward a dog that offers behavior independently
- When a trainer wants sessions that are effective, efficient, and grow genuine dog confidence

## Fails When
- No written plan before training begins — the session becomes hoping, and that is not shaping
- Cheap behaviors (bark, paw swipe, spin) appear without a prevention strategy — they get accidentally reinforced and become the default
- Trainer lumps too many criteria at once — the dog loses clarity and rate of reinforcement plummets
- Cue added before fluency — every messy, incomplete attempt gets locked under that word forever
- Reinforcement placement pulls the dog away from position — the trainer accidentally shapes a different behavior than intended

## Core Concept

If you've ever stood around waiting and hoping your dog would just offer you something so you could click or mark or throw a cookie, then you know exactly what I'm talking about. And what I just described — that's not shaping. That's hoping. Hoping that magic will happen and the dog will just hit on what you're looking for. Real shaping doesn't come from waiting. It comes from strategic design: designing what the dog will notice, designing what the dog will try, designing the dog's success. When it's done well, it looks like the dog magically hit on something brilliant, but it was actually your plan that made it inevitable. That's what I mean when I say I'm "arranging coincidences." It seems like a coincidence to the dog. It isn't.

The behavioral-science origin here belongs to B.F. Skinner and operant conditioning: reinforcement builds behavior. Every single time your dog gets a reinforcement, that behavior gets stronger. That truth works powerfully in your favor when you plan deliberately, and it works just as powerfully against you when you don't. My mentors, Doctors Bob and Marian Bailey, would say constantly: "Be a splitter, not a lumper." Break the finished behavior into smaller component pieces. Bring the dog success at each piece, then combine them. That is what separates elegant shaping from the kind of chaotic frustration where neither dog nor trainer is having a great time.

I plan every shaping session in three sections. The first is designing the learning: what exactly am I trying to shape, what does this particular dog bring in terms of strengths, fears, and emotional state today, and what is my own level of expertise right now? The second section is game day execution: the decisions I make once I actually go get the dog. The third is reflection: what data did I collect, and what is my plan for next session? The heavy lifting is in section one. If you can't clearly state the criteria for success, it is impossible for the dog to meet them. You need the finished picture in your mind before you can teach it to your dog.

A word about luring, because this matters. I was taught to food lure. I had a Jack Russell Terrier named Shelby and a Border Collie who won at the top levels of obedience, agility, and flyball using lure-and-correct methods. So I'm not dismissing anyone who uses lures — I've been there and had success there. But what I observed with Shelby was that she would go slow at the start of an agility course and absolutely blast through the last few obstacles, where the cookies were close. That's a dog doing agility for what she gets out of it, not for the love of the game. The science has a name for what I was creating: backwards conditioning. The food (unconditioned reinforcer) came before the behavior (unconditioned stimulus), and the conditioning that should have grown the dog's love of the sport never happened. When you shape, the dog makes a choice, earns the reinforcement, and the value builds inside the behavior itself. That is a fundamentally different relationship with work.

## How to Apply

1. **Write your criteria before getting the dog.** What is the finished behavior? What does it include — duration, motion, distance? What does it NOT include? List your anticipated cheap behaviors and decide in advance how you will handle them: ignore, reset, or change the environment to raise the response cost. For example, if I know my dog wants to use their paw when I'm shaping nose behaviors, I might raise the prop so the paw-swipe becomes too much effort.

2. **Break the behavior into small pieces.** If I want to teach a dog to put their face in water and blow bubbles, I am not going to bring out the bowl and wait. I will break it down: nose touch to bowl edge, nose lowering toward water, nose contact with water surface, and so on. Each piece gets shaped, then combined. The order may or may not be linear, but the map needs to make sense in your plan before it can make sense to the dog.

3. **Consider the dog's ideal emotional state for this behavior.** If I'm shaping something that requires calm and stillness — like lying on a side for a nail trim — I'm not going to start the session with a game of tug that has the dog revved up. Likewise, if I need a high-energy, fast-moving response, I'm not going to drain the dog beforehand. Your reinforcement choices feed into this too: a very high-drive food reinforcer in a dog who's already excited may work against you if the behavior requires thoughtfulness.

4. **Plan your reinforcement: rate, value, delivery, and placement.** These are four distinct variables and they all matter. Rate of reinforcement is highest in early sessions — if the dog isn't earning cookies often, your setup isn't creating enough clarity. Value scales with difficulty: I don't always need ultra-high-value food, because most of my dogs are so experienced with working that they get excited about nearly anything — but for something brand new and challenging, I'll go up in value. Delivery matters: does the food roll? Does it land in a useful place? Placement is the most underrated element — reinforcing the dog in the exact position or location you want builds the strength of that behavior far faster than breaking them off somewhere else.

5. **Set a timer. Use push, stick, or drop.** The very first session on any new behavior is an evaluation session: timer for one minute, five cookies. Can you find five things to reinforce before the timer goes off? If not, your antecedent arrangements are not creating enough clarity. Go back to your plan and change something. Your success rate in a session should ideally sit around 60 to 80 percent correct responses. If you're below that, there's not enough structure for the dog to work from.

6. **Film the session and write one line of reflection immediately after.** What was the rate of reinforcement? What was the dog's emotional state before and after? What is one thing you'd do differently? Write your plan for next session before you walk away. Too many people think shaping is about what behavior you choose to reinforce, but the heavy lifting is done in the planning before and in the reflection after.

## Examples

**Situation:** You want to shape your dog to back up, but every attempt ends with the dog sitting, spinning, or staring at you waiting for direction.

**Application:** Before getting the dog, you identify "sit" as the most likely cheap behavior and plan against it by getting down to the dog's level yourself — kneeling or cross-legged — so your posture doesn't invite the dog's head up. You choose a treat that rolls along the floor so the dog moves forward for the reset and recreates the opportunity to shift backward. Your criteria for this evaluation session is any rearward weight shift, however subtle. The reset is a tossed cookie forward.

**Illustrative result:** In the first minute, the dog offers several rearward weight shifts and one full step back, all reinforced. The sit appeared twice; each time you reset without reacting verbally. Rate of reinforcement was high enough to keep the dog engaged and offering. Next session plan: position the dog near a low barrier to increase the response cost of the sit.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Add a verbal cue or name the behavior the moment the dog does it once.
**Why:** Naming a behavior too early captures every messy, incomplete attempt that came alongside the good reps. The cue ends up predicting the whole messy history, not just the clean finished product. Add a cue only when you're confident the dog would produce the correct behavior on the very next attempt — and even then, use props and environment as the cue at first, because dogs often need a lot of reinforcement history before a verbal word alone carries weight.
