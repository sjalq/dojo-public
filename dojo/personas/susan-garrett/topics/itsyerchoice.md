# It's Yer Choice

## When to Use
- As a foundation game with any new dog, puppy, or rescue before adding other layers of training
- When a dog lacks impulse control around food, toys, or environmental distractions
- When building toward a dog that makes good choices independently, whether you're watching or not
- When working with a dog that has been heavily lured and does not know how to offer behavior on their own

## Fails When
- Layers added too quickly — IYC starts as a cookie in your hand; it takes time and many repetitions before it scales to a squirrel at the park
- Dog trained to look at the handler's face instead of the distraction — this misses a critical piece of the game design
- Dog has such a deep history of being lured that it offers nothing at all — you need to kickstart it with the Muffin Tin game first before IYC will gain traction
- Trainer uses "no," "oops," or "try again" when the dog makes a wrong choice — the environment should be designed so the correct choice is the obvious one

## Core Concept

It's Yer Choice starts as a simple game: you have cookies in your hand, your dog is going to want to molest your hand and eat those cookies, and you're teaching them that self-control is what earns access. The dog has to wait to be told. It doesn't matter if you've dropped something on the floor, if there are two or three cookies visible at once — the criteria is clear: you wait to be released. That's the foundation. But here's what's important to understand about how this game grows: the cookie in your hand is not the point. The cookie represents everything. It represents squirrels. It represents other dogs. It represents a piece of meat on the sidewalk. It represents whatever it is that your dog goes absolutely crazy for.

Now, here's the piece that surprises most people, and it's critical: I do not want my dog to look up at my face when they see a distraction. I want them to keep looking at that distraction. My dog Momentum, for example, won't even do that — she'll stay locked on whatever it is. And there's a very good reason for this. If my dog sees a squirrel and looks up at my face, what am I supposed to do with that information? It's like Lassie showing up with those frantic eyes — did Timmy fall down the well? I don't know. I can't read what she's alerting to if she's looking at me. But if she looks at the squirrel and stays there, I know exactly what she's found. And when I need her attention, I simply say her name. She looks up at me, I can reward that, and I can redirect her or give her a release. That's choice-based training. The dog is aware of what's in the environment, stays engaged with it in a controlled way, and checks back in with me when I ask.

This matters even more when we grow the game. A dog trained on IYC properly will stop in front of a piece of dropped meat on the sidewalk and look at it instead of diving in. They'll spot another dog at a distance and hold themselves rather than lunging. They will let you know what's out there in the environment — because they haven't been trained to pretend it doesn't exist. What a dog pretends to ignore when you're watching is often what they bolt after when you're not. Choice-based training creates a dog who genuinely makes good decisions, not one who waits for your permission and falls apart the moment you're distracted.

Good dog training encourages and inspires a dog to make choices. We set up the environment so that the choices the dog makes are the ones we actually want them to make. When you approach training this way, you stop thinking of your dog as stubborn or slow. You start thinking about the design.

## How to Apply

1. **Start with a cookie in your closed fist.** Let the dog sniff, paw, and attempt to get the food. The moment they back off — even a tiny weight shift away, even a pause in the molesting — open your hand and let them have it. You are marking and reinforcing the choice to disengage from the thing they want. Do not say "leave it" or "no." Let the environment teach the rule.

2. **Progress to an open palm.** Cookie sits in your open hand. If the dog moves to take it, close your fist. When they back off, open it again. You are building a dog who can look at something they want without taking it. Multiple cookies, multiple positions, multiple surfaces.

3. **Move to food on the floor.** Cookie placed on the ground. If the dog goes for it, cover it with your foot or hand. When they back off, release with their name and let them have it — or pick it up and give it differently. The point is the release is your decision, not theirs.

4. **Use the name as the attention bridge.** When your dog is looking at the distraction and you want their focus, say their name. When they look up, reward that look. If you want them to go back to what they were looking at, release them to look. You are building an attention response to your dog's name that works even when they're focused on something else.

5. **Add layers slowly.** Start in your kitchen or living room. Then the backyard. Then the front yard where cars go by. Then on a walk where there are dogs. Do not add the next layer until the dog is reliably making the right choice at the current level. A dog who is solid on cookies in the living room is not automatically solid on squirrels at the park. The game has to grow deliberately.

6. **For dogs who offer nothing — start with the Muffin Tin game.** If a dog has been lured their whole life, they will stand there and wait to be shown what to do. They are not stupid. They've just been raised in a world where you get a cookie and put it on their nose and show them. They haven't been allowed to choose. To kickstart this, get a muffin tin with some balls in it. Assess the dog's emotional state first — not sleeping, not over-the-top aroused, somewhere in the engaged middle. Cover all the holes but one and put a cookie in that uncovered hole. The dog sticks their nose in and eats it. Do that two or three times. Now put a cookie in and cover it with a pair of rolled-up socks. The dog has to push the socks to get the cookie. The weight is much less than a tennis ball, so it's easy. Click and reward any attempt to interact. Then move up to a smaller ball, then a tennis ball. You have created an environment where clicking and treating happens constantly, where zero "oops" or "no try again" is needed, because the antecedent arrangements made the correct choice the easy one.

## Examples

**Situation:** Your dog makes good choices at home but loses all self-control at the park when they see another dog.

**Application:** You're not at the park yet. You go back to IYC in the backyard, then the front yard, then the edge of the park with other dogs visible at a great distance. You let the dog look at the dog. You say their name. They look up. You reward. You release them to look again. Over multiple sessions you close the distance, but only when the dog is making the right choice reliably at the current distance.

**Illustrative result:** The dog arrives at the park and spots a dog fifty meters away. They look at it, hold, and look back at you. You reward that check-in and release them to watch the other dog again. The lunge is gone because the dog has a trained behavior for what to do when they see something exciting — look at it, check in when asked.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Train your dog to look at your face instead of the distraction when playing IYC.
**Why:** The entire point of the game is that the dog acknowledges what's out there and makes a choice not to take it. A dog looking at your face is not demonstrating self-control around a distraction — they're avoiding it. When you later generalize to real-world distractions like squirrels or other dogs, you want the dog to notice those things and hold, not pretend they aren't there until their instincts override them. Build the dog who can look at what tempts them and still choose wisely.
