# The MRI Formula

## When to Use
- Dog is doing something the owner wants to stop — counter surfing, jumping, pulling, chewing, reacting on leash
- Owner is in the moment and needs to know exactly what to do right now and what to do next
- Training has been reactive rather than proactive and there's no clear plan
- Owner can name the don't but hasn't clearly defined the do

## Fails When
- The underlying problem is emotional (fear, anxiety, stress) — you cannot train your way through an emotional state; the emotion must be addressed first
- The redirect immediately follows the don't in a way that the dog reads as "doing the don't leads to a cookie" — the redirect has to re-engage without becoming a reward for the don't
- The intensify phase only happens in response to mistakes rather than as a daily proactive investment

## Core Concept

Imagine having the confidence to tackle any dog training challenge that comes your way. To know exactly what to do in the moment and what you should do next. That's what the MRI formula gives you. It's simple. My mentor Bob Bailey always said dog training is simple — it's not always easy, but it's simple. And this formula is both simple and easy to remember. MRI. For us humans, an MRI is a diagnostic tool. This is very much a diagnostic tool as well, just not an imaging one.

M is Manage the don't. R is Redirect the dog. I is Intensify the do. That's it. For every don't there is a do — and possibly many dos. For every thing we want our dog to stop doing, there is a behavior we'd love to see instead. Our job is to stop the current reinforcement, move the dog out of the situation that was reinforcing the don't, and then invest every day in making the do so strong, so reinforcing, so automatic that the don't loses its appeal.

Before you can apply MRI well, you need to know which of the five categories your dog's don't falls into. Category one is stress, anxiety, fear, or overwhelm — that is an emotional component, and it changes the approach entirely because you must work on the emotional state first. Category two is excitement, overarousal, or anticipation for something amazing — that often looks exactly like category one but isn't. Category three is behaviors that happen when puppies are tired, like nipping or destruction. Category four is curiosity — this is a biggie, it covers a lot, and counter surfing almost always starts here. Category five is a previous history of reinforcement for the don't — chasing, chewing, anything the dog has gotten rewarded for doing, even accidentally. Once you know which category you're in, you know how to manage it, how to redirect it, and what the do actually is.

## How to Apply

1. **Manage the don't — stop the reinforcement.** In the moment, your only job is to prevent the dog from getting the payoff for the behavior you don't want. If the dog is getting on the counter, get in, collar grab with a history of positive reinforcement behind it, take the paws off, remove anything they grabbed. No anger. No punishment. Just calmly stop the reinforcement from happening. If the don't is an emotional event like reactivity, your job is to calmly get the dog below threshold, as far away from the trigger as you need to go, before anything else is possible. After any incident, open your journal and write down everything you can about what triggered it and how to prevent that environment from happening again.

2. **Redirect the dog.** Once the don't is managed, immediately redirect focus away from the environment of the don't. Back away, create some distance, let go of the collar. A dog with a solid reinforcement history with you will reorient and re-engage on their own. If they don't, that tells you something important: the reinforcement history between you and your dog isn't strong enough yet, and that's a simple fix — it's going back to recaller games and building more value for you. The redirect does not mean handing the dog a cookie right after the don't. That creates a behavior chain. The redirect is re-engagement and re-focus.

3. **Intensify the do.** This is where training actually happens, and it happens proactively — not just in the five seconds after a mistake. For every don't, list all the dos. For counter surfing, the dos might be It's Your Choice, Hot Zone, Reinforcement Zone in the kitchen, crate. Then invest in those games every single day. When you're intentionally intensifying the do, use all the things that are most reinforcing to your dog — not just cookies, but tug, release to chase, anything that lights them up. The more you make the do worth doing, the less interesting the don't becomes. When you catch early warning signs — the dog air-scenting near the counter before their paws even leave the ground — that's your cue to redirect. You never want to wait for the full don't to happen before you respond.

## Examples

**Situation:** A four-month-old puppy has started getting his front paws on the kitchen counter to investigate what's up there. It started with licking the floor, escalated to licking the dishwasher plates, and now he's at counter height.

**Application:** Manage: get in, collar grab calmly, remove paws, remove any food grabbed. No verbal scolding. Redirect: walk the puppy back to the edge of the kitchen, let go of collar, let re-engagement happen naturally. Log what preceded it. Intensify: play It's Your Choice daily with a cookie bowl at various heights, not just on the floor — this teaches reinforcement can't be stolen. Play Hot Zone so there's a specific place outside the kitchen that the puppy loves to be. Play Reinforcement Zone in the kitchen with nothing attractive on the counter, then later with attractive smells present, reinforcing the puppy for being in correct position rather than air-scenting toward the counter.

**Illustrative result:** Within a few weeks of proactive daily intensification of the do, the puppy is voluntarily moving to the Hot Zone when kitchen prep begins, because that behavior has a rich reinforcement history and the counter has none.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Manage the don't by verbally scolding, popping the leash, or using intimidation.
**Why:** Managing the don't has one job: stop the reinforcement. The moment you add punishment or intimidation, you've changed the emotional landscape for the dog. A dog managed with frustration or fear will have more anxiety around the environment of the don't, not less. You need them calm enough to redirect and to eventually learn the do.

**Don't:** Skip straight to intensifying the do without managing the don't and preventing the reinforcement from continuing.
**Why:** If the don't keeps paying off between training sessions, you're fighting an uphill battle. The environment keeps paying the dog to do the thing you're trying to change. The MRI formula only works when all three phases are active — manage, redirect, and intensify, not just the part that feels like training.
