# Writing for Other Mouths: The Roast Dais, Punch-Up, and the Writers' Room

## When to Use
- You're writing a roast set, a toast, or a monologue someone else will deliver
- You've been hired to "add some edge" to a show or a script
- Your comedy has to serve something else: a trick, a song, a product, a speech
- Someone says your joke sounds like theirs

## Fails When
- **The joke doesn't fit the speaker.** A line that kills in your mouth can die in theirs. Write for their persona and their delivery.
- **The client can't sell it.** Some people need impressions or performers to carry the jokes. Know who's actually reading.
- **It's theft.** Writers' rooms produce the same joke all the time. Lifting someone's bit word for word is a different thing.

## Core Concept

Writing on the Comedy Central Roasts was an honor, because it's a legit job. All of a sudden I have health insurance from literally making fun of people. And then all of a sudden Martha Stewart is saying something I wrote into a teleprompter for her, or Peyton Manning, these heroes that people look up to, saying dirty words that I got them to say. That's the fun of writing for other mouths: the joke lands harder because of who's saying it. Martha Stewart saying it is the joke.

One short writing job taught me how big the brief can be. I took a short gig writing on a pilot for Justin Willman, who's a genius magician. I showed up thinking, I'll punch up whatever: you guys show me the tricks you want to do and I'll write jokes around the trick. And the main guy goes, no. You write the trick and you write the jokes. So whatever my imagination thinks would be a cool magic trick, the magicians figure out how to do it. That's what excited them: they want to hear what a different mind thinks would be impossible. The comedy writer's job wasn't to decorate their act. It was to imagine the impossible thing and make it funny. That changes what you pitch. And the flip side: something that just hurts isn't a joke or a trick. If a doctor would watch it and go, okay, you're pushing something through muscle, it's not an illusion. It's just a guy doing something that hurts. Same with comedy: shock with no idea under it isn't a joke.

When you write for someone with power, write their voice, not yours. I wrote jokes for the President of the United States to make fun of the press at the White House Correspondents' Dinner. When that dinner got postponed, I ran the jokes myself at the Kennedy Center, and I had Adam Ray come out and read them in Trump's voice, seeing them for the first time. That's a test: if the jokes kill in an impression of the speaker, cold, they're written for that mouth.

Punch-up is the same skill at a smaller scale. I punched up a script for Peter Farrelly on an Amazon project. The guy made Kingpin. You're tightening somebody else's thing, not rewriting it into yours.

And about similar jokes: there's only so many notes and so many chords and so much timing. It's the same with comedy. In a writers' room, and I've been in so many of them, so many people writing on the same subject will have the same joke. There's stealing and then there's inspired by. Inspired by is what we're all doing. Lifting somebody's act word for word, long form, that's a buccaneer. That's a totally different thing.

Last, the archive. Go back through your old stuff. When you find an old joke, it's the best feeling. I watched Ron White go back over his material and go, you know what? This shit's pretty goddamn funny. Old material written for a different you can be new material for somebody else's mouth, or for yours.

## How to Apply

1. **Find out who's reading.** Persona, delivery, comfort with filth, how much they'll rehearse. Write to that.
2. **Use the speaker as part of the joke.** The surprise of who is saying it is free leverage. Write lines that are funnier because this person is the one saying them.
3. **Pitch the impossible, then make it funny.** When comedy serves another discipline, don't just caption their act. Imagine the version they couldn't think of.
4. **Test cold in a borrowed voice.** Have someone read the jokes in the speaker's voice without rehearsal. What kills cold is written for them.
5. **Punch up, don't take over.** Tighten their thing. Keep their structure. You're the edge, not the author.
6. **Check stealing vs inspired-by honestly.** Same premise, same subject, different construction: crossover. Same words in the same order: theft.
7. **Mine your archive.** Old jokes can fit new mouths. Go look.

## Examples

**Situation:** A CEO wants three jokes for a company all-hands roasting the leadership team, including herself.

**Application:** Interview people about each leader until you find the obvious-but-unsaid thing (see roast-writing.md). Write each joke so it's funnier because she's the one saying it, and make sure she gets it too, first. Have someone read them in her cadence to check they fit her mouth.

**Illustrative result:** The jokes sound like her on her best day, which is the only kind of ghost-writing that works.

**Situation:** A musician wants comedy bits between songs for a live special.

**Application:** Don't write jokes about the songs. Pitch something they'd never think of doing on stage that the band can actually pull off, then write the comedy around that.

**Illustrative result:** The bits feel like part of the show instead of a comic interrupting a concert.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Write your own act and hand it to someone else.
**Why:** It won't fit their mouth, and they'll deliver it like a hostage.

**Don't:** Treat every similar joke as theft.
**Why:** Rooms full of writers on the same subject land on the same joke. Inspired by is what we're all doing.

**Don't:** Mistake shock for a joke.
**Why:** A stunt that just hurts isn't a trick, and a line that's just offensive isn't a joke. There has to be an idea under it.
