# Build Where the Industry Isn't: The Austin Move

*Context: the Comedy Mothership in Austin is Joe Rogan's club; Kill Tony tapes there. Tony's claim is about the scene and the move, not ownership of the club.*

## When to Use
- You're a comic deciding where to live
- You're in LA or New York and spending more time thinking about other people's deals than writing
- You want to know why so many comics went to Austin
- You're building a room or a scene from scratch

## Fails When
- **Your job is the industry.** Staff writing and auditions still happen where the executives are.
- **There's no stage time for you there.** A great city with no spots for your level is a vacation.
- **You're running from the work.** Moving doesn't write the minute for you.

## Core Concept

I spent the first fifteen years of my career in LA, and I loved it. I always thought I'd grow old in LA and retire to Nashville. I never would have guessed another option would work. Even though I toured Texas more than anywhere else, Dallas three or four times a year. Texas has a big, dirty, renegade, raw sense of humor, and that's right down my wheelhouse.

Then the pandemic. Our show is live; LA shut down live. Austin said: come do it here, bars are open. I went and did a show there in November of 2020, Ron White put me up in his penthouse and rolled out the red carpet, and it started immediately. Everybody thinks Rogan went to Austin and all his homies followed. No. Rogan was the finishing touch. Austin sells itself. The live music, the food, and it was open. That's the most important thing.

The real difference is the industry isn't in Austin. We've watched the complete evolution of stand-up: a clean three minutes for the Tonight Show, a decently clean 22 minutes for a Comedy Central half hour, a Netflix hour where you could be edgier, then Netflix cutting down on edginess and giving notes unless you're really established. In Austin it's Wild West comedy, which is obviously what people want. You go to a strip club, you don't want to see a girl in a bikini.

LA does something to your head, too. On my last trip there I noticed the billboards. I'm looking up going, that show looks like crap, that movie tells its whole script on the billboard, why does that idiot have a show, that's not going to work. The whole place does that to people. All of a sudden I'm daydreaming about other people's deals. In Austin there's none of that, no "I wish I had that" or "this person would be better for that." You're comfortable, you're better rested, maybe you have a new hobby. In LA or New York it's easy to go, I gotta work, I gotta work, it's only about the next joke. But then you're not living, you're not having the great conversations where you say something you realize might be good on stage. Living is a huge part of what we do.

And the scene compounds. The New York joke gunslingers come through, do a weekend, and look at it with a twinkle in their eye: wait, you guys do this every Monday? Comedy Store and Comedy Cellar comics start talking about spending a week out of every month there. The new club was built by real comedians. Every other club, even the ones owned by a former comic, was never done by actual touring comedians who play theaters and arenas to surgically make it. I watched Louie come in and say it'd be even better than perfect if the ceiling was 12 inches lower, and Joe looks at the construction guy and they do it. I think Austin will be number one. I already see it in how casually we sell tickets, and people flying into Austin for a comedy weekend the way they used to fly into LA and New York.

## How to Apply

1. **List what you actually need from the industry.** If it's auditions and writers' rooms, the industry city matters. If it's stage time and an audience, it doesn't.
2. **Go where the rooms are open and the crowd wants your kind of comedy.** A renegade crowd for a renegade act.
3. **Count the stage times.** How many spots a week can you get at your level? That's the number that builds a career.
4. **Watch what the city does to your head.** If you spend your drive judging billboards and other people's shows, that's time not writing.
5. **Live there.** Rest, hobbies, conversations. Material comes from a life.
6. **Build the room you want.** If you're in a position to, let working comics design the club. Little details, a lower ceiling, decide whether the laughs stack.

## Examples

**Situation:** A comic with five years in LA is getting decent spots but spends most of his week stewing over who got which deal.

**Application:** Ask what he needs from LA. If it's the audition calendar, stay and stop reading billboards. If it's stage time and a crowd that wants edgy material, go do a month somewhere that's open and see if he writes more.

**Illustrative result:** Either he comes back with a clearer head or he never comes back. Both are better than the billboard spiral.

**Situation:** A group of comics wants to open a club in a mid-size city.

**Application:** Design it like comedians, not like a bar with a stage. Ceiling height, sightlines, phones locked up, cameras running so you own the footage. Then book killers every night.

**Illustrative result:** Touring comics notice, start dropping in, and the room becomes a destination instead of a gig.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Assume you have to be in LA or New York to make it.
**Why:** The podcast is the way now; you build your own fans wherever the rooms are good.

**Don't:** Move and stop working.
**Why:** The point of the move is more and better stage time and a better head, not a vacation.

**Don't:** Let the industry's notes define your act.
**Why:** Even Netflix started giving notes unless you're really established. The live crowd wants Wild West comedy, and the live crowd is who buys the tickets.
