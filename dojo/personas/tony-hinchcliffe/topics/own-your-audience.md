# Own the Audience: The Podcast, the Live Show, and the Kill Tony Business

## When to Use
- You're deciding whether to pitch a network or just start your own show
- You have a show with a following and want to make it a business
- Someone is offering a deal and you're trying to figure out what it costs you
- You're watching other shows grow off drama and wondering if you should too

## Fails When
- **The show isn't good yet.** Clips, touring, and deals amplify a show. They don't fix one.
- **You'll take any audience.** The audience you grow with is the audience you're stuck with.
- **You confuse independence with refusing every deal.** Partners who understand the show are fine. It's the notes and the drama that cost you.

## Core Concept

We learned that the podcast is the way. You can have your own show and build your own fans, people who are into what you're into and like the sound of your voice and want to hear it. The old model is: all right, let's throw this to the people and see who likes it. That's an old-school model, and it takes away from the ability to build something. People in LA and New York are at their last stand on that. Comedy went from a clean three minutes for the Tonight Show, to a decently clean 22 minutes for a Comedy Central half hour, to a Netflix hour where you could be edgier, and then even Netflix started cutting down on edginess and giving notes unless you're really established. Your own show doesn't have notes.

The live room is the engine. In the pandemic everybody else got to go to their studio and keep doing their show, but ours is in front of a live audience, so we were dying the hottest death. We tried people sending in minute-long clips and streaming the interviews; it was a nightmare. Redban and I needed a crowd, period. So we went where the rooms were open, which turned out to be Austin. Before that we'd already been taking the show on the road for years: Australia, Europe, every major Canadian city, everywhere in America. The live show is what people pay for. And everything stays on YouTube, which is how the clips find the next crowd. It's crazy to think that this thing I've worked on forever is better than ever.

Know which growth to refuse. You'd think Kill Tony would be a big poker in the drama stuff, and I get it, people like drama. But I've always looked at it like: if you play that game, those are the types of fans you're going to get. You want to do drama, you want to do gossip, then you're not even in the comedy world anymore, you're in the drama world. Same with outrage. All the online yelling doesn't convert to sold tickets or a bigger thing for the people doing it. Sold tickets are the scoreboard.

Know which money to refuse, too. I was offered vast sums of money to go do comedy in Saudi Arabia, the kind of money the bus boy, bag boy Tony from Youngstown would never have imagined turning down, and I turned it down. Then a streamer clipped my reaction shot at a roast to make it look like I was the guilty one. Owning the audience means they know who you are better than the clip does.

And then the deals come to you on your terms. Kill Tony now has a subscription tier, Kill Tony+, $5 a month on Supercast, and a distribution and ad-sales deal with Red Seat Ventures. Red Seat Ventures is the perfect partner for our podcast: they understand our brand, our vision, and the importance of capturing and delivering live content to audiences. The difference between that and a network deal in the old model is who's carrying whom.

Leverage is the point. I love the story about the Eagles, who supposedly wanted a piece of the parking too, and if the arena said no, they'd say fine, we'll play the stadium, or the next city over, and your city loses the night. Since they had all the leverage, they'd just fill stadiums, and people gave them what they wanted. That's what owning your audience buys you.

## How to Apply

1. **Start the show yourself.** Don't wait for a gatekeeper to "throw it to the people." Build fans who like your voice.
2. **Make the live room the core product.** Tickets, touring, a recurring room. The recordings and clips are how new people find the room.
3. **Keep everything up.** Old episodes and clips on YouTube are the funnel. The archive works while you sleep.
4. **Refuse drama-driven growth.** Feuds and gossip grow an audience that wants feuds and gossip. You'll be stuck with them.
5. **Use ticket sales as the scoreboard.** Online noise, positive or negative, is not your audience until it buys a ticket.
6. **Take partners, not owners.** Subscriptions and distribution deals are great when the partner understands your brand and doesn't give you notes. Turn down money that changes who you are.
7. **Build leverage before you negotiate.** When you can fill the room without them, you get the parking too.

## Examples

**Situation:** A comic with a strong live following is offered a development deal that requires a cleaned-up pilot.

**Application:** Ask what you'd lose. If the fans came for the uncleaned version, a cleaned pilot sells something your audience never asked for. Keep building the show you own; if the deal is right, it'll come back on terms that don't involve notes.

**Illustrative result:** Either the deal comes back better or you never needed it. Both beat trading your audience for a pilot.

**Situation:** A podcast's numbers jumped after the host started a public feud with another creator. The producer wants more feuds.

**Application:** Look at who arrived. Are they buying tickets and merch, or are they in the comments waiting for the next fight? Those are the types of fans you're going to get. Go back to the comedy.

**Illustrative result:** Growth is slower, but the audience stays for the show instead of leaving when the feud ends.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Measure success by outrage engagement.
**Why:** It doesn't convert to sold tickets. It's a temporary drug.

**Don't:** Let the show live only on someone else's platform.
**Why:** When the gatekeeper changes its mind about edginess, you're gone. Your own feed, your own room, your own fans.

**Don't:** Go remote and assume a live show survives.
**Why:** We tried. We needed a crowd, period. Protect the room first.
