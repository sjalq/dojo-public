# The Post-Set Interview: Squeeze Out More Than They Meant to Give

*Credit where it's due: this is Howard Stern's interview style, adapted. Tony was raised on old-school Stern interviews and says so. What follows is his version of it for a comedy stage, where the guest just did a minute and the panel is armed.*

## When to Use
- You host anything with guests and the guests are giving you their safe, rehearsed answers
- You interview strangers or amateurs live and need eight good minutes out of somebody who just did sixty seconds
- You're deciding, in real time, whether a joke at the guest's expense is landing or wounding

## Fails When
- **You ignore the tells.** The whole method is reading the person. If you push on the thing they don't want to talk about and the laugh isn't genuine, you've stopped doing comedy.
- **You make it about you.** The point is getting more out of them than they planned to give. A host who's just waiting to drop his own joke gets the same flat answers everybody else gets.
- **The guest doesn't want to be there.** This is built for people who signed up to get pulled out of a bucket. They want the shot. That's the leverage.

## Core Concept

I was raised on Howard Stern old-school interviews. He would always ask the right questions at the right time, and he'd get more information than it seemed like he should have out of these people. That's what I'm trying to squeeze out. So sometimes I'll just hit them with: what's the weirdest thing in your refrigerator? What's your biggest fear? What's your family like, were you raised by a single mom? I've asked so many of these questions that I've built up a sort of map, to where I know where things are going and I can already tell before they answer.

The tell is the sweat. You can see the beads of sweat that form on their forehead right after the set, or in the middle of it if things aren't going well. And if the set goes good, you see the beads form during the interview part. You can tell what they do and don't want to talk about. What do you do for a day job? Oh, they don't mind that. What were your parents like? All of a sudden, you see the sweat. So that means the parents are fans, will watch this, they think they're going to see this. So from body language I can see how they're feeling, what to push more on, and what I can get more of.

And there's a calibration built in. If I make fun of them, how do they laugh at it? If it seems genuine, that means I can go even harder. 'Cause I don't want to hurt anybody's feelings, you know what I mean? The laugh is the permission slip. A real laugh from the person you're roasting means the room is with both of you. A fake one means back off and find the next door.

The last piece is time. On my show, if somebody bombs, the interview lasts longer than their set. I'll only keep someone up there if they're super compelling, and if they are, I've spent 15, 20 minutes with somebody. At that point they're just the guest on a podcast. I've had people come back the next week because the interview was that good, and I get nervous for them: I hope they have another minute, I hope this interview goes as good as last time. The interview isn't a consolation prize for a bad set. Sometimes it's the whole reason they're memorable.

## How to Apply

1. **Start with cheap, weird, specific questions.** Weirdest thing in your refrigerator. Biggest fear. Day job. These are low-stakes, nobody prepared for them, and the answers tell you where to dig.
2. **Watch for the sweat.** Ask about family, money, relationships, the thing in their set they skated past. When their body changes, you've found the door.
3. **Push on the door, then check the laugh.** Make the joke. If they laugh for real, go harder. If they don't, change subjects; you'll get there another way.
4. **Let a good interview run and kill a dead one.** The set length is fixed; the interview length is your call. Interesting people get fifteen minutes. Boring people get a quick send-off.
5. **Build your question bank on purpose.** After enough of these, you can tell where an answer's going before it gets there. That's what lets you set up the panel's jokes instead of just reacting.
6. **Give the guest the win.** They came for the shot. A great interview can make someone more memorable than their minute did.

## Examples

**Situation:** A podcaster interviews founders and every episode sounds like a press release.

**Application:** Stop asking about the company first. Ask the refrigerator question. Ask what their parents think they do. Watch which answer makes them shift in the chair, and go there gently, with a joke, and check whether they laugh for real.

**Illustrative result:** The founder tells a story they've never told on a podcast, because nobody asked the question sideways. That's the clip that travels.

**Situation:** A live show host gets a nervous amateur who had a rough minute and is visibly sweating.

**Application:** Don't pile on the set. Ask about the day job, which they won't mind. Find the one thing that's weird about them, the drummer, the ex, the hometown, and make that the bit. If the guest laughs at the first jab for real, you've got a partner.

**Illustrative result:** The audience leaves remembering the person, not the bomb. That's the difference between mean and funny.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Open with the big personal question.
**Why:** You haven't earned it and you haven't read them yet. Cheap weird questions first; they tell you where the sweat is.

**Don't:** Keep roasting someone whose laugh has gone fake.
**Why:** The genuine laugh is the signal that you can go harder. When it's gone, you're not roasting them anymore, you're just hurting them, and the room turns on you.

**Don't:** Present this as your own invention if you teach it.
**Why:** It's Stern's move. I'm pulling from Howard Stern old-school interviews. Adapting it to a guy who just did a minute out of a bucket is the part that's mine.
