# Book Killers: Monsters or the Future, Never a Fear-Driven Lineup

*Attribution: Tony describes the no-fear lineup as something he and Joe Rogan "have always had in common." The booking rule for Kill Tony guests, monsters or the future, is his.*

## When to Use
- You're headlining and choosing who goes up before you
- You're booking guests for a podcast or a live show and wondering whether to go famous or go new
- You've been handed a nightmare follow or a bad slot and want to understand what happened
- You're a newer comic deciding whether to take a spot that puts you after a legend

## Fails When
- **The future isn't ready.** You can introduce someone as the future only if you've watched them destroy rooms. Otherwise you've spent your credibility on a guess.
- **You can't follow your own lineup.** If stacked openers bury you every night, you aren't ready to headline those rooms. That's information, not a reason to book weaker openers.
- **The room isn't built for it.** A slot right after the national anthem in a half-full arena that isn't mic'd or lit for stand-up is a different sport.

## Core Concept

On my show the guests are always either famous monster comedians or the future. Your Brian Simpsons who are just starting to get recognized now. Three years before that it was Shane Gillis. Five years before that it was Tim Dillon. And I would literally say, with no hesitation: your guest tonight is the future. You might not know him now, but one day he will be recognized as one of the biggest comedians of all time. And the crowd would go, aw, I was hoping for somebody famous. They don't know yet. What's funny is they really don't want the famous name for its own sake. They want people who are in the moment, with their own defined styles, who are still there to prove themselves. Not just some guy that's like, hey, I think you did good.

The same thing applies to who goes up before you. Sometimes you'll go see some of the biggest comedians on planet Earth and you'll notice their opening acts aren't their own killers. That's fear. Joe and I have always had that in common: we thrive on giving the people the best possible crazy-ass show and pulling it off. It's not fear driven. Who else takes Joey Diaz to feature for him in an arena, and Ari Shaffir, and Duncan, all these wild people? Somebody who wants to get better and help the people he thinks are the best. I've watched him follow Chappelle in an arena like butter. I've gone up before and after Theo Von for over a decade; I was never like, oh, I hope I can follow him.

And I know what a real follow feels like. I had to follow Seinfeld at the Comedy Store the night he came back after 35 years and told the whole story about the owner telling him he wasn't funny. Toughest follow I ever had in my entire life. He buried me with a shovel. Seventy percent of the room left, the other thirty were texting their families. The only laugh I got was off a fruit fly that flew through the lights: even the fruit fly is getting out of here. You survive that, and you learn that a stacked lineup makes you better. The bad follows teach you; the soft lineups don't.

Slot matters too. At the Madison Square Garden rally I went up at 3 PM, right after the national anthem, on a 34-person lineup, in a half-filled arena waiting five hours for the main speaker, in a place that isn't mic'd for stand-up or lit for stand-up. It obviously wasn't the best position for me on that lineup. It worked in the house anyway, but know what you're walking into.

## How to Apply

1. **Book in two buckets.** Monsters (proven, famous, guaranteed to destroy) and the future (people you've personally watched kill who aren't known yet). Nothing in between.
2. **Vouch out loud for the future.** Tell the crowd with no hesitation that this person is the future. It frames their set and puts your name on the line, which is why you only do it when you mean it.
3. **Book openers who could headline.** If your openers are weak so you look strong, the crowd's night is worse and you never get better.
4. **Treat a brutal follow as training.** When a legend buries you, note what they did: Seinfeld told the room the whole story so even people who didn't know how big the moment was understood it. Then find your one laugh and get off.
5. **Read the slot before you accept it.** Who's before you, what's the room for, is it lit and mic'd for comedy, how long has the crowd been waiting? If it's wrong, adjust the material or adjust your expectations.

## Examples

**Situation:** A headliner going out on a theater tour is choosing between a funny friend who's safe and a newer comic who's been destroying every open mic.

**Application:** Take the killer. If the newer comic outshines you some nights, that's the tour making you better and giving the crowd a better night.

**Illustrative result:** The crowd talks about the whole show, the opener builds a following off your tour, and you've earned a reputation as someone who brings killers.

**Situation:** A podcaster can either book a mid-level celebrity who'll give a bland interview or a young comic nobody knows yet who's hilarious live.

**Application:** Book the comic and introduce them as the future. A bland famous person is the "hey, I think you did good" guest. The audience wants somebody still there to prove themselves.

**Illustrative result:** If you were right, you get to say you had them first, and that's a brand.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Pad your lineup with weak acts so you look better.
**Why:** It's fear-driven, the crowd's night is worse, and you never learn to follow anyone.

**Don't:** Call someone the future as a favor.
**Why:** The line only works because people learned you're right about it. Spend it on a favor once and it's worthless.

**Don't:** Blame the room for a bad slot you didn't read.
**Why:** The slot, the lighting, the sound, and who went before you were knowable. Ask before you go up.
