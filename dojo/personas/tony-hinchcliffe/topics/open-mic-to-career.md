# From Open Mic to Career: Start in the Room You're Not Ready For

## When to Use
- Someone wants to start stand-up and is "waiting until they're ready"
- A beginner bombed and wants to quit, or got buried by a better act
- An early-career comic is deciding where to spend their nights
- Someone asks how anyone gets good at this

## Fails When
- **They want a shortcut.** There isn't one. There's stage time and what you learn from it.
- **They only want soft rooms.** Friendly rooms feel good and teach you less.
- **They're building an online presence with no act.** Clips are the trailer. The movie is the set.

## Core Concept

I almost started at some little club in North Hollywood. I was procrastinating, and I told a guy I'd met at a Starbucks in Burbank, who happened to be Shia LaBeouf's dad, that I was going to start at one of those places. He goes, you shouldn't do that. You should start at the Comedy Store. Let them see you start, sink or swim. When you think you're ready for there, you're not going to be ready for there either, so you might as well not be ready for there now. Great advice, and it's his, not mine. I signed up, got number nine out of fifteen, watched the first eight people I'd ever seen do stand-up, and they were all bombing. I'm like, I think I got this. People sucking helps in the beginning.

My headspace was: I'm already winning. I'm happy to be in LA, I'm happy to be at the dance. No matter how this goes it's going to be great, plus I'm going to get better. This is not going to be this bad again. I'd been in fights with bigger kids my whole life growing up; I used words as a defense mechanism. All my teachers said making fun of people is never going to get you anywhere. I wanted to prove them wrong. I think you can get good at anything.

The open mic itself always boggled my mind, because it's free. I was paying for college with loans for a minute; I dropped out fast. Then I realized you can just do these open mics, and it's like free college, except it's for one specific career. And at a place like the Store you're surrounded by sixteen headliners a night working on their stuff. Road clubs don't run like that. If you're lucky you work a place with one headliner who walks straight into the green room and leaves right after. Pick the room where the best people are, even if they bury you. I've been buried with a shovel by Seinfeld. That's tuition.

Then it's work and living. The guys who came up on my show write and perform a new minute every single week, for years. That's the gym. But the other half is living: in LA and New York it's easy to just go, I gotta work, I gotta work, it's only about the next joke. Then you're not living. You're not experiencing things, not having great conversations where you say something you realize might be good on stage. Living is a huge part of what we do.

And the jobs along the way count. Writing on the Comedy Central Roasts was a legit job: all of a sudden I had health insurance from literally making fun of people. That's not selling out; that's getting paid to practice.

## How to Apply

1. **Go up now, in the room that scares you.** You won't be ready for it later either. Sink or swim where the good comics are.
2. **Watch the people before you.** Bad sets show you the floor; great sets show you the ceiling. Both teach you.
3. **Treat open mics like free college.** Show up multiple nights a week. The tuition is bombing.
4. **Expect the honest set to beat the rehearsed one, early.** If you blank, say so. Truth pops a room.
5. **Survive the bad follows.** When someone buries you, find one real laugh and get off. Then go back the next night.
6. **Write a new minute every week.** That's the discipline of the regulars on my show, and it's the gym.
7. **Live a life.** Take note of what happens to you. Have conversations. The material comes from being out there, not from staring at a notebook.
8. **Take the comedy jobs.** Writing for roasts, punch-up, writers' rooms. They pay the bills and sharpen the pen.

## Examples

**Situation:** A 28-year-old has written jokes for a year and wants to "polish them" before ever going up.

**Application:** Sign up this week, at the best open mic in town, not the easiest. The jokes aren't going to be ready; neither is she. Get the first set over with so the second one can be better.

**Illustrative result:** She learns more in one bad set than in the year of writing, and the jokes that survive are the ones that worked in a room.

**Situation:** A comic with two years in is discouraged because he went up after a famous headliner and got nothing.

**Application:** Tell him about following Seinfeld: 70% of the room left, and the only laugh was a fruit fly line. That's the job. Note what the headliner did that worked, go back to the open mic tomorrow, and keep writing a new minute every week.

**Illustrative result:** The bad night becomes a story he tells later instead of the reason he quit.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Wait until you're ready.
**Why:** When you think you're ready for the big room, you won't be. Might as well not be ready now and start learning.

**Don't:** Grind with no life.
**Why:** It's only about the next joke, until you have nothing to talk about. Living is half the job.

**Don't:** Turn down paid comedy-adjacent work out of pride.
**Why:** Writing for roasts gave me health insurance from making fun of people. That's the career working.
