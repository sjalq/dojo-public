# The Kill Tony Format: Bucket Pull, One Minute, Then the Interview

## When to Use
- You're building a live comedy show, a live podcast, or an open mic and want it to be watchable by people who weren't in the room
- Your show has a slow, predictable middle and you want stakes in every segment
- You run a weekly show and you, the host, are getting bored of it (the audience will be next)
- You want a format that discovers new talent instead of only showcasing known names

## Fails When
- **The host can't carry dead air.** A random name out of a bucket is sometimes a disaster. The host and panel have to turn a bad minute into a good eight minutes. If nobody on the stage can riff, the bombs just sit there.
- **There's no crowd.** We tried running it remotely in the pandemic: people sent in minute-long clips, we livestreamed the interviews, everybody's internet sucked, people frozen on the screen. No laughter. It was dying the slowest death. The format is a live-room format.
- **You need every act to be good.** If the business requires a guaranteed-quality lineup, this isn't that. Half the fun is the bad ones.

## Core Concept

It's a crazy comedy show where comedians watch newer or older comedians do one minute. They sign up for the chance to get one minute uninterrupted in front of a bunch of fans on the internet. Names go in a bucket, I pull a name out, they get a minute. Then I interview them afterwards, and my guest comedians dose in jokes and contribute, and we find out more about the person. So not only do they win a minute of uninterrupted stand-up, all of a sudden they're on a podcast for like eight more minutes where I grill them about their life, make jokes about their set, and try to figure out what's different about them.

Every piece of that does a job. The bucket means nobody, including me, knows who's next, so the room can't settle. The minute is short enough that a bad act is survivable and long enough that a good one can really pop; luckily they're only doing 60 seconds, and that mix is what makes it fun. The interview is the second half of the bit: if they bomb, the interview lasts longer than their set. If somebody's super interesting I've spent 15, 20 minutes with them, and then they're just the guest on a podcast. And the rules are part of the show. Everybody knows you're not supposed to say anything after the bucket pull comes up; that's the moment the next person owns.

Here's the part people miss: the audience comes for both outcomes. On the road I do this poll. I'll go, how many of you like it when comedians do good on this show? And the crowd goes woo. Then: how many of you like it when comedians do bad? And the place goes insane. The cooler the city, the louder that roar. After a taping once, five different people told me they couldn't believe I get to be mean to people for two hours every week for a living, and five totally different people told me they couldn't believe I created a format to help people and I'm the only one giving these random people a shot to be seen. Same show. They're seeing two different shows, and the format serves both of them.

The other thing is that it has to stay interesting for me. I've been there every episode of Kill Tony, over 600 of them before we even hit our ten-year anniversary. The secret sauce is I try to keep it interesting for myself, and the rest is pro wrestling: surprise guests, storylines, a band that a drummer from the crowd can challenge for his chair (it's never happened that the resident drummer got beaten, ever). And a golden ticket: if you kill so hard and your interview is so great that you get to show up at any Kill Tony, anywhere, and do a minute. That's rare on purpose; there have only been about ten in ten years.

## How to Apply

1. **Make the draw random and visible.** Names in a bucket, pulled on stage. Randomness is the stakes. The second you hand-pick the lineup you've built a showcase, which is a different and more boring product.
2. **Fix the set length short and protect it.** One minute, uninterrupted. Nobody talks over it. The constraint is what makes beginners watchable and makes the good ones look like killers.
3. **Make the interview the real segment.** The set is the audition; the interview is the show. Budget more time for the interview than the set, and let it run long when the person is compelling. Cut it short when they aren't.
4. **Put working comics on the panel.** Their jokes rescue the bombs and their reactions validate the hits. The guest is either a famous monster comedian or the future (see booking-and-lineups.md).
5. **Build recurring stakes.** A band seat that can be challenged. Regulars who write and perform a new minute every single week. A rare reward like the golden ticket. Things the audience can root for over months.
6. **Have house rules and enforce them on stage.** "Nobody says anything after the bucket pull" is a rule, and breaking it on camera is itself a bit.
7. **Run it live, in a room.** If you can't have a crowd, you don't have this show. Plan your business around keeping a room.

## Examples

**Situation:** A comedy club owner wants to turn a dead Tuesday open mic into a filmed show.

**Application:** Don't film the open mic as-is; nobody wants to watch twelve people do five minutes each. Cut the sets to one minute, draw names from a bucket on stage, seat two working comics and a host at a desk, and interview every comic after their minute. Run the poll on night one so the crowd learns it's allowed to enjoy the bombs.

**Illustrative result:** The bad acts stop being dead time and start being material. The good ones get a clip that actually shows what they can do in sixty seconds, plus a personality reveal in the interview.

**Situation:** A host on episode 300 of a live show admits he's on autopilot.

**Application:** That's the most important warning sign. Add something that surprises you: an unannounced guest, a recurring challenge, a reward that's rare enough to mean something. If you're bored, the room can feel it before you can.

**Illustrative result:** The host has a reason to show up curious, and curiosity is what makes the interviews good.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Let the set length creep ("just give them three minutes so they can get into it").
**Why:** Three minutes of a beginner is forever. One minute is enough for the good ones to pop and short enough that the bad ones are fun. Sometimes a minute is way too long for some of these people. That's the point.

**Don't:** Pre-screen the bucket for quality.
**Why:** The format works because anybody can get pulled. Screened lineups lose the danger and the discovery, and the regulars and golden-ticket winners mostly came out of that randomness.

**Don't:** Go remote and assume the format survives.
**Why:** We tried it. Clips sent in, interviews on video, no laughter. I feed off the room, and so does the format. Without a crowd it's just a clunky call.
