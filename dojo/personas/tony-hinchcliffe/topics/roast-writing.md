# Roast Writing: Authentic, Original, and Everybody Gets It

*Context: the roast is an old institution (the Friars Club tradition, then the televised Comedy Central Roasts). Tony didn't invent the form; he wrote on the Comedy Central Roasts and has performed on Netflix roasts. What's below is how he writes and runs a roast set.*

## When to Use
- You're writing jokes about a specific person who'll be sitting there
- You're on a dais with other comics and a hard time limit
- You need to decide what's in bounds at this roast, in this room
- A roast joke blew up after the fact and you're trying to understand why

## Fails When
- **It's the wrong room.** There's a time and a place for jokes like that, and a roast is the time and the place. A bachelor party might be; a company all-hands is not.
- **You didn't do the research.** Generic insults ("you're old, you're fat") are what you write when you don't know the person. The crowd can smell it.
- **Only one side gets hit.** A set that only punches one direction reads like an agenda, and then the people who are offended have a point.

## Core Concept

The goal of a roast is that you want to make everyone laugh, including your victim. That's the whole standard. If the person in the chair isn't laughing, you wrote insults, not roast jokes.

What makes a roast joke land is a hard combination: it has to be both authentic and original. You want to hit them with something they've never heard about themselves, but also something that's really obvious once you've said it, that was hiding there on the surface. If it's authentic but not original, it's a joke they've heard a thousand times and they've got a face ready for it. If it's original but not authentic, it's a random insult that doesn't stick to them. Both at once is the laugh where the whole room goes, oh my god, that's exactly right.

How you get there depends on the time you have. When I get to prepare, I do a bunch of research and look at it like it's a math problem. Everything about the person goes on the table and you work it until the obvious-but-never-said thing falls out. If I don't have much time and they're right in front of me, I'll go for something about their appearance, something obvious for people to pick up on, because the room can see it too and gets there with you. So much of it depends on the person: how well-known they are, how long you've got, there are lots of variables. If they're famous, there's more to make fun of. Some people are just hard to attack; when someone's done great work and he's undeniably cool, you have to work harder for the angle.

Then there's the set, not just the jokes. On the Kevin Hart roast there were so many people on it and it was such a long, big roast that they set you to an allotted time. So I did something I don't normally do: I blasted off more jokes per minute than I normally do. Instead of milking it and getting applause breaks, I created a bang-bang-bang-bang final stand of jokes. Know your time and pick your tempo for it.

And everybody gets it. That's always my formula. I have something that's supposed to offend everybody. I don't want you to like 100% of any of my jokes; I'm not that guy, I'm a heel. When people got offended by one joke at the end of my set and pointed at somebody else's joke from the other side of the spectrum, what they didn't mention is that I did that same target in my set too. If everybody gets it, nobody can say you had an agenda. You had jokes.

Last thing: the clip of your dismount is not your set. If you just watch the clip of my last joke, it's a crazy clip. If you watch the entire flow of the set, it's just one last departing joke after standing ovations halfway through. Write the set to work as a set, and know that the internet will only ever show the last line.

## How to Apply

1. **Do the math problem.** List everything about the target: job, history, habits, relationships, how they talk, what everyone privately says about them. You're hunting the thing that's obvious once said and that nobody has said to their face.
2. **Test each joke for both halves.** Authentic: is it true about them? Original: have they heard it before? Cut anything that fails either.
3. **Write the fast version too.** For a no-prep roast or a heckler, go to what's visible: appearance, what they're wearing, how they're sitting. The room sees it with you.
4. **Scale to fame.** Famous targets have public material; private targets need inside information. Interview the people who know them (see post-set-interview.md for how to get the good stuff).
5. **Choose your tempo to your time slot.** Long slot: milk it, let applause breaks happen. Tight slot on a long show: more jokes per minute, no waiting.
6. **Make sure everybody gets it.** Hit the dais, hit the room, hit the other side's sacred cows too. Balance is your defense.
7. **Build the dismount knowing it will be clipped.** Your last joke is the one that travels. Make sure it's one you'll stand behind when it's stripped of the 40 jokes before it (see never-apologize.md).

## Examples

**Situation:** Someone's writing a roast for a friend's 40th birthday: he's a dentist, divorced twice, just bought an expensive electric car.

**Application:** Do the math. Dentist: years of school and still not "a real doctor" in everyone's teasing. Two divorces: two different women got to know him and left. Electric car: the one thing in his life that drives itself. Then find the authentic angle only his friends know, the thing that's been hiding on the surface. Hit the friends in the room too, and the new girlfriend, so it's a party and not an ambush.

**Illustrative result:** The birthday guy laughs hardest at the one about him that nobody has ever said out loud. That's the authentic-and-original joke.

**Situation:** A comic gets a four-minute slot on a twelve-person roast and has 20 minutes of material.

**Application:** Don't try to milk four minutes like it's fifteen. Cut to the best eight or ten, run them fast, and save one you'll stand behind for the dismount.

**Illustrative result:** The set reads as a barrage instead of a series of pauses waiting for applause that the long show doesn't have time to give.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Write generic insults and swap in a name.
**Why:** They're authentic to nobody. You want something they've never heard about themselves that's obvious once you've said it.

**Don't:** Spare one side.
**Why:** Everybody gets it is the formula. The moment one group is exempt, your set has a politics, and the offended people have a case.

**Don't:** Judge your set by the clip.
**Why:** The clip is the dismount, not the flow. If it worked in the room with the target laughing, the set did its job.
