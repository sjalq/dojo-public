# Going Off Script: Premises, Riffs, and the Tags That Protect You

## When to Use
- You're stiff on stage and your memorized jokes feel dead
- You have premises with no endings and want to know whether to try them live
- You're cutting a long bit down for a short, high-profile spot
- An ad-libbed line went somewhere you didn't intend and you want to understand why

## Fails When
- **There's nothing underneath.** You can go off script because there's a script. A comic with no tested act who "just riffs" is improvising a bomb.
- **The ad-lib lands in the wrong room.** A filler line on a political stage gets clipped and stripped. On my show the room knows the context; on someone else's stage it doesn't.
- **You never finish anything.** A premise you've riffed for six months without an ending is a habit, not a bit.

## Core Concept

My first open mic, I'd prepared for months, blanked on all of it, and just told the truth. That went really well. Then for months after, when I remembered my jokes, those were the bad ones. Which is pretty foreshadowing for people who know my comedy style, 'cause I like going off script. I like playing and being in the moment, trusting it. It's sort of like jumping out of a plane without a parachute. I like that dangerous part, where maybe I'll go say something I thought of on the drive to the club that night that's not finished, it's just a premise, and you have a race to finish the joke. You're like, maybe I'll think of it, got a couple seconds left, almost to the end of the sentence, and then. I like to flip it around in my hands a couple of times before throwing it. Catch it behind my back once and see what happens.

That's also why I'm a bad podcast guest and a great live one. Sit me down for an interview and I shut down after 20, 30 minutes. Put me on a stage with two microphones and a crowd and I could riff for hours. I feed off the laughter. The crowd is the other half of the riff.

Now the warning, because it cost me. The Madison Square Garden line wasn't supposed to be in my original thing. It was a last-second filler because they gave me more time than everybody. And the joke itself wasn't new: I'd been doing it since the pandemic, as part of a much bigger chunk, with tags on it. What's funny is I left those tags of the longer joke out of the rally version, which probably would have protected me. People don't realize that was a small bit of a much, much bigger chunk. The tags are what tell the room how to take the line. Strip them off and drop the line on a stage that isn't a comedy stage, and the clip goes out without its armor.

Same thing with what ends up being the hit. Much like the crazy jokes that end up getting me in trouble, you never know which one's going to be the one that flies. So assume any line can be the one that travels alone.

## How to Apply

1. **Build an act first.** Riffing works off a base of tested material. Know your minutes cold, so you can leave them and come back.
2. **Bring one unfinished premise a night.** Something from the drive to the club. Put it in the middle of a set where a stumble won't kill you, and race to the end of the sentence.
3. **Keep what worked.** The riff that landed is tomorrow's bit. Write it down after the show and start tightening.
4. **Use the crowd as fuel.** Riffing is a conversation with laughter. If the room's dead, go back to material; don't drown alone.
5. **Know your tags.** For every joke that could be misread, know which tag tells the room it's a joke. When you shorten a bit for a special spot, keep the tags, not just the punch.
6. **Read the stage before ad-libbing.** Is it lit and mic'd for comedy? Is the crowd there for comedy? On a non-comedy stage, the filler line is the one that gets clipped.
7. **Assume any line can travel alone.** If you'd be fine defending it stripped of context, say it. If you wouldn't, keep its tags on.

## Examples

**Situation:** A comic is great in the green room but stiff on stage, word-perfect and lifeless.

**Application:** Have him bring one premise from the drive each night, unfinished, and drop it in the middle of his set. Let him race to the end of the sentence. Even when it misses, the crowd can feel that something live is happening, and the stiff parts loosen up around it.

**Illustrative result:** Over a few weeks the riffs that landed become new material, and his tested jokes start sounding like he just thought of them.

**Situation:** A comic has a ten-minute chunk and gets offered three minutes at a charity gala that isn't a comedy crowd.

**Application:** Don't just cut to the three hardest punchlines. Keep the setups and the tags that frame the edgy parts. Gala rooms aren't comedy rooms; the tags are what let them in on the joke.

**Illustrative result:** The short version plays as a joke instead of as a quote, and nothing gets clipped into a headline.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Fill extra time with your sharpest line minus its context.
**Why:** That's exactly what happened at MSG. The filler was the line without its tags, on a stage that wasn't built for comedy.

**Don't:** Over-rehearse yourself into a robot.
**Why:** For me the bad sets were the ones where I remembered every joke. The danger is part of the show.

**Don't:** Riff forever without writing.
**Why:** A premise that never gets an ending is a crutch. The race to finish the joke is supposed to end with a finished joke.
