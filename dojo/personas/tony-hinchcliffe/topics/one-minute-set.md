# Winning a One-Minute Shot

## When to Use
- You've got a minute (or three, or five) in front of a crowd that matters
- It's somebody's first time ever and they want notes
- You're a regular on a short-spot show and need a fresh minute every week
- You're deciding between your safest jokes and something that's actually you

## Fails When
- **There are no reps behind it.** A great minute is a minute that's been through a lot of rooms. Notes on top of no reps are decoration.
- **The notes make you cleaner and blander.** The whole value of a room like mine is that there are no network notes. If you walk away from feedback sounding like everyone else, you took the wrong note.
- **It's an hour.** An hour has to breathe and build. A minute has to hit.

## Core Concept

A minute uninterrupted is a real gift. Unlike the Tonight Show or America's Got Talent or Last Comic Standing, on a show like mine there's no notes going in. You don't have to be clean. It's just about being funny. The people who push the limits are the ones who break through, because that's what comedians know people want to see: they want to go have fun, they want a naughty night. Nobody drove four hours to hear your social values in sixty seconds.

When my mom did Kill Tony, she'd never done stand-up in her life, and she murdered. She listened to my few notes. I told her two things: smile, enjoy it, don't forget to smile, and leave spaces in between the jokes. Those were the two things. That's the whole beginner package. Smiling tells the crowd you're having fun, so they're allowed to. Spaces let the laugh happen; first-timers rush, and they step on their own laughs because silence scares them.

The other lesson is from my own first open mic. I'd prepared for months, got up, and blanked on everything. So I just told the truth: I've never done this before, I prepared for months, and I can't remember one single thing I wanted to say. And there was a pop in the room, because the eight people before me were all doing jokey-jokey jokes and I was the first honest thing they'd heard all night. That went great, and then the ones after it, for months, when I remembered my jokes, were the bad ones. Being yourself out loud beats being a joke machine.

Roseanne said it better than anybody on our show once, so I'll give her the credit: even to the people who were doing good, she was telling them, yeah, you could do good doing jokes like that, but you could do even better by looking within yourself. If you're doing that well being that surface-level, what do you really have inside? She had a crazy career by just being herself and writing the story only she can tell.

And then there's the job of keeping it going. Our regulars write and perform a new minute every single week. William Montgomery did it for years straight. I am enamored by their courage and their work ethic, because the whole internet is there going, eh, I'm sort of sick of this guy. That's what a shot turns into if you treat it like a job: a weekly minute, every week, in front of people who are tired of you, until you're undeniable.

## How to Apply

1. **Bring your best tested minute, not your newest idea.** A shot isn't the place for an untested premise unless you're already good enough to land a riff (see riffing-without-a-net.md).
2. **Smile.** You are allowed to be having fun. The crowd takes their cue from you.
3. **Leave spaces between the jokes.** Let each laugh finish. Don't step on it with the next setup.
4. **Get personal.** The thing only you can say beats the thing anybody could say. Look within yourself, as Roseanne puts it.
5. **Don't preach.** A minute is for laughs. If the minute is a position statement, it's not a minute of comedy.
6. **Prepare for the second half.** On a show with an interview, have a real answer for your day job, your family, the weirdest thing in your fridge. The interview is where people decide whether they want to see you again.
7. **If it's weekly, write weekly.** A new minute every week is the gym. Do the reps even when the crowd's getting sick of you.

## Examples

**Situation:** A comic with eight months of open mics just got a one-minute spot on a filmed show.

**Application:** Pick the minute that's killed the most in the worst rooms. Cut every word that isn't setup or punch. Run it with a timer. Then practice pausing after each punchline long enough for a laugh you're not sure you'll get. And decide in advance what you'll say when the host asks what you do for a living, because he will.

**Illustrative result:** Even if one joke dies, the spaces make the others land harder, and the interview gives the crowd a person to remember.

**Situation:** A friend who's never performed wants to do a charity comedy night and asks for advice.

**Application:** Two notes. Smile, enjoy it, don't forget to smile. And leave spaces in between the jokes. Then tell them to have fun.

**Illustrative result:** Beginners who follow those two notes look like they belong up there, which is most of the battle.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Rush to fill silence.
**Why:** The space after the punchline is where the laugh lives. Beginners panic in the quiet and stomp on their own best joke.

**Don't:** Use your minute to tell the crowd what you believe.
**Why:** They came for a naughty night. Social-values comedy in sixty seconds reads as a lecture with no time to earn it.

**Don't:** Recycle the same minute on a weekly show.
**Why:** The regulars who make it write a new minute every single week. The crowd remembers, and the internet remembers even better.
