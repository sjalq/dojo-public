# Run the Show Like Pro Wrestling: Surprise, Storyline, and the Heel

*Credit: Tony names the source himself. "The other part is Vince McMahon. Like, it's really just pro wrestling." This is the pro wrestling booking tradition applied to live comedy; the comedy application is his.*

## When to Use
- Your show is good but nobody talks about it the next day
- You announce your whole lineup in advance and the crowd shows up already knowing everything
- You're a performer whose material offends somebody every time and you need a frame for it
- You want recurring bits and characters that build across episodes

## Fails When
- **There's no delivery behind the character.** A heel works because he can actually wrestle. A comic who leans into being the bad guy has to be funny, or it's just a guy being a dick.
- **The surprise is a dud.** If the crowd hushes for the reveal and it's somebody they don't care about and won't care about, you've spent trust.
- **Storyline turns into drama.** Feuds and gossip about real people's personal lives pull you out of the comedy world. Those are the types of fans you're going to get.

## Core Concept

I'm old school. I was raised on pro wrestling; I didn't have a dad in my life growing up, so that's what I had. And one of the big parts of pro wrestling that makes it wildly successful is the element of surprise, which I love more than anything, combining that with our weird world of stand-up comedy. I literally never announce who the guest for the live show is going to be. Everybody thinks one person is coming out and instead it's somebody else, from that city, a legend, in the room. The pop, the people looking at the person they came with like, no way. It's the Undertaker: the lights go out, you hear the bell toll, and you get the chills because you're like, no way.

The secret sauce of Kill Tony is that I try to keep it interesting for myself, and the other part is Vince McMahon. I always keep our guests a secret surprise. No one ever knows who's going to be there. That's how you make a weekly show feel like a pay-per-view.

The second wrestling idea is the heel. I'm heelish. I'm a bad-guy wrestling fan. I don't want you to like 100% of any of my jokes; I have something that's supposed to offend everybody. That's not an accident or a flaw I'm managing, it's the character. A babyface needs you to like everything. A heel needs you to react. When I see a fighter like Colby Covington do the pro-Trump super bad guy character for real in the UFC, I've been pitching for years on our wrestling podcast that WWE was missing that exact character. As Joe said about him, you can't call him a joke, because he smashes people in the cage. That's the rule: the heel has to be able to go.

Third: storyline. When the UFC is at its best, it's like WWE. It feels raw and real, but also storyline. The best moments on my show are like that: right after the first 2024 debate I booked Shane Gillis as Trump and Adam Ray as Biden, I'm pulling names out of the bucket in real time, and whatever comes out of the bucket becomes part of the story. Nothing is scripted, but everything is framed.

## How to Apply

1. **Stop announcing everything.** Keep at least one guest unannounced. Let the crowd learn that coming in person is the only way to see what happens.
2. **Engineer the reveal.** Who brings them up? Who does the crowd think is coming? A hometown legend in their hometown is a bigger pop than a bigger name with no connection.
3. **Pick your alignment.** Are you the babyface who needs everyone to like you, or the heel who needs everyone to react? Commit. The heel can't apologize for being the heel.
4. **Back up the character.** The heel persona buys you latitude only if the jokes are good. Without the delivery, it's just unpleasant.
5. **Build running stakes.** A challengeable seat in the band, a rare golden ticket, regulars with ongoing bits. Things that pay off over months.
6. **Frame the chaos.** Unscripted moments become storyline when you, the host, name what's happening as it happens. When Shane's Trump asked how many more guys were coming out of the bucket, "Anything can happen" was the frame.
7. **Keep the feuds on stage.** Characters and rivalries inside the show are wrestling. Real-world gossip about real relationships is a different business.

## Examples

**Situation:** A comic runs a monthly show and posts the full lineup a month early. Attendance is fine; buzz is zero.

**Application:** Post three names and "plus a special guest." Book one surprise per show and tell no one. Have a recurring bit that pays off for regulars. Make the host the guy who knows something the crowd doesn't.

**Illustrative result:** The people who were there tell the people who weren't, and next month the "special guest" line sells tickets on its own.

**Situation:** A comedian keeps getting complaints that his material offends people and wonders if he should soften it.

**Application:** Decide what character you are. If you're the heel, the reaction is the job; the move is to make sure everybody gets it so it's a character and not an agenda (see roast-writing.md), and to make sure the jokes are strong enough to justify the heat.

**Illustrative result:** The audience that's actually yours finds you faster, because you stopped trying to be liked by 100% of people.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Burn your surprise on someone the crowd doesn't care about.
**Why:** The hush before a reveal is trust. Spend it on monsters, or on the future with a promise you can keep (see booking-and-lineups.md).

**Don't:** Play the heel and then apologize for it.
**Why:** It breaks the character and tells the crowd the heat was real. See never-apologize.md.

**Don't:** Confuse storyline with drama.
**Why:** You want to be in the comedy world. Once you're doing gossip, you're in the drama world, and those are the fans you'll get.
