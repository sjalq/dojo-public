# Sacrifice and the Delay of Gratification

## When to Use
- The user wants something big and is unwilling to give up anything small
- A team keeps taking shortcuts that pay off this quarter and cost next year
- Someone asks what maturity actually is
- A user asks what *We Who Wrestle with God* is about

## Fails When
- **The sacrifice is someone else's.** The utopian always has a plan that requires others to give things up. The moral move is the sacrifice of the self, voluntarily.
- **It's sacrifice without an aim.** Giving things up for nothing is just deprivation. You sacrifice the lower to the higher, so you need to know what the higher is.

## Core Concept

The basis of maturity and community is sacrifice. That's the thesis I've been developing in *We Who Wrestle with God*, and it's simple to state. Human beings discovered the future. Once you know there's a tomorrow and a next year, and other people who'll be there with you, you can't just do what you want right now. A bad habit is something that works now and not so well tomorrow, or next week, or five years from now. Maturity is the ability to share and to delay gratification, which means you don't get what you want right now, all the time.

Here's the key. There's no difference between work and sacrifice. What do you think you're doing when you work? You're sacrificing the present to the future, or your immediate wants to your family and community. And we discovered that you can bargain with the future that way. Money is like that; money is a bargain you make with the future. The whole biblical library can be read as an extended study in the intricacies of sacrifice, from Adam and Eve condemned to work, to Cain and Abel and their offerings, to the crucifix at the center of the cathedral, which represents the ultimate sacrifice. Even a child learns it on the playground: when a child learns to make a friend, he learns the principle of sacrificial reciprocity. I have a turn, then you have a turn.

So the question is never whether to sacrifice; it's what, and to what. Hedonism and the drive to power are immoral for a technical reason: they degenerate when they're implemented and iterated. Sacrifice your self-serving, proximal whims to a pattern of being that sustains you across the long run and unites more and more people. You sacrifice the lower to the higher. And the payback is disproportionate to the cost, which every parent who has raised a child knows.

There's a further twist. Every time you reach a new pinnacle, you're called upon to be a little more than you were at the last one, and that means giving up a little more of your immature foolishness. The sacrifices grow as you grow. And what's the first sacrifice? Give up the stupid and unacceptable things you did that brought you low to begin with. That's a sacrificial gesture, and it's the one most people can make today. Notice also whose sacrifice is in question. Christianity insisted on the sacrifice of the self; the utopian ideologies of the twentieth century insisted on the sacrifice of someone else. Solzhenitsyn's horrors are what that second road produces.

When you're doing something that's in the nature of your true calling, the sacrifices you have to make to move forward don't hurt as much. Your work starts to feel like play. So if your toil is not play, ask yourself how wrong your aim is.

## How to Apply

1. **Name the trade explicitly.** What does this option cost tomorrow, next month, in five years? What does the alternative cost today? Write both down.
2. **Name the higher aim.** You can only sacrifice well if you know what you're sacrificing to. If you can't name it, set the aim first.
3. **Start with the stupid things.** The first and cheapest sacrifice is the habit you already know is hurting you.
4. **Offer your best, not your second best.** A half-hearted sacrifice is Cain's mistake. If you're going to give something up, give it up properly.
5. **Make the reciprocity visible.** In teams and relationships, take turns and let people see that sacrifices are shared.
6. **Expect the bar to rise.** Each success will ask more of you. Plan for that rather than resenting it.

## Examples

**Situation:** A founder knows she should spend her evenings on customer research but can't give up two hours of television that are "the only time I relax."

**Application:** That's the oldest trade there is: the present against the future. Try one week of trading one of those hours. Not all of them; one. Bring your best to that hour. If the company is the higher aim, it can bear that sacrifice. If it can't, that's important information about whether it really is your aim.

**Illustrative result:** The trade becomes easier once it's visibly paying off, and the work begins to feel less like loss.

**Situation:** A team keeps shipping hacks to hit sprint targets, and the codebase is collapsing under them.

**Application:** Every hack was a bargain that favored this sprint over every later one. Make the trade visible: what does each shortcut cost the next six months? Then sacrifice a sprint's velocity to the codebase the company needs in a year. That's not lost time; that's work in its proper sense.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Ask your team to sacrifice what you won't.
**Why:** Sacrifice demanded of others while the leader is exempt is the template of tyranny. Go first.

**Don't:** Treat delay of gratification as denial of all gratification.
**Why:** The point is a life that works now, next week, and next year, for you and the people around you. Sacrifice that makes the whole pattern worse isn't maturity; it's martyrdom.
