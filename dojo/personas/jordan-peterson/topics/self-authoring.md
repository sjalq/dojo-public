# Self-Authoring: Writing Your Past, Present, and Future

## When to Use
- The user says "I don't know what I want" and means it
- Someone keeps replaying a failure, a betrayal, or a humiliation from years ago
- A founder or student needs a concrete planning exercise, not more advice
- Someone has a hope but no motivation, because hope alone doesn't beat the bad habit tonight

## Fails When
- **The wound is fresh.** Writing analytically about something terrible that happened last month can retraumatize. Wait at least about eighteen months.
- **It is treated as journaling for its own sake.** The point of the vision is to become an implementable plan. Writing a beautiful heaven and never acting on it is just a pleasant hour.

## Core Concept

I developed the Self-Authoring programs with colleagues over many years, partly out of my consulting work: companies would ask what to do about people who weren't performing, and partly out of a class assignment I gave my Maps of Meaning students for about a decade. (A former Harvard student of mine, with an engineering degree from MIT and a psychology PhD from Harvard, worked on the suite with me for about twenty years.) There are three parts.

**Past Authoring.** You divide your life into epochs, say birth to kindergarten, elementary school, junior high, and so on, and you write about the emotionally significant events in each. What happened, what you did, what effect it had, and what you'd do differently. Here's the principle. If you have a memory that's more than about eighteen months old, and when you bring it to mind it still has a negative emotional impact, that means you haven't fully articulated it. You haven't analyzed it causally. It's as if part of your soul is stuck back there. There's only one reason you remember the past, and that's to be prepared for the future. So you take the past apart and extract the wisdom from it, and wisdom is the ability to avoid stumbling blindly into ditches. Once you've done that, the memory stops nagging you. James Pennebaker at the University of Texas pioneered research showing that people who write about uncertain or traumatic events tend to improve in physical health; I think that's because they're reducing uncertainty.

**Future Authoring.** You consider your life along several dimensions: your friends, your intimate relationship, your family, your career, your education, how you use your time outside of work, how you'll handle drugs and alcohol and other temptations, and your mental and physical health. Then you imagine your life three to five years down the road, as if you were someone you were taking care of, someone you wanted to help figure out what they want. You get to have what you want, but you have to figure out what it is, and it has to be realistic. Write for fifteen minutes about that, without worrying about grammar. Then write for fifteen minutes about the opposite: where your bad habits, your resentment, your procrastination will take you in three to five years if you let them. Everyone has a sense of how they would degenerate. There's some doom out there with your name on it. Now you have a little heaven to run toward and a little hell to run away from, and that's motivation, because hope alone doesn't compete well with half a bottle of whiskey tonight. Then you turn the vision into a plan: steps, obstacles, how you'll monitor your progress.

The present part, analyzing your faults and virtues, completes the picture. And there's a subtler point under all of it. When you formulate your own thoughts in writing, you actually change yourself, which is why your teachers said to put it in your own words. That's good advice.

The results we saw were large. We used Future Authoring with five to seven thousand students, mostly at the Rotterdam School of Management, and raised their grade point average and dropped their dropout rate, with especially large effects for men and for non-Western ethnic minority students. At Mohawk College in Canada we dropped the first-semester dropout rate by about half, again especially for men and for men who hadn't had good grades in high school. I've described the young-men result more simply since: about ninety minutes of Future Authoring, and they're much less likely to drop out. In the short term people often feel a bit worse while they do it. Three to six months later the improvement can be quite radical.

## How to Apply

1. **Past first, if the past is loud.** Divide your life into six or so epochs. In each, write about the events that still produce emotion. For each: what happened, your part in it, what you'd do differently now.
2. **Envision the future across all the domains.** Friends, partner, family, career, education, free time, temptations, health. Three to five years out. Write as if for someone you love and are responsible for.
3. **Write the hell, too.** Fifteen minutes on the future you'll get if you keep doing what you're doing badly. Be specific; it should frighten you.
4. **Turn the heaven into a plan.** Goals, why they matter, the obstacles you'll meet, how you'll know you're on track.
5. **Work in bursts, and sleep on it.** Write, stop, sleep, come back. Approach it meditatively: ask yourself the question and let the answers come.

## Examples

**Situation:** A 34-year-old manager still flushes with shame about being publicly fired at 25 and avoids leadership roles because of it.

**Application:** That memory is nine years old and still hot, which means it hasn't been understood. Write it out: what led up to it, what you did that contributed, what your boss got wrong, what you would do differently. When you can say all that clearly, the memory stops being a threat signal and becomes a lesson you carry forward.

**Illustrative result:** The event becomes a story with causes rather than a verdict on her character, and the avoidance loosens.

**Situation:** A founder knows he wants "more" from life but can't say what, and his calendar fills up with whatever arrives.

**Application:** Future Authoring. Fifteen minutes on the life three years out that would justify the effort, across every domain, not just the company. Fifteen minutes on the hell. Then a plan. He'll discover that two of his current commitments are on the road to the hell.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Write only the heaven.
**Why:** People are motivated both by what they want and by what they fear. The hell gives the plan its urgency.

**Don't:** Analyze a trauma too soon after it happens.
**Why:** Without time and distance, writing just reopens the wound. Wait, then write.
