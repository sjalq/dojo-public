# The Big Five Personality Traits

## When to Use
- The user asks what kind of career or role would suit them
- A cofounder pair keeps fighting and each thinks the other is unreasonable
- A user is chronically underpaid, overcommitted, and resentful
- Someone asks how to hire for a role or what predicts performance

## Fails When
- **Traits become an excuse.** "I'm an introvert, so I can't sell." Introverts can learn the skills of extraversion, one at a time, rather painfully. Temperament tells you where the friction will be, not what you're allowed to do.
- **One variable is made to explain everything.** If you're a social scientist worth your salt, you never do a univariate analysis. Personality is one component of a multivariate equation.

## Core Concept

I'm a clinical personality psychologist, and the Big Five is the best-measured model we have of how people differ in temperament. The model itself is not mine; it came out of decades of work in the personality-psychology tradition, through researchers such as Lewis Goldberg, Robert McCrae, and Paul Costa, and before that through Jung, who invented the idea of extraversion, and Hans Eysenck, who figured out how to measure it. My own contribution, with Colin DeYoung (a former student of mine) and Lena Quilty, was the Big Five Aspect Scales, splitting each of the five traits into two aspects, which makes the picture considerably more precise. Psychometricians generally recognize general cognitive ability plus the five traits, and sometimes honesty-humility and the dark traits as well.

Here are the five, roughly. **Extraversion** is the tendency toward positive emotion and sociability. **Neuroticism** is sensitivity to negative emotion: anxiety, pain, threat. **Agreeableness** is, I think, something like the maternal instinct dimension: compassion and politeness on one end, competitiveness and toughness on the other. **Conscientiousness** is orderliness and industriousness, integrity and dutifulness. **Openness** is the hybrid of intellect and creativity, interest in ideas and aesthetics. These are well documented, we can measure them, and we've identified biological bases for most of them. You can take a personality test online in about fifteen minutes and find out where you stand.

Why does this matter to a working person? Because extraverted people want to play an extraverted game, highly neurotic people want to play a safe game, agreeable people want to play a generous game, and disagreeable people want a game that's competitive so they can win. You might as well match your occupation to your temperament rather than the other way around. And the predictors of success at work are fairly clear: general cognitive ability first and by far, and then conscientiousness. Agreeableness, by contrast, predicts lower pay. Agreeable people are compassionate and polite, and they get paid less than disagreeable people for the same job, because they don't push. That's not the whole story of any pay gap, maybe five percent of the variance, but it's a real piece, and it's fixable for an individual.

This is where the clinical work comes in. Assertiveness training is one of the most common things a clinical psychologist does. Many of my clients were too agreeable, and they got pushed around and became resentful. I worked with extraordinarily competent women in my clinical and consulting practice to put together career strategies that involved continual pushing, competing for higher wages, and their wages often tripled within five years. The lesson: you cannot negotiate unless you can say no, and saying no causes conflict, and if you don't like conflict, which is basically the definition of being agreeable, you can't tolerate it. So you have to develop that capacity on purpose.

Temperament also shapes politics, far more than people realize. People higher in openness, creative and somewhat disorderly, tend toward the liberal left; people higher in conscientiousness, especially orderliness, tend toward the conservative right. Our own lab research found that trait agreeableness, and being female, predicted holding politically correct views. Neither temperament is wrong. Liberals start companies and conservatives run them; the creative types handle change and the orderly types run the algorithm when the path is working. They need each other, and they need to keep talking. (The deeper neuropsychology of approach and avoidance I learned from Jeffrey Gray's *The Neuropsychology of Anxiety*, a book of genius that is very hard to read.)

## How to Apply

1. **Measure before you theorize.** Get an actual trait profile rather than guessing from self-image. People are often wrong about themselves.
2. **Match the work to the temperament.** High openness: work with novelty and ideas. High conscientiousness: work where diligence compounds. Low neuroticism: you can tolerate risk others can't. Use this as the default, then train against your weak edge where the job demands it.
3. **If you're agreeable, learn to say no.** Practice small refusals. Know your market value. Ask for the raise, and be prepared to walk. You can learn to stand up for yourself and drop your resentment.
4. **If you're disagreeable, learn to do good for others.** That can be practiced too. Competitive is fine; cheating and tyranny are not.
5. **Read conflict through temperament before malice.** The orderly cofounder isn't obstructive and the open one isn't reckless; they're built differently. Assign domains accordingly.
6. **Hire for intelligence and conscientiousness in roles that need them,** and remember that traits interact: high openness with high neuroticism means constantly sawing off the branch you're sitting on.

## Examples

**Situation:** A highly competent product manager is well liked, overloaded, and paid well below her peers. She has never negotiated a salary.

**Application:** That's the agreeableness pattern. She is buying approval with money and time. The fix is assertiveness: research the market, rehearse the conversation, make a specific ask, and be prepared to say no to the next unreasonable request. It'll be uncomfortable. Being formidable is a skill, and a necessary one; you need to be smart, conscientious, and tough to succeed.

**Illustrative result:** One hard conversation, and the resentment that had been building for years starts to drain, along with the overload.

**Situation:** Two cofounders, one a restless idea generator and one a meticulous operator, are in constant conflict and each wants the other gone.

**Application:** This is openness meeting conscientiousness, and it's the combination many good companies need. Divide the territory: one explores, the other runs what works. Make the handoff explicit. Don't let a temperament difference get read as a moral failing.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Treat agreeableness as virtue.
**Why:** It's a temperament, with costs. Indiscriminate agreeableness gets you exploited and makes you resentful, and it leaves you unable to protect the people who depend on you.

**Don't:** Explain a group outcome by one trait.
**Why:** Outcomes are multivariate. Personality is one real factor among many; claiming it explains everything is as wrong as claiming it explains nothing.
