# Do Not Let Your Children Do Anything That Makes You Dislike Them

## When to Use
- A parent describes a two- or three-year-old who rules the household
- Bedtime, mealtime, or leaving the house is a nightly war
- A parent admits, quietly, that they don't like being around their own child
- A parent wants to protect a child from every difficulty

## Fails When
- **There's an underlying condition.** Some behavior has medical or developmental causes. Get it assessed.
- **The parent uses the rule as license for harshness.** The rule exists because parents are capable of cruelty. Discipline has to be calm, minimal, and immediately followed by welcome.

## Core Concept

People think they like their kids. Don't be so sure. Small children are hard to like sometimes: they're pushy and provocative, like young chimps testing the hierarchy, and lots of parents end up not liking their children and do terrible things to them their whole lives. As a clinician I saw families where everyone had their hands around the neck of the family member closest to them, squeezing just tight enough to strangle them in twenty years. Freud's fundamental observation was that a great deal of psychopathology is rooted in the family. So I wrote this rule after I understood I was a monster. I'm much bigger than my kids, I'm crueler than they are, and I have tricks they can't imagine. If they irritate me enough, I'll take it out on them. And if you don't think you're the kind of person who'd do that, you're the kind of person who is doing it.

So the rule protects the child from you. And it does something else: if the things your child does make you dislike them, the probability that other people will dislike them is extraordinarily high. Your job, roughly from birth to four, is to make your child someone other children want to play with and other adults are delighted to see. If a child is an outcast at four, the literature on correcting antisocial behavior suggests the probability that anything can be done is very low, because after four the peers do the socializing. And there's nothing more terrible you can do to someone than to put them in a world where all the goodwill directed toward them is false.

How do you do it? When my son, who was disagreeable by temperament, which I regard as a good thing, misbehaved at two, he'd sit on the steps until he had control of himself, and then he was welcome back, immediately, no grudge. Be a civilized human being and you're welcome again. That rewards the child exactly at the moment he masters himself. Keep the relationship pristine: no grudges, no resentment. Tell them when you're in a bad mood and they should play in their room for a while, because you're likely to be unreasonable. Reward what you want more of, specifically; if you ignore them when they do something good, you've just punished them for doing what you wanted. And do the arithmetic on recurring fights. One client was fighting his four-year-old for forty-five minutes every night at bedtime; added up, that's something like a month and a half of work weeks a year. You think you're going to like someone you spend that long fighting? Fix it.

There's the other side, too. Children need to confront difficulty to grow. I never told my son not to be afraid, which I think is bad advice. I told him to keep his eye on the hero. Rough-and-tumble play matters; Jaak Panksepp found that juvenile rats who aren't allowed to play don't develop properly. One of the problems with being wealthy as a parent is that you can't provide your children with necessity, and they need necessity to call them into being. Don't make them naive; you're supposed to teach them to identify serpents and handle them.

## How to Apply

1. **Admit your own capacity for anger.** Then build routines that keep you from acting on it.
2. **Use the minimal effective consequence, then welcome.** A timeout until the child regains control, followed immediately by warmth. No grudges.
3. **Reward the good, specifically and sincerely.** Catch them doing what you want and say so.
4. **Fix the recurring battles.** Calculate the hours. Then redesign the routine, because no relationship survives that many hours of war.
5. **Aim for a child others like.** Ask: would other children want to play with mine? Would other adults be glad to see them? Work on what's in the way.
6. **Let them face challenges in measured doses.** Don't eliminate fear; teach them to face it.

## Examples

**Situation:** Parents of a three-year-old describe nightly hour-long bedtime fights and admit they dread evenings.

**Application:** Add it up: an hour a night is more than three hundred hours a year of mutual misery. That is a relationship-destroying amount. Design a short, fixed routine, hold the boundary calmly and consistently, reward the nights it goes well, and expect a couple of hard weeks before it settles.

**Illustrative result:** The evenings become something both parent and child look forward to again, and the parents start liking their child again, which the child can feel.

**Situation:** A parent refuses to let their eight-year-old walk to a friend's house two blocks away, ride a bike without supervision, or play anywhere unstructured.

**Application:** You're protecting him from the very experiences that make him competent. Find a measured dose of independence he can handle and grant it. Then the next one. Make him bigger rather than making the world smaller.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Hold grudges against a small child.
**Why:** A grudge makes you blind to the good behavior and you'll punish it. Children need the relationship restored the moment they've regained control.

**Don't:** Protect children from all difficulty.
**Why:** Naive children are the ones devastated when they meet the real world. Graduated challenge is how they become formidable.
