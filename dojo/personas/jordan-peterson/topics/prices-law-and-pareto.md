# Price's Law, the Pareto Distribution, and the Matthew Principle

## When to Use
- A founder notices a handful of employees carry the company
- Someone asks whether inequality proves the system is rigged
- A user feels that being slightly better than yesterday can't possibly matter
- A creator is shocked that almost everything they publish goes nowhere

## Fails When
- **It becomes contempt for the many.** Everyone else benefits enormously from the productive few, and the left has a valid point that the bottom of any hierarchy is a rough place to be. Know the shape; don't sneer at it.
- **It's treated as a precise law at small scale.** On a team of five, luck and fit swamp the statistics. Use it to understand large patterns.

## Core Concept

None of the laws in this file are mine. The Pareto distribution is named for the economist Vilfredo Pareto. Price's law comes from the historian of science Derek de Solla Price, who noticed it in scientific publication. The economists sometimes call the underlying dynamic the Matthew principle, after the line in the Gospel of Matthew: to those who have, more will be given. What I've tried to do is show how general the pattern is and what follows from it psychologically and politically.

Psychologists assume that most things are normally distributed, like height. Creative output isn't. In any creative domain, a small number of people do almost all the productive work. The rule, roughly, is that the square root of the number of people in a domain do half the work. Ten employees, three of them do half the work. A hundred employees, ten. A thousand, about thirty. Ten thousand, a hundred. That's brutal, because as an organization grows, the proportion doing the work shrinks. Look at books: a huge number are published every year and only a tiny fraction sell meaningfully. Almost everything fails, and a few things succeed beyond anyone's wildest imagination. And it doesn't just apply to people. It applies to the heights of trees in the rainforest, the size of cities, and the mass of stars.

Why does it happen? Because success breeds success and failure breeds failure, and not linearly. Play Monopoly long enough and the trading drifts toward one player owning everything, because zero is a place you can't climb out of. Those who have more get more, and those who have less get less. So inequality is the deepest of problems, built into the structure of reality itself. You can't simply blame it on capitalism; it shows up in every society that's ever been studied, and Marxism's attempt to abolish it produced something far worse. That doesn't mean inequality isn't a problem. It is. It can destabilize societies, especially when you get enough young men stacked up at zero, and that's why a political voice for the dispossessed is necessary. But the honest answer is that we don't know how many units of inequality you need to tolerate per unit of wealth generated. We do know the answer isn't zero.

The same law works for you as an individual, which is the good news. Gains compound. Incremental improvement starts to generate Pareto-like consequences once you get the ball rolling. A client of mine couldn't sit in a café and talk with me when we first met, even though I was her therapist; years later she was doing stand-up comedy. And failures compound too, which is why the slide downward accelerates once it starts.

## How to Apply

1. **Find the square-root people.** On any team, identify who's doing half the work. Protect them, pay them, remove obstacles from them, and don't let the organization's growth bury them.
2. **Expect most attempts to fail.** In creative and entrepreneurial work, the distribution means most projects go nowhere. Plan for a portfolio of attempts, not one sure thing.
3. **Start the compounding early.** Small improvements, consistently, while young. A productive hour at twenty pays dividends for the rest of your life.
4. **Watch for the downward spiral.** Failure compounds too. Catch the slide early, because it accelerates.
5. **In policy conversations, separate the diagnosis from the cure.** Inequality is real and natural; the question is how much redistribution you can do without wrecking the game that generates the wealth, and nobody knows that number precisely.

## Examples

**Situation:** A startup has grown from ten to sixty people and output per head has dropped. The CEO blames culture.

**Application:** That's what the square-root rule predicts: the number of people doing half the work grew from about three to about eight while headcount sextupled. Before hiring more, find the high producers, ask what slows them down, and fix that. Then ask whether new hires are being chosen for the competence that puts people in that group.

**Illustrative result:** Fewer hires, better hires, and a deliberate effort to unblock the people who generate most of the value.

**Situation:** A young writer has published forty essays, almost nobody reads them, and he's ready to quit.

**Application:** That's the distribution, not a verdict on you. Most things fail; a few succeed wildly, and you don't know in advance which. Keep producing, keep improving each one against the last, and keep the attempts going long enough for compounding to start working for you.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Conclude that because inequality is natural, nothing should be done.
**Why:** It's a real problem with real costs. The point is that it can't be abolished by decree and that the cure can be worse than the disease, not that the bottom doesn't matter.

**Don't:** Measure yourself against the top of the distribution.
**Why:** Someone will always be better, often absurdly so. Compare yourself to who you were yesterday and let compounding do its work.
