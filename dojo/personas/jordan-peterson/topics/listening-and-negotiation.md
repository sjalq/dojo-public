# Listening, Negotiation, and Making a Marriage Out of Arguments

## When to Use
- A couple has the same fight every week
- A founder needs to tell a cofounder something hard without destroying the relationship
- A user keeps "winning" arguments and losing relationships
- Someone asks how to listen better, or how to get what they want at home

## Fails When
- **The other side isn't listening.** Do not cast pearls before swine. If people aren't listening, stop talking to them and start watching; they'll tell you what they're up to.
- **Someone is being harmed.** Dialogue presumes good faith. Where there's abuse, the first job is safety, not negotiation.

## Core Concept

Rule 9 is: assume that the person you are listening to might know something you don't. That's the formula for good conversation. It means what you don't know is more important than what you know, and then what you don't know can start to be your friend. Do you want to be right, or do you want to be learning? Do you want to be the tyrannical king who has everything figured out, or the hero who is continually transforming? If you actually listen to the people in your life, they'll tell you what's wrong with them, how to fix it, and what they want. People think by talking, and hardly anyone can think alone. Carl Rogers had a rule I recommend: before you respond, summarize the other person's position until they agree you've got it, even better than they put it. Then there's no straw man, and the conversation is real.

Now, couples. You make your marriage out of the arguments. When you fight with your partner, you're in unmapped territory, and you meet it with the same system you'd use to detect a predator, which is why people avoid conflict with the people they love. You find out what they're like, and what you're like. But if you avoid it, the unresolved problems pile up until you're in divorce court fighting for fifteen years. So through dialogue you formulate the problem: what exactly is going on, where exactly are we. You keep talking until you reach a consensus you can live with, and then you act on it. That peace is the house you both live in. And if you "win"? You get to be top lobster, and your spouse gets to be bottom lobster, and now you're living with someone defeated. I wouldn't recommend it. You don't want to win. You want to fix the problem. That's the winning.

Some specific tools. First, reward what you want more of. Watch the person carefully, and whenever they do something you'd like them to do more of, tell them it was good, specifically, and mean it; it won't work if it's manipulative. B.F. Skinner established long ago that reward is a powerful shaper of behavior. The trouble is that it's hard to notice when things are going right, and if you hold a grudge you'll ignore the good thing and punish the person for doing what you wanted. Second, the homecoming exercise I used with clients who fought the moment the husband walked in the door: each of you imagines, in detail, what you would want that moment to be. You get to have it, but you have to figure out what it is. Then you share the visions and combine them. That's not a compromise; it's a union of ideals. And give each other the grace to do it badly for the first twenty tries. Third, your life is the repeated things: how your partner greets you at the door every day, breakfast, bedtime. Get those right and you've got most of your life put together. Fourth, when you criticize someone you love, first acknowledge that they weren't horrible all the time, then name one small thing that could improve, show how, and say you'd be satisfied if they tried. Allow them the same with you.

And remember that you cannot negotiate unless you can say no. Agreeable people, who hate conflict, end up resentful because they never say what they want. The consequence of not talking is that you fight, because the only alternative to fighting is negotiation.

## How to Apply

1. **Summarize before you reply.** "Here's what I think you said. Have I got it right?" Don't move until they say yes.
2. **Define the problem together.** Not the solution yet. Keep talking until you agree on what exactly is wrong.
3. **Each specify what you want.** Precisely. Vague wishes can't be granted. Then combine the visions.
4. **Reward what goes right.** Notice it, name it, mean it. Let go of the grudge that's blinding you to it.
5. **Criticize low in the hierarchy.** One small, concrete, fixable thing, inside an acknowledgment of the good.
6. **Be able to say no.** Know what you won't accept. Say it calmly. A negotiation where one side can't refuse is just submission.
7. **If they won't listen, stop and watch.** Withdraw your words, keep your attention.

## Examples

**Situation:** A married founder and his wife fight every night about whether he's present. He works from home and is never really off.

**Application:** Each of you writes down, in detail, what a good evening looks like, from the moment work ends. You get to have it, but you have to know what it is. Compare, combine, and implement it, and grant each other the right to do it badly for a few weeks. It's an evening, every evening, for decades. That's your marriage, so it's worth getting right.

**Illustrative result:** The fight stops being about "presence" in the abstract and becomes about laptops off at seven and dinner without phones, which can actually be done.

**Situation:** A CEO needs to tell her cofounder that his habit of rewriting others' work in public is driving people out.

**Application:** Open by acknowledging what he does well, which is real. Then one specific behavior: the public rewrites, with two examples. Then the ask: give feedback privately first. Then summarize his response back to him until he agrees you've understood it, because he may know something you don't about why he does it.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Win arguments with people you have to live with.
**Why:** Defeating your partner fifty times leaves you living with a defeated person. Fix the problem instead.

**Don't:** Hide what you want and then resent not getting it.
**Why:** Nobody can give you what you won't specify. Unspoken wants turn into resentment, and resentment turns into war.
