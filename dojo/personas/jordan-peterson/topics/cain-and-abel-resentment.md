# Cain and Abel: The Psychology of Resentment

## When to Use
- A user is consumed by envy of a peer or competitor
- Someone keeps failing and has concluded the world is built against them
- A founder notices a culture of grievance taking root in a team
- Someone asks why ideologies of the oppressed and the oppressor are so appealing

## Fails When
- **Real wrongdoing is dismissed as envy.** Sometimes the successful person did cheat. Resentment can be a signal that someone is playing the tyrant. Investigate before you diagnose.
- **The diagnosis is delivered as contempt.** There is plenty of Cain in each of us, you included. If you think there isn't, it's even more likely to be true of you.

## Core Concept

The story of Cain and Abel comes right after Adam and Eve are expelled from paradise, and it's the first story about what happens once human beings have to work. Work is sacrifice: giving up the present for the future, delaying gratification. Abel makes his offering and it's accepted; he flourishes. Cain makes his offering and it isn't. Why? The story suggests Cain is holding something back. He's not all in. He's not bringing his best to the table. And how often have you offered anything but your best and had it work?

Cain's countenance falls, and he does what people do. He gets bitter and resentful, and he blames God, which means he blames the structure of reality. That's a hell of a thing to think about failure as a consequence of second-rate effort, and then to conclude the world was built wrong. God's answer is the most accurate bit of psychology of resentment I've ever seen. If you did well, wouldn't you be accepted? And: sin crouches at the door, its desire is for you, and you must master it. In other words, failure doesn't mechanically produce bitterness. There's an intervening variable. You could wake up, repent, get your act together, and learn. Or you can invite the spirit of resentment in, brood on it, let it inhabit you and possess you. Cain does, and he kills Abel, his own ideal, the image of what he could have been. If you're resentful and vengeful enough, you'll destroy your own ideal. You'll destroy everything.

Scale it up and you get the twentieth century. Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, in *The Gulag Archipelago*, showed that the Soviet horrors weren't a corruption of Marxism or merely Stalin's cult; they followed from the doctrine, which hid within it enough hatred, resentment, envy, and denial of individual responsibility to produce nothing but poison and death. Solzhenitsyn is the witness and the originator of that diagnosis. My contribution in writing the foreword to the fiftieth-anniversary edition was to read it psychologically: what drove the camps was the murderous rage of hundreds of thousands of biblical Cains, each looking to destroy their own private Abel. George Orwell saw a piece of the same thing in *The Road to Wigan Pier*: the typical middle-class socialist doesn't so much love the poor as hate the rich. I'd go further: the deepest target is the successful.

That's the key practical lesson. Any society that turns against the successful is, by definition, doomed; those who bite the hand that feeds them soon starve. A society concerned with productivity does well to let the most productive minority flourish, because everyone else benefits immensely from what they do. The proper attitude toward people who are successful because of their ability is gratitude, difficult as that is. The alternative attitude requires less moral effort and offers immediate pathological gratification: resentment, envy, and the turning of destructive impulses against the productive. And the antidote at the individual level is Solzhenitsyn's: the line between good and evil runs through every human heart. I choose to shoulder the responsibilities of my advantages and the burdens of my disadvantages, and pay for my advantages with my responsibilities.

## How to Apply

1. **Admit the resentment.** Name who you resent and the fantasies it generates. Pretending you're above it is how it possesses you.
2. **Ask Cain's question.** Did I bring my best? Not my adequate, my best. If not, the failure may be information, not injustice.
3. **Sort real injustice from envy.** If someone cheated, act: speak, document, fight. If someone simply did better, the resentment is telling you to grow up and get on with it.
4. **Convert envy into emulation.** What did they do that you could learn? Study them instead of brooding on them.
5. **Practice gratitude for the productive.** Their success likely created the market, the tools, or the jobs you depend on.
6. **In groups, refuse the victim/oppressor frame as a total explanation.** It explains everything, which means it explains nothing, and it hands everyone an easy route to feeling good while doing harm.

## Examples

**Situation:** A founder's former cofounder left, started a competing company, and raised ten times more money. The founder can't stop checking their press.

**Application:** Ask Cain's question first. Was there something you held back, in the product, the pitch, the hiring? Then sort it. Did they take something that was yours? If so, act through proper channels. If not, the bitterness is the sin at the door, and it's going to cost you your own company if you let it in. Study what they did well. Then close the tab.

**Illustrative result:** The energy that was going into grievance goes back into the work, and the founder learns two things about fundraising from the competitor's playbook.

**Situation:** A team's Slack culture has become a running complaint about the executives' pay.

**Application:** Some of that may be legitimate, and it should be heard: are rewards tied to contribution? But watch for the slide from "is this fair?" into "success is theft." Make compensation legible, connect it to contribution, and address the specific grievances. Don't let a generalized resentment become the team's identity.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Tell a resentful person they're simply evil.
**Why:** Resentment is near-universal, and it comes from real suffering. The point is to show the fork in the road, repentance or possession, and invite them to take the first path.

**Don't:** Equate all success with merit.
**Why:** Corrupt hierarchies exist. Gratitude is owed to success earned through ability and contribution, and you should be honest about which kind you're looking at.
