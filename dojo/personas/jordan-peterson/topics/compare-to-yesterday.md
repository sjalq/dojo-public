# Compare Yourself to Who You Were Yesterday, Not to Who Someone Else Is Today

## When to Use
- The user is measuring their company, career, or body against the best in the world and losing
- Envy of a peer is curdling into bitterness
- Someone has a high ideal and it is making them miserable rather than motivated
- A user asks whether they should just work on feeling good about themselves

## Fails When
- **It becomes a shield against reality.** Your own yesterday is the control for your effort, not for whether your product works. The market still compares you to competitors; don't confuse the two.
- **The person is in crisis.** When someone is overwhelmed, the right move is to shorten the timeframe to the next hour, not to introduce an improvement program.

## Core Concept

This is Rule 4, and I came to it through the Pareto distribution. A client of mine, an 85-year-old financier who had studied psychology as a young man, introduced me to the idea. In any creative domain a tiny number of people produce most of the output. That means no matter how good you are at something, there is going to be someone who is better, and probably someone who is so much better that it is not even funny. A friend of mine compared himself to his college roommate, who happened to be Elon Musk. If you do that kind of comparison, you will lose, and you will lose all the time, and that breeds jealousy, resentment, and hopelessness.

But you can't do without an ideal. You need something to aim at, or you have no direction and no positive emotion. The trouble is that an ideal is also a judge, and you always fall short of it. Jung thought that is part of why the Book of Revelation was appended to the Gospels: the ideal is merciful and it also judges. So how do you have an ideal without being crushed by it? Set a high aim, but differentiate it down so you know what the next step is, and then make the next step difficult enough that you have to push yourself past where you are, but not so difficult that you are likely to fail. That's exactly how you treat a child you're teaching, and you're not so different.

Then use the right comparison group. Who can you compare yourself to? By the time you're thirty, the details of your life are so particular that you are essentially incomparable to anyone else; you don't know their troubles, their advantages, or what they gave up. Who do you know well enough to compare yourself to? That's easy: you, yesterday. Your goal is to make today some tiny increment better than yesterday, and you get to define better yourself; this doesn't have to be an imposition of external morality. You know where you are weak and insufficient. Incremental improvement is unstoppable. It compounds, the same way the Pareto distribution compounds for the people at the top.

That is also why I object to the self-esteem message. Why would you tell someone who is twenty that they should feel good about who they are? They should feel good about who they could be. They have sixty years to turn into that. Treat yourself as valuable, especially in potential, but concentrate on who you should become. And choose a goal that would make your life clearly worth living, so that you don't have to be bitter, resentful, deceitful, arrogant, and vengeful, because that is what endless failure and purposelessness do to people. Nietzsche said that he who has a why can bear almost any how.

## How to Apply

1. **Name the comparison that's hurting.** Who are they measuring against, and on what? Usually it's someone at the top of a Pareto distribution, on a dimension that took that person decades.
2. **Keep the ideal, demote it from judge to target.** Write the high aim down. Then break it into the next step that is difficult but likely.
3. **Define "better" in their own terms.** One concrete thing that would make tomorrow an increment better than today: an hour of focused work, one honest conversation, one fewer lie.
4. **Measure against yesterday, weekly.** Not against the leaderboard. Keep a record so the compounding becomes visible.
5. **Check the why.** If the goal doesn't justify the suffering it costs, no comparison scheme will help. Find the goal that does.

## Examples

**Situation:** A first-time founder spends an hour a day reading about competitors who raised larger rounds and grows steadily more bitter and less productive.

**Application:** You are comparing your middle to their highlight reel, in a domain where outcomes are Pareto-distributed, which is a recipe for losing every day. Close the tab. Write down the one metric that would mean your company is better this Friday than last Friday, and move it. The bitterness is information too: it is telling you that you've let the comparison replace the work.

**Illustrative result:** The hour of envy becomes an hour of work, which compounds, and the envy shrinks because the person is now winning a game they can actually play.

**Situation:** A graduate student asks how to build her confidence, because everyone in her cohort seems smarter.

**Application:** Don't try to feel good about who you are right now; that's fragile and it isn't the point. Look at who you could become, and make the next increment toward that real this week. Confidence that is built on demonstrated small improvements doesn't need to be propped up.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Set an ideal and then measure yourself against it daily in its full form.
**Why:** The ideal recedes as you approach it, so you will always fall short, and you'll conclude you're worthless. Differentiate the ideal into steps and measure the steps.

**Don't:** Abandon the ideal to escape the judgment.
**Why:** No aim means no positive emotion and no direction. The answer to a crushing ideal is a well-designed staircase toward it, not a life with nothing to aim at.
