# Aim, Perception, and the Hierarchy of Value

## When to Use
- The user is drifting and waiting for clarity before committing
- A founder has a hundred priorities, which means none
- Everything seems hard, hostile, or pointless to someone
- A small criticism produced a large emotional reaction and the person wants to understand why

## Fails When
- **The aim becomes a fixed idol.** You are supposed to move the target as you learn. A plan you refuse to update is just a different way of not learning.
- **Aim is confused with ambition for status.** An aim that serves only you, now, at the expense of others, degenerates; it isn't the kind of aim that produces durable positive emotion.

## Core Concept

You cannot look at the world without a hierarchy of value. There are an infinite number of things you could pay attention to: the pattern on the floor, the color of the wall, the hum of the lights. If everything is equally important, or equally unimportant, it's chaos. So you simplify the world to what you actually see, and you do that with your aim. Your aim specifies the landscape of your perceptions. What you see aren't so much objects as pathways forward: tools that help you move toward your goal, obstacles in the way. A cliff isn't just a cliff, it's a falling-off place. The neurobiology of that is quite clear. That's why, in Daniel Simons's famous experiment, more than half of the people watching the video don't see the man in the gorilla suit thumping his chest in the middle of the screen; they're counting basketball passes. This is a radical thing to understand, because we normally think we just look at the world and there it is. No. If the world is manifesting itself to you as nothing but thorns and impediments, there's something wrong with your aim. The Greek word for sin, *hamartia*, is an archery term. It means to miss the target, and you can miss it by never picking up the bow.

Second, positive emotion comes from moving toward a goal, not from reaching it. When you accomplish something, you're king of the mountain for a day, and then you're left with the problem of what to do next. So no goal, no positive emotion. And when you're aimless, you're overwhelmed with anxiety because there are too many choices, and you have no hope, because hope is experienced in relation to a goal. You need a goal. Any goal is better than none. Better is a goal other people will let you pursue, or help you with. Better still is one that makes you better at pursuing goals. Best: aim at the highest good you can conceive, even if you can only see it dimly, and things will work out as well as they can. People avoid specifying what they want because it specifies their conditions of failure. Keep it foggy and you can't tell if you're failing. Great. Except you can't hit anything you don't aim at.

Third, the value hierarchy explains why errors hurt so much. Is the joke that fell flat at the party a minor flaw in an otherwise good personality, or proof that you're a creep to the core? The magnitude of your upset tells you how far up your hierarchy you think the error reaches. Tell a child the spoon is in the wrong place and that's low in the hierarchy. Tell them they're a rotten kid and you've taken them out at the highest level, and it wipes out their whole map. That's also why your internal values have to nest inside a structure you share with other people. When doing the right thing isn't rewarded, when the promotion goes to someone who didn't earn it, the match between your values and the world's breaks, and that's devastating.

## How to Apply

1. **Make a stupid plan.** A plan is better than no plan, and a stupid plan is way better than no plan. Don't wait to "find yourself." Pick something and move toward it, and you'll succeed and fail specifically.
2. **Learn from the specifics, then move the target.** You're moving toward a target and at the same time moving the target toward something like an ultimate ideal. That's not inconsistency; it's how you find out what the ideal is.
3. **Aim high, then differentiate down.** The ultimate aim is vague; the next step must be concrete. If you can't hit the next step, lower it until someone as competent as you are right now can.
4. **Nest your aims.** This hour serves this week, which serves this year's goal, which serves the life you want. If an hour serves nothing above it, question the hour.
5. **When upset, locate the error in the hierarchy.** Ask whether the failure really reaches as high as it feels. Usually it's a spoon, not a character verdict.
6. **When criticizing others, aim low in their hierarchy.** First acknowledge that they weren't horrible all the time. Then name one small, specific thing, show how it could improve, and say you'd be satisfied if they tried.

## Examples

**Situation:** A 26-year-old has spent three years "exploring options," traveling and doing short contracts, and is increasingly anxious.

**Application:** The anxiety is the aimlessness. Too many options, no hope, because there's no goal to be hopeful about. Make a stupid plan this month: one direction, a one-year horizon, a first concrete step. You'll find out quickly what's wrong with it, and that information is exactly what you've been missing.

**Illustrative result:** Within months the plan is revised, and the revision is better informed than three years of open-ended exploring produced.

**Situation:** A founder's head of sales is devastated after a mild critique of one presentation and has been withdrawn for a week.

**Application:** He heard a verdict on himself, not on the slide deck. Go back and fix the frame: first, his track record is strong; second, there's one specific thing about the opening that would help; third, here's how. You've moved the error back down to where it belongs.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Wait for certainty before committing.
**Why:** Staying in that malaise until you know what to do makes you older and more miserable, and you gather no information along the way.

**Don't:** Set goals that only you can benefit from.
**Why:** A goal others won't let you pursue is one you'll be fighting the world to reach. An aim that works for you and the people around you, now and later, is the one that sustains effort.
