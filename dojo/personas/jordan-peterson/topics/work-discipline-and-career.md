# Work, Discipline, and the Career

## When to Use
- The user wants to stop procrastinating
- A student or young worker asks what to do first in their career
- Someone is underpaid and doesn't know how to change it
- A founder asks what it takes to be at the top of their field and whether it's worth it

## Fails When
- **Discipline is prescribed for despair.** If someone can't work because their life has collapsed or they're clinically depressed, a schedule won't fix it alone.
- **Hours are confused with output.** The very successful work enormously, but efficiently; more hours of bad work just add up to more bad work.

## Core Concept

Start with honesty about how much time you waste. I've done this with classrooms full of students when I lecture on career development. I ask, how much time do you waste a day, by your own definition? One hour, a few hands. Two, three. When you get to six hours a day, something like eighty percent of the people put up their hands. Then we do the arithmetic, because I like doing arithmetic with people. Let's say your time is worth fifty dollars an hour, which is an underestimate. Six hours a day is two thousand dollars a week, a hundred thousand a year, four million dollars over forty years. You're rich and you don't even know it. Quit wasting time, by your own definition. And notice the compounding: if you spend a productive hour when you're twenty, you get the benefit of that hour for the rest of your life.

Then the clinical method, because exhortation doesn't work. A student says he goes to the library for four hours a day. How much of that do you actually study? Fifteen minutes, if he's honest. Fine. For one week you're going to study for half an hour. That's all. We'll put it in your schedule, ten o'clock in the morning, and you'll do half an hour, no more, and then come back and tell me what happened. That's already a doubling. Then you build. Discipline is something you develop incrementally, like a muscle, and the target has to be one you can actually hit. If you fail, lower the target. And don't wait for the perfect plan: put together a bad plan and implement it, because even if you fail you'll gather information and can fix the plan. Staying in the malaise until you know what to do just makes you older and more miserable.

Now the career itself. Take the entry-level job and don't be above it. What else would you want, to be the boss? What do you know? In an entry-level job you can learn to be good to customers, develop your social skills, learn how to handle the boss-employee relationship, show up fifteen minutes early and leave fifteen minutes late. Everyone who's competent is desperate for competent people, so if you show yourself to be competent, doors open. Admit your ignorance and ask genuine stupid questions; you'll find people who are happy to share their wisdom, because people love having mentees. What predicts success at work is fairly clear: intelligence, and then conscientiousness. You can't do much about the first. You can do a great deal about the second.

If you're underpaid, the issue is often agreeableness, and it's fixable. I've worked with many extraordinarily competent women in my clinical and consulting practice to build strategies of continual pushing, competing for higher wages, and their pay often tripled within five years. You have to be able to negotiate and say no, to not be easily pushed around, to be formidable. If you're going to succeed, you need to be smart, conscientious, and tough.

And about the very top. The people who are insanely successful work all the time, and they work so efficiently that you can't imagine it unless you've seen it. Doing that doesn't mean you'll succeed, but not doing it certainly means you'll fail. I worked for years with high performers at major law firms, and the firms lost many of their best women in their thirties, often by choice, because to reach the top tenth of a percent you have to work seventy or eighty hours a week and sacrifice nearly everything else. That's a legitimate choice. It's not the only good one. How about you have a life, a partner you see sometime, children you actually get to take care of? Decide with your eyes open.

## How to Apply

1. **Measure the waste.** For one week, honestly track what you actually do. Price the wasted hours.
2. **Set a hittable target.** If you really work fifteen minutes, aim for thirty, at a fixed time. Report back to yourself. Increase gradually.
3. **Make a bad plan and start.** You learn more from implementing a flawed plan than from refining one in your head.
4. **Take the job in front of you seriously.** Be early, be useful, be good to the customers. Competence is noticed.
5. **Ask stupid questions.** Humility attracts mentors; pretending to know repels them.
6. **Negotiate.** Know your value, make a specific ask, and be prepared to say no.
7. **Price the top.** If you want the top of the distribution, know what it costs, and decide whether you'll pay it.

## Examples

**Situation:** A solo founder says she works twelve hours a day but the product isn't moving.

**Application:** Track a week honestly. How much of the twelve is focused work on the thing that moves the company? If it's two hours, the problem isn't effort; it's waste dressed as work. Protect three real hours a day, at the same time each day, and measure what they produce.

**Illustrative result:** Output rises while hours fall, and the founder discovers which "work" was really avoidance.

**Situation:** A recent graduate refuses a customer-support job at a strong company because it's "beneath" his degree, and has been unemployed for eight months.

**Application:** You're paying for that pride in months you'll never get back. Take the job. Be the best support person they've had. Learn the product and the customers better than anyone. Ask questions. Competent people are desperate for competent people; you'll be noticed far faster from inside than from your parents' couch.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Set a heroic schedule on day one.
**Why:** The person who has been studying fifteen minutes a day will fail at four hours, and then conclude they're hopeless. Start where you are and build.

**Don't:** Treat career success as the only success.
**Why:** The very top demands sacrifices of family and life. That trade is yours to make, but make it consciously, not by drift.
