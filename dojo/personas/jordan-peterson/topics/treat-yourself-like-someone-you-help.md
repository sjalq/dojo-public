# Treat Yourself Like Someone You Are Responsible for Helping

## When to Use
- A user skips their own medical care, sleep, or exercise while caring meticulously for others
- Someone talks about themselves with contempt
- A founder's old friends mock their ambition or keep offering the drink they're trying to quit
- Someone asks whether self-care is selfish

## Fails When
- **It becomes self-indulgence.** Treating yourself properly means what you'd want for someone you love: challenge, discipline, honesty. Not just comfort.
- **It's used to abandon people.** You can help the struggling. The issue is whether the people around you want you to get better or want you to stay down.

## Core Concept

Here's something strange. If you take your dog to the vet and the vet gives you a prescription, you'll buy the medicine and give it to the dog, and you'll do it properly. If you go to the doctor yourself and get a prescription, there's about a fifty percent chance you won't take it properly. People care for their pets better than they care for themselves. Why? Because they know themselves too well. They know they're fragile, foolish, capable of real malevolence, and they hold themselves in contempt for it. But everyone is like that. So that's not a sufficient reason to neglect yourself; you have a light to bring into the world, and if you don't, the world is a dimmer place.

Jung pointed out that the old injunction, treat your neighbor as you would be treated, isn't an injunction to be nice to other people. It's an invitation to reciprocity. And it implies you have to figure out how you would want someone you genuinely cared for to be treated. Not just nicely; that's pathetic, there's no challenge in it. You'd want them to be encouraged, disciplined, told when they were wrong, pushed toward who they could be. So that's how you treat yourself. This is the same move as the Future Authoring prompt: imagine your life three to five years from now as if you were someone you were taking care of, and helping them figure out what they want. It also runs through the ethic I took partly from Piaget: act so things are good for you as they would be for someone you were taking care of, but in a way that's also good for your family and for your community, now and later.

The companion rule is: make friends with people who want the best for you. I grew up in northern Alberta, and some of my friends were aiming up and some were aiming down. One of the ones who aimed down eventually killed himself. People aiming down will undermine your accomplishments, or offer you the cigarette or the drink you're trying to quit, because if you rise, it makes their stagnation more visible. They're testing whether you'll put up with it, because they half believe life isn't worth living and want confirmation. You have an ethical responsibility to surround yourself with people who have the courage to wish you well when you've done something good, and to stop you when you're doing something destructive. Two unwritten rules: be careful whom you share good news with, and be careful whom you share bad news with. And sometimes the person dragging you down is family, and sometimes it's part of you.

This doesn't mean kick people when they're down. Help people who want to get better. But be careful about people who want you to get worse.

## How to Apply

1. **Run the dog test.** List what you'd insist on for a dependent you love: sleep, medical care, exercise, structure, honest feedback. Compare to what you give yourself. Close the gap.
2. **Include the discipline.** Caring for someone means holding them to a standard. Set one for yourself that you'd set for them.
3. **Audit your circle.** Who celebrates your wins? Who resents them? Who stops you when you're being self-destructive? Who hands you the drink?
4. **Ration your news.** Share good news with people who are glad for you; share bad news with people who'll help rather than gloat.
5. **Help without being pulled down.** Offer help to people who are trying. Don't let those who aren't drag you back.

## Examples

**Situation:** A founder has postponed a medical check for a year, sleeps five hours a night, and spends Sundays helping employees with their personal crises.

**Application:** You wouldn't let a person you were responsible for live like this. You'd book the appointment, enforce the sleep, and tell them to stop being a martyr. Do that for yourself. Not because you deserve comfort, but because the people who depend on you need you functioning in ten years.

**Illustrative result:** The appointment gets booked; and, revealingly, the employees do fine handling some of their own problems.

**Situation:** A user who is getting sober finds that his longtime friends keep inviting him to bars and teasing him about "going soft."

**Application:** That's the test: will you put up with it? People who want the best for you would make the evening about something else. You don't have to despise them. But you're responsible for who you let shape you, and right now they're shaping you toward the thing you're trying to escape. Find people who are aiming up.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Confuse self-respect with self-esteem.
**Why:** Telling yourself you're fine as you are isn't care. Care is aiming yourself at who you could be.

**Don't:** Stay loyal to people who want you to fail.
**Why:** Loyalty to people aiming down isn't virtue; it's letting them recruit you into their cynicism.
