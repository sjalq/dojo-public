# Taking the Temperature of the Market

## When to Use
- When assessing whether to lean aggressive or defensive in portfolio positioning
- When the market's behavior feels extreme in either direction and you want a framework for reading it
- When reconciling a stated philosophy of not market-timing with occasional tactical adjustments

## Fails When
- Used as a basis for frequent trading; temperature-taking is for rare extremes, not routine adjustments
- The investor lacks the decades of experience needed to distinguish a genuine extreme from normal market noise
- Confused with forecasting; taking the temperature tells you where you are, not where you're going

## Core Concept

Taking the temperature of the market is pattern recognition at a level that permits rare, high-conviction tactical calls, deployed only at genuine sentiment extremes. It is not market timing. It is not forecasting. It's an assessment of where we are in the cycle of investor psychology.

The operating rule is simple: "We never know where we're going, but we ought to know where we are." You can't predict the future. But you can assess the present. Are investors behaving with caution or recklessness? Are risk premiums generous or meager? Is capital abundant or scarce? Are lending standards tight or loose? Are people optimistic or pessimistic, and how uniformly and intensely do they hold those views?

Marks has made five directional calls in more than 50 years. Five. Not fifty. The rarity is the point. When his son Andrew observed that his market calls had been "about right," Andrew added: "Yeah, Dad, that's because you did it five times in 50 years." The epiphany was that these calls only work at genuine extremes, and attempting more frequent calls would put you in the mushy middle where no dependable conclusion is available.

The five case studies illustrate what a legitimate non-forecasting market call looks like. The January 2000 tech bubble call. The 2004-2007 gradual turn to defense as credit standards deteriorated. The late-2008 post-Lehman deployment of $10 billion. The March 2012 recognition that universal equity pessimism echoed the famous 1979 "Death of Equities" BusinessWeek cover. The March 2020 pandemic panic buying. Each had the same structure: extreme, uniform, intense sentiment; a clear historical parallel or mechanism; and a position that didn't require timing precision, just the recognition that the extreme couldn't persist.

The discipline reconciles with Oaktree's stated philosophy through three constraints: base-case assumptions are always neutral (never "distinctly better or worse than normal"), portfolio design includes a margin of safety that absorbs disappointment, and departures from the normal risk posture happen only at rare extremes, never routinely.

The single clearest operational rule from this framework: selling at the bottom, not buying at the top, is "the cardinal sin in investing." A mistimed buy at the top costs you a drawdown but keeps you on the escalator of a rising economy and rising markets. A mistimed sell at the bottom gets you off that escalator permanently, and it's the one mistake from which many investors never recover.

## How to Apply

1. **Observe, don't predict.** The question is not "what will happen next?" but "what is happening now?" What opinions are being expressed? How uniformly? How strongly? How self-satisfied are the people holding them?

2. **Look for extremes, not trends.** In the midrange, no dependable conclusion is available. Only at genuine extremes does regression toward the mean become dependable enough to act on.

3. **Name the historical pattern.** Each of the five legitimate calls had an explicit historical parallel. The tech bubble rhymed with Edward Chancellor's histories of speculation. The 2012 pessimism rhymed with the 1979 "Death of Equities." Pattern matching against historical extremes is more reliable than abstract sentiment scoring.

4. **Make the call rare.** If you're making tactical calls every quarter, you're market timing, and you should expect market-timing results (poor). The discipline is to hold a neutral posture almost all the time and depart from it only when the evidence is overwhelming.

5. **Never sell at the bottom.** If you must err, err on the side of staying invested. The cardinal sin is selling during panic. The asymmetry of mistakes favors action over inaction at market bottoms and caution over aggression at market tops.

## Examples

**Situation:** In March 2020, the S&P 500 has fallen 34% in a month. Clients are terrified and asking whether to go to cash.

**Application:** The temperature is extreme: uniform panic, near-universal pessimism, and the conviction that things can only get worse. This is one of the rare moments where a directional view is justified. The exact bottom is unknowable ("the bottom is the day before the recovery begins... thus it's absolutely impossible to know when the bottom has been reached"), but the sentiment extreme is clear.

**Illustrative result:** The investor who buys into the panic captures the recovery. The investor who sells at the bottom gets off the escalator permanently. This is the cardinal-sin framework in action.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Try to take the temperature monthly or quarterly. Most of the time the temperature is "moderate, no action needed."

**Why:** If you're making tactical calls routinely, you're market timing. Market timing doesn't work. Temperature-taking works precisely because it's rare.

**Don't:** Confuse knowing where you are with knowing where you're going.

**Why:** Knowing the market is at an extreme doesn't tell you when the extreme will correct. It tells you the probability of eventual correction is high. The distinction between probability and timing is the difference between useful pattern recognition and useless forecasting.
