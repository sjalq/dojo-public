# The "I Know" School vs. the "I Don't Know" School

## When to Use
- When assessing your own relationship with uncertainty in investing
- When evaluating whether to follow a confident forecaster or strategist
- When building an investment organization's culture around decision-making
- When someone is either overconfident or paralyzed by uncertainty

## Fails When
- Used to justify permanent fence-sitting, where the investor never acts because they "don't know"
- Conflated with lack of analytical rigor; "I don't know" is an epistemological position, not an excuse for not doing the work
- Applied to bottom-up security analysis where genuine informational edges exist, not just macro forecasting

## Core Concept

There are two broad schools of thought about investing, and every investor belongs to one of them, whether they've articulated it or not.

The "I know" school believes the future is knowable and that knowledge of the macro future is necessary for investment success. Its adherents produce and consume forecasts of interest rates, GDP, corporate earnings, and market direction. They position portfolios based on those forecasts. They are often charming, confident, and articulate. They are desirable dinner guests, because they always have an opinion on what's coming next. And their forecasting records, if they kept them honestly, would show that they are not right consistently enough to be relied upon.

The "I don't know" school believes the future is genuinely unknowable in the macro sense. Its adherents don't pretend to have knowledge they lack. They focus on doing the best possible job of investing in the absence of that knowledge, concentrating on individual asset values, risk posture, and preparation for a range of outcomes. They are less popular at dinner parties. And they tend, over long periods, to produce better risk-adjusted returns.

Marks identifies himself and Oaktree squarely with the "I don't know" school. The motto is: "it's okay to have an opinion; you just shouldn't act as if it's right."

The "I know" school's failure is not that its members are wrong. They're often right: most of the time, extrapolation works and the economy does roughly what the consensus expects. The problem is twofold. First, being right via extrapolation doesn't make you any money, because the extrapolation is already priced in. Second, the non-extrapolative forecasts, the ones that could make money if right, are almost never right consistently enough to act on.

The social cost of "I don't know" is real and must be accepted explicitly. No one wants to hear "I don't know" at a client meeting, a board meeting, or a conference. The forecaster who confidently states a number, even a wrong one, gets more attention, more airtime, and more assets under management than the person who honestly says the future is uncertain. This social asymmetry is what keeps the "I know" school in business despite its poor track record. The "I don't know" school has to accept that penalty as a permanent cost of intellectual honesty.

## How to Apply

1. **Decide which school you belong to, and be honest about it.** If you make investment decisions based on macro predictions, you're in the "I know" school. The question is whether your forecasting track record justifies that approach.

2. **Ask forecasters for their track record.** An economist who doesn't present their historical accuracy is like a portfolio manager who doesn't show returns. The information is either embarrassing or nonexistent. No economist presents their record because they wouldn't be hired if they did.

3. **Structure portfolios to be robust across outcomes.** The practical expression of "I don't know" is a portfolio that doesn't depend on any single macro scenario. Neutral assumptions, adequate diversification, avoidance of bets that only work if a specific prediction is correct.

4. **Separate opinion from action.** Having opinions about the macro is fine. Betting the portfolio on those opinions as though they were facts is the error. The operative discipline is: hold opinions loosely, position portfolios for a range.

5. **Accept the social cost.** Saying "I don't know" will cost you professionally and socially. This is a real cost. Accept it, because the alternative, pretending to know things you don't, will eventually cost you much more when a confident prediction proves wrong at scale.

## Examples

**Situation:** At a client meeting, a sophisticated allocator asks: "Where do you think the market is going over the next twelve months?"

**Application:** The "I don't know" school response is honest: "We don't know, and we don't believe anyone else does either. What we can tell you is that the market's risk posture today is moderately elevated, spreads are tighter than historical averages, and we've positioned the portfolio to do well in most environments while limiting exposure to the downside if the environment turns out worse than expected."

**Illustrative result:** The client may be initially disappointed by the lack of a prediction. But over time, the consistency and honesty of this approach builds trust, especially when the clients who received confident predictions from other managers watch those predictions fail.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use "I don't know" as an excuse for not doing the analytical work. The school is about macro humility, not analytical laziness.

**Why:** Marks is deeply analytical about individual investments, market risk posture, and cycle positioning. "I don't know" applies to macro forecasting, not to the entire analytical enterprise. The best "I don't know" investors work harder on what they can know (individual assets, relative value, risk assessment) precisely because they accept they can't know the macro future.

**Don't:** Apply the framework to suppress all forms of conviction. High-conviction bottom-up analysis is a different activity from macro forecasting.

**Why:** Knowing that a specific company is mispriced relative to its fundamentals is very different from predicting where interest rates will be in twelve months. The first is achievable by skilled analysts. The second is not reliably achievable by anyone.
