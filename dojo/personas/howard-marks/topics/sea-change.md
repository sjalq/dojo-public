# Sea Change (Regime Shift vs. Ordinary Cycle)

## When to Use
- When evaluating whether a major market or economic shift is cyclical (it will revert) or structural (the old rules no longer apply)
- When deciding whether investment strategies optimized for a particular interest-rate environment should be continued or reconsidered
- When someone cites the 2009-2021 era as evidence for what to expect going forward

## Fails When
- Every downturn or rally is labeled a "sea change," diluting the concept's meaning; genuine sea changes are exceedingly rare
- Used to make specific predictions about the new regime's trajectory, rather than to adjust assumptions and risk posture
- The investor lacks the historical perspective to distinguish a real structural break from a normal cycle

## Core Concept

In more than 50 years in the investment world, Marks has witnessed only three genuine sea changes, as distinct from the many cycles, pendulum swings, manias, panics, bubbles, and crashes that are part of normal market life.

The first was the birth of risk/return-conscious credit investing in the late 1970s, when Michael Milken and others demonstrated that non-investment-grade bonds could be bought prudently if the yield adequately compensated for default risk. This replaced the old binary view, still embodied in Moody's language at the time, that anything below investment-grade was simply a "bad investment" regardless of price.

The second was Paul Volcker's 1979-82 war on inflation, which broke the inflationary spiral of the 1970s and ushered in four decades of secularly declining interest rates, from a peak fed funds rate of 20% to effectively zero.

The third, which he identified in December 2022, is the reversal of that 40-year declining-rate regime: the pivot to higher interest rates, positive real yields, and a tighter-money environment that has fundamentally changed the arithmetic of investing.

The key mechanism is what Marks calls the "moving walkway." For 40 years, investors didn't just benefit from economic growth and their own skill. They were also carried forward by a continuous tailwind of falling interest rates. Falling rates simultaneously cut borrowing costs, raised the present value of future cash flows, lowered the return investors demanded, and supercharged leveraged strategies. Much of what was attributed to skill was, in part, the moving walkway. When the walkway stops, or reverses, the strategies that depended on it should be expected to perform differently.

The practical conclusion is not a specific forecast. It's a reexamination of assumptions. If you've been investing in levered equity strategies, private equity, or growth assets for the past decade, and your return expectations were built during a declining-rate environment, those expectations need updating. Meanwhile, credit and fixed-income instruments, which were starved of return during the ultra-low-rate years, now offer yields that compete with historical equity returns. This doesn't mean equities will do badly. It means the relative attractiveness of credit has improved substantially.

## How to Apply

1. **Distinguish the rare structural shift from the frequent cyclical swing.** A sea change is not a bad quarter, a bear market, or a recession. It's a multi-decade regime change that alters the fundamental arithmetic of investing. Apply the label sparingly.

2. **Audit your return assumptions for tailwind dependency.** Ask: "How much of my historical return came from the moving walkway of falling rates?" Private equity, leveraged strategies, and long-duration growth assets benefited disproportionately from the declining-rate environment.

3. **Reassess the relative appeal of ownership vs. lending.** When credit instruments offer 7-10% yields with contractual, not speculative, returns, the hurdle for taking equity risk rises. The sea change framework says: don't assume the strategies that served you best since 2009 will do so in the years ahead.

4. **Don't extrapolate the new regime either.** The error of extrapolation applies in both directions. Rates went to zero and people assumed they'd stay there. If rates stay elevated, people will eventually assume they'll stay there too. Resist both assumptions.

## Examples

**Situation:** A pension fund has allocated heavily to private equity, generating strong returns from 2012-2021 through leveraged buyouts funded with cheap debt.

**Application:** The sea-change framework asks: how much of that return came from the moving walkway? In a leveraged buyout, a declining cost of debt mechanically amplifies returns over the holding period. If debt costs 3% and the unlevered return is 10%, leverage works beautifully. If debt costs 7%, the math changes materially. The fund should re-examine whether its private-equity allocation reflects skill or the tailwind of an era that has ended.

**Illustrative result:** The fund doesn't necessarily abandon private equity, but it adjusts return expectations downward for levered strategies and reallocates incrementally toward credit, where yields now compete with private equity's historical returns at substantially lower risk.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Call every market decline a sea change. True sea changes are rare by definition. Overuse of the term is a form of macro forecasting dressed up as structural analysis.

**Why:** Most market shifts are ordinary cycles that will revert. Labeling them as sea changes leads to portfolio reconfigurations that prove unnecessary and costly when the cycle simply reverts.

**Don't:** Extrapolate the new regime as confidently as people extrapolated the old one.

**Why:** The whole point of the sea-change concept is that regimes don't last forever. If you respond to a sea change by building a portfolio that only works in the new regime, you've made exactly the same error in the new direction.
