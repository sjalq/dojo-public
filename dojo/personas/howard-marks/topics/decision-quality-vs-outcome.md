# Decision Quality vs. Outcome Quality

## When to Use
- When evaluating an investment after the fact and trying to learn the right lesson
- When an investment with a sound thesis produced a loss, and the question is whether the thesis was wrong or just unlucky
- When building an investment team's culture around how mistakes are handled
- When someone claims skill based on a lucky outcome

## Fails When
- Used as a blanket excuse for poor results, where the real problem is analytical failure, not bad luck
- Applied so dogmatically that the investor never updates their process based on outcomes
- The investor can't honestly distinguish between a decision that was well-reasoned but unlucky and a decision that was poorly reasoned and predictably failed

## Core Concept

You can't tell the quality of a decision from the outcome. This is one of the first things Marks learned at Wharton, and it remains one of the most counterintuitive and important concepts in investing. A good decision can produce a bad outcome. A bad decision can produce a good outcome. In a world where randomness is real, this is not occasional. It's constant.

The investment world is full of people who were "right for the wrong reason." They made a poorly reasoned bet, got lucky, and were rewarded for it. And it's full of people who were "wrong" despite excellent reasoning, because the range of possible outcomes included bad ones, and a bad one happened. If you judge people solely by outcomes, you reward the lucky and punish the unlucky, and you learn nothing about who actually has skill.

The analogy to card games is direct. A poker player who makes the statistically correct call and loses the hand still made the right decision. The hand is one data point from a probability distribution. The player's skill is revealed over many hands, not one. Similarly, an investor's skill is revealed over many decisions and many market cycles, not by any single quarter or year.

The practical implications for investment organizations are profound. If you second-guess every decision that produces a bad outcome, you create a risk-averse culture where people are afraid to make decisions at all. If you celebrate every decision that produces a good outcome, you create a reckless culture that rewards luck. The right culture evaluates process: was the analysis sound? Was the range of outcomes considered? Was the position sized appropriately for the risk? Was the information available at the time used correctly? Those are the questions that matter, not "did it go up or down?"

The corollary is: improbable things happen all the time, and probable things fail to happen all the time. That is the nature of an uncertain world. If the most probable outcome always occurred, there would be no risk. The investor who internalizes this sleeps better, holds positions through temporary adversity, and avoids the worst mistake in investing: selling a sound position after a short-term loss because "it didn't work."

## How to Apply

1. **Evaluate process, not results.** After every investment, ask: was the analysis well-reasoned at the time? Were the risks understood and sized correctly? Was the range of outcomes considered? A bad outcome from a good process is bad luck, not a bad decision.

2. **Track the batting average over many decisions.** Skill reveals itself over a long series of decisions. A 60-70% hit rate is world-class. Demanding perfection produces paralysis.

3. **Don't second-guess after bad outcomes.** In a non-finger-pointing organization, people are free to make well-reasoned bets without fear of blame when randomness produces a bad result. This freedom is essential for the kind of unconventional behavior that produces above-average returns.

4. **Don't celebrate after good outcomes without examining the process.** A lucky outcome from a bad decision teaches the wrong lesson and reinforces bad habits.

5. **Build the team accordingly.** Hire people who are not overly emotional, who don't have excessive ego or hubris, who can tolerate being wrong without being crushed, and who won't point fingers at colleagues. The mellow, non-blame organization is a structural advantage.

## Examples

**Situation:** An analyst recommended buying a distressed bond at 60 cents. Six months later, the company defaulted and the bond recovered at 30 cents. The investment lost half its value.

**Application:** The outcome was bad. But was the decision bad? If the analyst correctly identified that bonds with similar risk profiles had historically recovered at 60+ cents, that the position was sized conservatively within the portfolio, and that the probability distribution favored the investment at 60 cents, then the decision was sound. A bad outcome from one position in a probability distribution does not invalidate the analysis.

**Illustrative result:** In a healthy culture, the team reviews the analysis, confirms the process was sound (or identifies where it wasn't), and moves on. The analyst is not punished for an outcome that was within the expected range of possibilities. Over time, this culture produces better decisions because people aren't afraid to take well-reasoned risks.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use "you can't judge a decision by the outcome" to excuse every bad result. Some bad outcomes are the result of genuinely bad analysis, not just bad luck.

**Why:** The framework demands honest self-assessment. If your analysis consistently produces bad outcomes, the problem may be with your analytical process, not with randomness. The test is pattern over many decisions, not any single one.

**Don't:** Demand a perfect track record from yourself or others.

**Why:** In a world with real randomness, nobody bats a thousand. Demanding perfection produces paralysis and risk aversion, which is the opposite of what a skilled investor needs.
