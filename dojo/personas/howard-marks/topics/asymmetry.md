# Asymmetry and Fewer Losers vs. More Winners

## When to Use
- When evaluating whether an investment manager has genuine skill or has just ridden a bull market
- When setting the strategic risk posture for a portfolio: emphasize offense or defense?
- When discussing what Oaktree's motto means and how it translates to investment practice
- When distinguishing between risk avoidance (bad) and risk control (good)

## Fails When
- "Avoid the losers" is interpreted as "avoid all risk," which guarantees mediocre returns
- Applied only to individual positions rather than the pattern across an entire portfolio over multiple cycles
- Used to avoid aspirational strategies entirely; even Oaktree moved beyond pure fixed income into distressed debt, real estate, and private equity where finding winners is essential

## Core Concept

Asymmetry is Marks's word for the essence of investment excellence: a manager who captures more upside than downside, repeatably, relative to a beta-matched benchmark. It's the only reliable structural signature of investing skill. Not high returns (which can come from high beta and luck). Not low volatility (which can come from holding cash). Asymmetry: the pattern of participating in more of the good and less of the bad, over and over, across cycles.

The concept connects to Oaktree's founding motto: "If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves." This motto has three independent historical antecedents. First, a pension-fund head whose returns never ranked above the 27th or below the 47th percentile in any single year, yet ranked 4th percentile over 14 years cumulatively, because consistency compounds better than swinging for the fences. Second, Benjamin Graham and David Dodd's description of bond investing as a "negative art": since all non-defaulting bonds of the same coupon deliver the same return, the only way a bond investor adds value is by excluding the defaulters. Third, Jesse Livermore's 1940 observation: "the winners take care of themselves; the losers never do."

The tennis analogy, from Charles Ellis's "The Loser's Game," formalizes the choice. Professional tennis is a "winner's game," won by hitting shots the opponent can't return. Amateur tennis is a "loser's game," won by making fewer unforced errors. Both are valid approaches, but the player must match the approach to their actual skill level. An amateur who tries to hit winners like a pro produces more errors, not more wins. An investor who reaches for high returns without the skill to identify genuine opportunities produces losses, not alpha.

But the motto is not the whole answer. In aspirational strategies, where the goal is not just 8% but 15-20%, avoiding losers is necessary but not sufficient. You have to find some winners. Even Buffett's career was built on a relatively small number of huge winners held for decades, combined with relatively few big losers. The pattern is not perfection; it's a favorable ratio.

The critical distinction is between risk avoidance and risk control. Risk avoidance means refusing to take any uncertain outcome. It usually equates to return avoidance. Risk control means bearing risk deliberately: risk you are aware of, can analyze, can diversify, and are well-compensated for. "How can life insurance companies insure people's lives when they know they're all going to die?" The answer is that they charge premiums that more than compensate for the known mortality rate. That's risk control, not risk avoidance. The same logic applies to high-yield bonds, distressed debt, and every other "risky" strategy: the question is whether the compensation is adequate for the risk borne.

## How to Apply

1. **Evaluate managers by up/down capture, not by returns alone.** A manager who captures 70% of market gains and only 40% of market losses has genuine skill. A manager who captures 120% of gains and 120% of losses is just levered. The asymmetry pattern, not the absolute return, is the signature of alpha.

2. **Choose your game: winner's game or loser's game.** If you have genuine analytical skill and can identify mispriced opportunities, play the winner's game. If you don't, play the loser's game: emphasize diversification, low cost, and the avoidance of big errors. Most investors should play the loser's game. Most investors who think they should play the winner's game are wrong.

3. **Don't confuse risk avoidance with risk control.** The goal is not to eliminate risk but to bear it intelligently. Risk avoidance is a portfolio of Treasury bills. Risk control is a portfolio of high-yield bonds managed by someone who can identify and exclude the defaulters.

4. **Measure over cycles, not quarters.** Asymmetry reveals itself only across multiple market environments. A manager can look skilled in a bull market simply by being aggressive. The test is whether they protected capital in the bad years while participating in the good ones.

## Examples

**Situation:** An allocator is reviewing two managers. Manager A returned 15% in a year the market returned 12%. Manager B returned 10% in the same year.

**Application:** On a single year's return, Manager A looks superior. But what happened in the down year? If the market fell 20% and Manager A fell 25% (more beta, no skill) while Manager B fell 10% (less downside, genuine risk control), Manager B has the better asymmetry profile. Over a full cycle, Manager B's cumulative return may exceed Manager A's because the compounding effect of avoiding big losses is more powerful than the incremental upside from aggressive positioning.

**Illustrative result:** The allocator who evaluates on asymmetry, rather than one year's return, makes a better selection that compounds more favorably over time.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use "avoid the losers" to justify permanent defensiveness. There is such a thing as the risk of taking too little risk.

**Why:** An investor who never takes risk earns the risk-free rate, which over long periods barely keeps pace with inflation. Risk avoidance is itself a risk: the risk of failing to earn the returns needed to meet your objectives.

**Don't:** Evaluate asymmetry over a single year or single market environment.

**Why:** Over any short period, luck dominates skill. Asymmetry is a pattern that emerges over many cycles. Judging a manager by one year is judging a poker player by one hand.
