# Ownership vs. Debt (The Two Asset Classes)

## When to Use
- When making the primary portfolio allocation decision: how much risk to take
- When evaluating whether credit or equity offers the better deal in the current environment
- When someone is confused by the proliferation of "asset classes" and needs to simplify

## Fails When
- Used to claim credit is always better than equity or vice versa; the answer depends on yields, spreads, and the investor's risk posture
- Applied too rigidly to instruments that blend ownership and lending characteristics (convertibles, preferred equity, mezzanine)

## Core Concept

Despite the proliferation of labels, there are fundamentally only two asset classes: ownership and debt. Everything else is implementation detail.

Owners take a residual claim on a business or asset. There's no promise of return. The upside is theoretically unlimited and the downside extends to zero. An equity investor is betting that the business will generate returns above what was paid for the ownership stake. But nothing is guaranteed.

Lenders have a contractual claim. A bond promises specific interest payments and return of principal at maturity. The outcomes are narrower: in the best case, you get exactly what was promised. In the worst case, the borrower defaults and you recover some fraction through bankruptcy. "Fixed income" is really "fixed outcome" investing, because the return is known in advance (assuming the promise is kept) in a way that equity returns never are.

This distinction is the foundation of the single most important portfolio decision: the balance between offense and defense. You cannot simultaneously maximize growth and preserve capital. Every portfolio sits somewhere on a continuum between pure ownership (maximum offense, maximum uncertainty) and pure lending (maximum defense, maximum contractual certainty). All other allocation decisions, large cap vs. small cap, domestic vs. international, public vs. private, are just implementation within that primary choice.

The practical significance shifts with the interest rate environment. During the 40-year declining-rate era (1982-2022), credit was systematically unattractive: yields were low and falling, and the opportunity cost of lending instead of owning was enormous. In the post-2022 sea change, with credit yields of 7-10%, lending suddenly competes with historical equity returns. An investor who can earn 8% from contractual, non-speculative credit has a meaningfully different decision to make than one who can only earn 2% from the same instruments.

When a client asks "should I invest in private credit?" the right first question is: "Can we talk about credit?" The private/public distinction is important but secondary. The primary question is whether the compensation for lending, in any form, is adequate for the risk borne.

## How to Apply

1. **Start every allocation conversation with the offense/defense question.** Before discussing sectors, geographies, or managers, decide: what is the right balance between growth-seeking (ownership) and capital-preserving (lending) for this investor's needs, time horizon, and risk tolerance?

2. **Understand what you're buying.** When you buy a bond, you're buying a contractual promise. Your return is fixed unless the issuer defaults. When you buy a stock, you're buying an uncertain future. The expected return may be higher, but the range of outcomes is much wider.

3. **Compare across the divide.** In some environments, credit yields are so low that lending makes little sense (2009-2021). In others, credit yields are competitive with historical equity returns (post-2022). The allocation between ownership and debt should reflect the relative attractiveness of each, not a fixed percentage.

4. **Don't let labels obscure the underlying reality.** Private credit, bank loans, mezzanine, direct lending, these are all forms of lending. Private equity, venture capital, real estate equity, these are all forms of ownership. The asset-class labels change; the fundamental distinction doesn't.

## Examples

**Situation:** In mid-2025, a pension fund allocator asks whether to increase their credit allocation given that high-yield bonds yield roughly 8%.

**Application:** The ownership-vs-debt framework starts with the fundamental question: given that credit now offers 8% contractual returns versus the S&P 500's elevated earnings yield (which historically at current P/E ratios has produced roughly 0-4% annualized 10-year returns), is the allocator being adequately compensated for the additional uncertainty of equity ownership? If 8% from lending is close to or better than the realistic expectation from ownership, and comes with narrower outcome dispersion, the case for increasing credit is strong.

**Illustrative result:** The allocator shifts incrementally toward credit, not as a permanent allocation, but as a rational response to the current relative pricing of the two fundamental asset classes.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Treat ownership vs. debt as a permanent preference. The right balance shifts with the environment.

**Why:** An investor who was permanently overweight credit during 2012-2021 sacrificed enormous equity returns for meager yields. An investor who remains permanently overweight equity in a 7-10% yield environment is ignoring the improved compensation for lending.

**Don't:** Confuse credit's contractual nature with risklessness. Borrowers default. Credit risk is real.

**Why:** "Fixed outcome" means the outcome is contractually defined, not that it's guaranteed. The whole art of credit investing is identifying which borrowers will keep the promise and which won't.
