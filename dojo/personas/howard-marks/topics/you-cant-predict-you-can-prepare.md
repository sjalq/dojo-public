# You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare.

## When to Use
- When someone asks "where is the market going?" or "when will the Fed cut rates?"
- When an investment thesis depends on a specific macro outcome
- When evaluating whether a forecaster's track record justifies following their predictions
- When designing a portfolio approach that should be durable across environments

## Fails When
- Interpreted as "ignore all macro context." Individual investments exist in an economic framework. You need an economic view; you just shouldn't bet the portfolio on one specific macro outcome.
- Used to avoid all tactical positioning even at genuine extremes, where the anti-forecasting stance should be relaxed

## Core Concept

The investment world is full of people who will confidently tell you where interest rates are going, what GDP growth will be next year, and whether we'll have a recession. They are paid to do this. They are socially rewarded for it. And the overwhelming evidence is that they cannot do it reliably.

The reason macro forecasting fails is not that forecasters are stupid. It's structural. An economy consists of hundreds of millions of participants whose behavior is driven by psychology, not physics. There are no fixed laws. The past does not reliably guide the future because the assumption of stationarity, the premise that the statistical properties of the system remain constant, fails constantly in economics. Richard Feynman said physics would be much harder "if electrons had feelings." People have feelings. Their behavior is unpredictable.

Consider what has to go right for a chained macro forecast to pay off. If you're predicting that a particular stock will rise because the economy will grow because the Fed will cut rates because inflation will fall because supply chains will normalize, you need five conditional forecasts to all be correct. If each is individually right 67% of the time, the probability of getting all five right is about 13%. And that's generous. This is what Marks calls "single-scenario forecasting," and it's the standard mode of operation for most market strategists.

There are two schools of thought. The "I know" school believes the future is knowable and positions aggressively based on forecasts. The "I don't know" school believes the future is genuinely unknowable, and focuses on preparing for a range of outcomes rather than predicting a specific one. Marks identifies himself squarely with the "I don't know" school.

The practical implication is not passivity. It's portfolio construction that doesn't depend on any single outcome. Build a portfolio that will do reasonably well in most environments, survive the bad ones, and capitalize on opportunities when they arise. Warren Buffett's filter is the clearest test: for information to be useful, it has to be both important and knowable. Macro is important. But it's not knowable. So it fails the test.

## How to Apply

1. **Stop asking "what will happen?" Start asking "what's the range of what could happen?"** Build a portfolio that is prepared for the likely range, not optimized for a single scenario.

2. **Don't act as if things that should happen are things that will happen.** Overpriced is not the same as going down tomorrow. Interest rates should normalize is not the same as they will normalize this quarter.

3. **Evaluate forecasters by their record, not their confidence.** Nobody presents their forecasting batting average. If they did, they wouldn't be hired. The forecasters who get the big calls right are usually the ones who always make extreme calls and happen to be right once, surrounded by a career of being wrong.

4. **Construct the portfolio for survival first.** Never forget the six-foot-tall man who drowned crossing the stream that was five feet deep on average. You have to survive the bad days, not just the average ones. The portfolio has to be set up so that a bad outcome doesn't force you out before your thesis can prove correct.

5. **Use macro context without depending on macro forecasts.** It's one thing to say "we'll invest in this company because it will do fine if oil stays around $50, survive if it goes to $30, and thrive if it goes to $70." It's another to say "oil is $50 and I predict it's going to $110, so I'll buy the company that only works at $110." The first approach uses macro context intelligently. The second bets the outcome on a prediction.

## Examples

**Situation:** In late 2023, the consensus expects the Fed to cut rates six times in 2024. An investor is tempted to position aggressively for falling rates.

**Application:** The "I don't know" school asks: why does the market believe six cuts when the Fed itself is signaling three? Is the consensus engaged in Goldilocks thinking, assuming everything works out perfectly? What happens to the portfolio if cuts come slower, or not at all?

**Illustrative result:** By early 2024, the consensus revises to two or three cuts. The investor who positioned for six is offside. The investor who prepared for a range of outcomes (two, four, or six cuts) navigated the uncertainty without being dependent on getting the call right.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Use anti-forecasting as a license to ignore macroeconomic conditions entirely. You need an economic framework for individual investment decisions.

**Why:** Saying "I don't forecast" doesn't mean "I don't think." You have to have assumptions, but your assumptions should be neutral (close to the historical norm) rather than heroic, and your portfolio should be able to withstand scenarios where those assumptions are wrong.

**Don't:** Confuse the inability to forecast with the inability to sense extremes. At the extremes, directional calls are both possible and valuable, but rare by design.

**Why:** The anti-forecasting stance is about routine macro prediction, not about the rare moments when investor behavior is so extreme that regression toward the mean is dependable. Those are different activities, and confusing them produces paralysis at exactly the wrong time.
