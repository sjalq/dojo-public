# Daring to Be Different / Daring to Be Wrong

## When to Use
- When you've done the analysis, believe the consensus is wrong, and need the conviction to act
- When evaluating whether to take an unconventional position that will look uncomfortable in the short term
- When someone asks what separates investors who outperform from those who merely identify opportunities but can't act

## Fails When
- Used to justify poorly reasoned positions that happen to be different from the consensus
- The investor doesn't understand that the 2x2 matrix has a bottom-left quadrant: unconventional behavior with bad judgment produces below-average results
- Applied without the partnership, culture, or temperament to sustain the position through the period when it looks wrong

## Core Concept

Every great investment begins in discomfort. If it were comfortable, everyone would already own it, and it would be fully priced. The discomfort is not a bug; it's the source of the opportunity.

The conventional/unconventional matrix from "Dare to Be Great" (2006) lays this out starkly. There are four possible combinations: conventional behavior with a favorable outcome produces average good results. Conventional behavior with an unfavorable outcome produces average bad results. Only unconventional behavior can produce above-average or below-average results. And the difference between ending up in the upper right (above-average) or the lower right (below-average) is whether the unconventional judgment was correct.

"You can't take the same actions as everyone else and expect to outperform." This is an arithmetic truth, not an aspiration. Active management is inherently zero-sum: every dollar earned above the market return by one investor is a dollar earned below it by another. So outperformance requires positions that differ from the consensus. But differing from the consensus means accepting three risks simultaneously.

First, you have to dare to be different. Your portfolio must look meaningfully unlike the index or the consensus portfolio. David Swensen at Yale called this "uninstitutional behavior from institutions," and acknowledged it's professionally uncomfortable.

Second, you have to dare to be wrong. Every departure from consensus carries the risk of being wrong. You cannot have "try to be right" without "run the risk of being wrong." They are inseparable. If an investment were guaranteed to work, everyone would do it, and the opportunity would disappear.

Third, you have to dare to look wrong. Even if your analysis is correct, the market may not recognize it for months or years. During that time, your position will look wrong. You will be questioned by clients, colleagues, and your own doubts. Being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong. You have to survive the interim.

The practical implication: the ability to bear this discomfort is not just a personality trait. It's a structural requirement. It requires partners who support rather than second-guess, a culture that doesn't finger-point after bad outcomes, and clients who understand the process well enough to give you time. Without those structural supports, even the best analysis will be abandoned at the worst possible moment.

## How to Apply

1. **Check the 2x2 matrix honestly.** Before celebrating your contrarian position, ask which quadrant you're likely in. Unconventional behavior only produces above-average results with superior judgment. Without the analytical edge, contrarianism is just a different way to lose money.

2. **Build the support structure.** Great contrarian investing is not a solo activity. It requires partnership (someone who reinforces your conviction when it's tested), organizational culture (no blame for honest mistakes), and client understanding (they don't pull money at the worst time).

3. **Size the position for the interim.** If you're right but early, you need to survive until the market agrees. That means position sizing that can weather drawdowns, time horizons that don't require quarterly vindication, and liquidity management that prevents forced selling.

4. **Accept that most of the time, the consensus is roughly right.** Daring to be different is for the rare moments when you have strong reason to believe the consensus has made a specific, identifiable mistake. It's not a daily operating mode.

## Examples

**Situation:** In September 2008, after Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy, the consensus is that the financial world may be ending. Marks and Bruce Karsh at Oaktree begin deploying $650 million per week into distressed assets.

**Application:** The thesis was explicit and "unsupportable" in any rigorous analytical sense: they assumed the financial world would continue to exist. If it didn't, nothing they did would matter. If it did, and they didn't invest, they would have failed their responsibility to clients. They bought $10 billion in assets over 15 weeks.

**Illustrative result:** The financial world didn't end. Those purchases produced extraordinary returns. But the willingness to act came from decades of partnership, mutual support, a non-finger-pointing culture, and the rehearsal of similar (smaller) contrarian actions in prior cycles. The analysis was simple. The conviction to act was hard.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Romanticize being a contrarian. The admirable outcome is not being different; it's being different and right. The graveyard of investing is full of contrarians who were merely wrong.

**Why:** The 2x2 matrix is symmetric. The lower-right quadrant (unconventional + wrong) is just as real as the upper-right (unconventional + right). Being different is necessary for outperformance, but it's not sufficient.

**Don't:** Assume you can sustain contrarian positions alone. Without structural support, conviction evaporates under pressure.

**Why:** Our emotions conspire at every turn to make us do the wrong thing. Partnership, culture, and client alignment aren't luxuries. They're structural requirements for the kind of behavior that produces above-average results.
