# Bubble Diagnosis (Psychology, Not Formula)

## When to Use
- When market enthusiasm around a sector, asset class, or technology reaches extreme levels
- When people are buying based on "it can't miss" rather than on an analysis of price versus value
- When you need a framework for distinguishing genuine overvaluation from a mere bull market

## Fails When
- Used to call the exact timing of a bubble's collapse; that can't be done
- Applied mechanically via a valuation threshold ("P/E above X is a bubble"); bubbles are psychological states, not valuation levels
- The investor stays permanently short or uninvested because they see bubbles everywhere

## Core Concept

A bubble is not a valuation level. It's a state of mind. It exists when investors exhibit four simultaneous characteristics: highly irrational exuberance, adoration of the subject asset and the belief it "can't miss," FOMO, and the conviction that "there's no price too high."

This is the key distinction. A market can be expensive without being a bubble. The S&P 500 can trade at 22 times forward earnings, and that might be elevated, worrisome, even ill-advised, without being a bubble. A bubble requires psychology that has detached from any anchor in valuation or historical comparison. It requires people buying not because they've analyzed the value, but because they believe the asset will keep going up forever, and they can't stand to miss it.

Bubbles need "newness" to take hold. A new technology, a new asset class, a new financial innovation. Newness is essential because it eliminates the historical comparison that would serve as a tether on valuation. You can look up the historical P/E of the S&P 500 and see that 90x has never ended well. But if AI is "unprecedented," if internet commerce was "unprecedented," if radio was "unprecedented," there's no track record to serve as a warning. And "The Emperor's New Clothes" social dynamics take over: no one wants to look foolish by doubting a consensus that is making everyone else rich.

There's an important refinement: not all bubbles are equally destructive. Mean-reversion bubbles, like the South Sea Company or subprime mortgage-backed securities, are pure financial fads that destroy wealth for no lasting benefit. Inflection bubbles, like the railroads, electricity, or the internet, are tied to genuinely transformative technologies. They still destroy wealth for individual latecomers, but they accelerate society's adoption of something real. The question with AI is which kind this is.

The three stages of a bull market describe the psychological sequence: first, a few perceptive people recognize that improvement is possible. Second, most people accept that improvement is underway. Third, everyone believes things can only get better forever. The bubble starts when the third stage takes hold.

## How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the psychology, not the valuation.** Ask: are investors buying because of a careful analysis of value, or because they believe the asset can't go down? Is anyone saying "no price is too high"? Is there adoration of the asset? Is there FOMO? If yes to multiple, the psychological conditions for a bubble exist.

2. **Look for "newness."** Bubbles cluster around new things because novelty eliminates the historical comparison that would moderate enthusiasm. If people are saying "this has never existed before, so historical valuation metrics don't apply," that itself is a warning sign.

3. **Check the financing mechanism.** Historically, bubbles funded by debt are more dangerous than bubbles funded by equity. Debt requires repayment regardless of outcome. When speculative ventures are funded with 30-year bonds and off-balance-sheet leverage, the risk of systemic damage increases.

4. **Don't try to time the top.** Bubbles can persist far longer and inflate far larger than seems rational. Being early in identifying a bubble is functionally indistinguishable from being wrong. The correct response is to moderate exposure, not to go short.

5. **Take a moderate position.** The practical conclusion in most genuine bubble environments is: no one should go all-in without acknowledging the risk of ruin if things go badly, and no one should stay all-out and risk missing one of the great technological steps forward. A moderate position, applied with selectivity, beats either extreme.

## Examples

**Situation:** In early 2025, AI stocks account for a disproportionate share of S&P 500 gains, capex, and profits. Nvidia has briefly become the world's first $5 trillion company. Clients are asking: is this a bubble?

**Application:** The bubble-diagnosis framework checks the psychology. Are people buying AI stocks based on fundamental analysis of earning power, or based on "it can't miss" thinking? Is there genuine FOMO? Is anyone saying "no price is too high"? The answer is mixed: the leading companies have real revenue and reasonable P/E ratios (unlike 1999), but there are signs of lottery-ticket thinking in pre-revenue AI startups, circular deals between AI companies, and heavy reliance on debt to fund infrastructure.

**Illustrative result:** The framework doesn't produce a verdict. It produces a calibrated assessment: elevated, worrisome, some bubble characteristics present but not the full set. The appropriate response is moderation, selectivity, and close monitoring, not all-in or all-out.

## Anti-Patterns (tactical)

**Don't:** Confuse high valuation with a bubble. Markets can be expensive for years without being in a bubble.

**Why:** The bubble label implies an imminent, devastating collapse. A merely expensive market can stay expensive or get more expensive. Misusing the label either keeps you permanently underinvested or desensitizes you to the real thing.

**Don't:** Assume that because a technology is transformative, its stock can't be overpriced.

**Why:** The technology can be real and the stocks can still be too expensive. Radio was real. The internet was real. The companies that pioneered them mostly went bankrupt anyway, because "no price is too high" thinking priced them for perfection.
